Symptoms

Symptoms, graded by how well each one is documented — and the presentations that are not hard flaccid syndrome.

30 min read · 12 references

Contents

    Summary

    • Seven symptoms of hard flaccid syndrome (HFS) have been quantified in a published cohort. Roughly a dozen more are described in peer-reviewed case literature without frequency data.
    • A further group of symptoms is reported consistently by patients but does not yet appear in any peer-reviewed source. This article lists them and labels them as such.
    • Symptoms cluster into six domains: the flaccid state itself, sensory change, erectile function, pain, urinary and bowel function, and psychological distress.
    • Symptoms are characteristically position-dependent, improving when lying down and worsening on standing.
    • Several presentations that overlap with HFS require urgent assessment and are listed separately in section 9.

    A symptom list is a clinical instrument, not a catalog. Used well, it tells a clinician which questions to ask and tells a patient whether their experience has been described before. Used badly, it becomes a checklist against which anxious people measure themselves, and every unfamiliar sensation becomes evidence of deterioration. The list below is intended for the first use.

    It is also longer than the published evidence supports, and that is deliberate. Patients report symptoms that no researcher has yet counted. Excluding those symptoms would misrepresent the condition as experienced; including them without qualification would misrepresent the state of the evidence. The grading system in section one exists to hold both positions at once.

    1. How to read this list Method

    Every symptom below carries one of three tiers. The tier describes the strength of documentation, not the severity of the symptom or the distress it causes.

    Table 1 · Evidence tiers used in this article
    TierMeaning
    Tier A Frequency quantified in a published cohort or survey. A numerical figure is available and is reported exactly as published.
    Tier B Described in peer-reviewed case reports, case series or review articles, but not quantified. The symptom is documented; its prevalence is not known.
    Tier C Reported consistently by patients and in clinical settings, but not identified in any peer-reviewed source located for this article. Listed for recognition, not as established fact.

    Tier C entries should be read with care. Absence of documentation is not evidence that a symptom is imagined, and it is not evidence that it belongs to HFS either. It means the question has not been studied. Several Tier C items have plausible mechanisms; plausibility is not data.

    Two sources supply most of the quantified data. The first is a cross-sectional online survey of 143 self-identified patients conducted between 9 May and 9 June 2023, using a 42-question instrument with purposive and convenience sampling and recruitment through social media.3 The second is a 2025 systematic review that pooled eight eligible studies and summarized that survey’s findings.4 Qualitative description comes largely from two 2020 papers: a four-patient case series1 and a review of current knowledge by Abdessater and colleagues.5

    The limitations of that evidence base apply to every figure in this article. Survey respondents were self-identified rather than clinically diagnosed. Recruitment through social media selects for patients with persistent, distressing symptoms. Symptom attribution was not independently verified. No study has compared HFS patients against matched controls for any symptom listed here. Frequencies should therefore be read as descriptions of a self-selected population, not as prevalence estimates.3,4

    Key points

    • Tier A symptoms have published frequencies; Tier B are described but uncounted; Tier C are patient-reported only.
    • Most quantified data come from one survey of 143 self-identified patients.
    • No matched-control study of any HFS symptom has been published.

    2. The core presentation Tier A

    Seven symptoms have published frequencies. These are the anchor of the syndrome, and six of the seven were reported by more than 70% of respondents.

    Table 2 · Quantified symptom frequencies (n = 143)
    SymptomFrequencyDomain
    Change in penis shape or size92.3%Structural
    Rigid penis when flaccid90.9%Flaccid state
    Psychological distress, anxiety or depression89.5%Psychological
    Weak or overactive pelvic muscles85.3%Pelvic floor
    Penile numbness or sensory loss74.8%Sensory
    Erectile difficulty74.1%Erectile
    Decreased urinary stream72.7%Urinary

    Frequencies as reported by Niedenfuehr and Stevens and summarized in the systematic review.3,4

    The defining feature is the first one on the list in clinical terms rather than numerical terms: a penis that remains firm and semi-rigid in the absence of sexual desire or arousal. Abdessater and colleagues describe it as a constantly semi-rigid penis in the flaccid state, accompanied by penile engorgement at baseline.5 This is the symptom that distinguishes HFS from erectile dysfunction of other origins. Erectile difficulty is common in HFS, but erectile difficulty alone is not HFS.

    One entry requires interpretation. “Change in penis shape or size” is the most frequently reported symptom at 92.3%, but the survey instrument groups a range of experiences under that heading, and the published summary does not disaggregate them.3,4 Patients describe shortening, retraction, altered contour and altered girth. Which of these drove the 92.3% figure cannot be determined from the published data.

    Key points

    • Seven symptoms carry published frequencies, six of them above 70%.
    • The semi-rigid flaccid state without arousal is the defining feature and separates HFS from ordinary erectile dysfunction.
    • The most frequent single item, change in shape or size, is a composite that the published data do not break down.

    3. Structural and vascular features

    Tier B Baseline penile engorgement is documented in the review literature.5 Physical examination is otherwise described as unremarkable, with possible hardness palpable at the base of the penis and flaccidity of the glans.5 Mild corporal fibrosis was reported on Doppler in one published case.6 That is a single patient and should not be generalized.

    Tier C Several structural observations are reported frequently by patients without appearing in the peer-reviewed literature. They are listed here because clinicians will hear them described:

    • Prominent or engorged superficial veins, including fine “spider” veins. Distension of superficial penile veins is visible in some patients. No study has assessed venous calibre in HFS.
    • Hourglassing or bottlenecking of the shaft during the flaccid or partially erect state, reported to resolve fully once a complete erection is achieved. The reported resolution with full erection is the feature that patients use to distinguish this from a fixed deformity, but it is examination, not self-report, that separates it from the tunical indentation of Peyronie’s disease.
    • Lateral deviation or tilt of the penis, flaccid, erect, or both. Any new curvature or deviation requires examination for a palpable plaque, since Peyronie’s disease is a distinct condition with its own management.
    • “Long flaccid” — a penis more extended at rest than previously, described as either firm or as fluid-filled and unstable.
    • Incomplete filling during erection, in which the erection is attained but the penis does not reach its former dimensions.
    • Discoloration of the penile skin, variably described as dusky, pale or mottled.

    An important caution on color change. Transient color change that varies with position and temperature is what patients typically describe. Persistent duskiness accompanied by pain, or any progressive discoloration, is not an HFS symptom to be cataloged — it requires same-day medical assessment to exclude ischemia.

    Key points

    • Baseline engorgement and a soft glans are documented; other structural findings are patient-reported only.
    • Any new curvature, deviation or indentation warrants examination to exclude Peyronie’s disease.
    • Persistent painful discoloration is a red flag rather than a symptom to monitor.

    4. Sensory symptoms

    Tier A Penile numbness or sensory loss was reported by 74.8% of survey respondents.3,4 Tier B The case literature describes these as odd sensory changes, specifically numbness or coldness with decreased sensitivity, particularly at the level of the glans.5 Sensory change was one of four consistent themes identified in a thematic analysis of 12 online forum discussions containing 6,150 comments, alongside semi-hardness in the flaccid state, erectile dysfunction and emotional distress.2

    Tier B A cold sensation in the glans, shaft or whole penis is documented, and a soft, cold glans during an otherwise adequate erection is described as characteristic.5 This last finding — sometimes called glans insufficiency — is one of the more specific observations in the literature, because it dissociates corporal rigidity from glans engorgement.

    Tier B Dysesthesia, meaning an unpleasant abnormal sensation, is central to how the 2026 International Consultation on Sexual Medicine recommendations frame this condition. Those recommendations place HFS within the spectrum of persistent genital arousal disorder and genito-pelvic dysesthesia (PGAD/GPD), described as a sensory hyperactivity condition involving unrelenting unwanted dysesthesias.8 Under that framing, abnormal sensation is not a secondary complaint. It is close to the center of the disorder.

    Tier C Patients describe sensory disturbance in finer detail than the literature records:

    • Selective loss of specific tactile modalities — temperature, pressure, vibration or texture — rather than uniform numbness. No quantitative sensory testing study in HFS has been published, so modality-specific loss is undocumented.
    • Loss of erogenous sensation as distinct from loss of tactile sensation, in which touch is detected but is no longer pleasurable.
    • Paresthesia: tingling or pins-and-needles in or around the penis. This falls within the dysesthesia umbrella described in consensus documents, but has not been separately characterized in HFS.8
    • A sense that the penis feels hollow, disconnected or unstable, as though it were not part of the body. Patients report this consistently and often find it the hardest symptom to articulate to a clinician. It has not been studied.

    The last item deserves a note. Altered body ownership of a body part has been studied in other neurological contexts, but no such work exists for HFS, and the symptom should not be assumed to be psychological in origin merely because it is difficult to describe. It should also not be assumed to be neurological. The honest position is that nobody has investigated it.

    Key points

    • Numbness or sensory loss affects roughly three-quarters of surveyed patients.
    • Coldness and a soft glans during erection are documented and reasonably specific.
    • Expert consensus positions abnormal sensation as central, placing HFS within the PGAD/GPD spectrum.
    • Modality-specific sensory loss and altered body ownership are reported by patients but entirely unstudied.

    5. Erectile and sexual symptoms

    Tier A Erectile difficulty was reported by 74.1% of survey respondents.3,4 Tier B The original case series described difficulty achieving or maintaining erection together with decreased frequency of morning and nocturnal erections in all four patients.1 Loss of erectile rigidity and reduced frequency of morning and nocturnal erections are both documented in the review literature.5 Reduced libido appears in the case literature.5,6

    The loss of nocturnal and morning erections carries diagnostic weight and is worth explaining rather than simply listing. Nocturnal erections are generated during REM sleep by central mechanisms that operate independently of conscious arousal and independently of anxiety.9 Their absence therefore indicates that the problem is not solely performance-related. In HFS this is consistent with the proposed mechanism, in which excess sympathetic outflow to cavernosal smooth muscle would suppress erection irrespective of the state of mind of the patient. That interpretation is inference from established physiology, not a demonstrated finding. Inference

    Tier B Painful erection and painful ejaculation are both described. Abdessater and colleagues report penile and perineal pain during urination and ejaculation.5 Pain with erection and post-ejaculatory pain are also recognized features of hypertonic pelvic floor disorders generally, which is relevant given that 85.3% of surveyed HFS patients reported weak or overactive pelvic muscles.3,10

    Tier C Patients additionally report a complete absence of spontaneous erections during waking hours, and erections that feel tight or constricted rather than simply painful. Neither has been separately quantified.

    Key points

    • Roughly three-quarters of patients report erectile difficulty; loss of morning and nocturnal erections is documented across the case literature.
    • Absent nocturnal erections indicate a mechanism operating independently of conscious arousal.
    • Painful erection and painful ejaculation are documented, and overlap with recognized features of hypertonic pelvic floor disorders.

    6. Pain Tier B

    Pain is not universal in HFS, and its absence does not exclude the diagnosis. Where present, it is described in the penis, at the base of the penis, and in the perineum.3,5 The multimodal-therapy case report describes intermittent chronic pain around the perineum and penis.6 Pain during or after ejaculation and pain during urination are both documented.5

    HFS-associated pain has been proposed to represent a form of genito-pelvic dysesthesia arising from pathological activation of the pelvic/pudendal-hypogastric reflex rather than from ongoing tissue damage.7,8 This distinction matters clinically. Pain generated by a persistent reflex is not an indicator of continuing injury, and treating it as a damage signal tends to drive the checking and avoidance behaviors that patients describe. The mechanism remains a hypothesis. Hypothesis

    Key points

    • Pain occurs in the penis, penile base and perineum, and may relate to urination or ejaculation.
    • Pain is not required for the diagnosis.
    • It is proposed to be dysesthetic rather than a signal of ongoing tissue damage; this is unproven.

    7. Urinary and bowel symptoms

    Tier A Decreased urinary stream was reported by 72.7% of survey respondents, making it one of the most common symptoms in the syndrome and one of the most frequently overlooked in consultation.3,4 Tier B Reduction in urinary flow is documented in the review literature, where it is described as less common than the sexual symptoms.5 The discrepancy between that characterization and the 72.7% survey figure is unexplained and probably reflects differences between clinic-derived and community-derived samples.

    Tier B Constipation is listed among the symptoms reported in the survey publication, though a frequency for it is not available in the summarized data.3 Its presence is consistent with pelvic floor involvement: defecatory dysfunction from paradoxical contraction or inadequate relaxation of the pelvic floor muscles during attempted defecation is a recognized consequence of pelvic floor dysfunction,11 and elimination problems are established features of hypertonic pelvic floor disorders.10

    Tier B Urinary hesitancy, delay in the stream, urgency and a sensation of incomplete emptying are documented features of pelvic floor dysfunction generally.10,11 They are reported by HFS patients, but the HFS-specific literature quantifies only stream reduction.

    Tier C Post-void dribbling and a divided or double urinary stream are reported by patients. Neither appears in the HFS literature. A persistently divided stream has structural causes that merit urological assessment in its own right and should not be attributed to HFS without evaluation.

    Key points

    • Reduced urinary stream affects roughly seven in ten surveyed patients.
    • Constipation is reported and is mechanistically consistent with pelvic floor involvement.
    • Broader urinary symptoms are documented in pelvic floor dysfunction but not quantified in HFS.
    • A divided stream should be assessed urologically rather than assumed to be part of the syndrome.

    8. Autonomic and psychological features

    Tier A Psychological distress, anxiety or depression was reported by 89.5% of respondents, making it the third most common symptom recorded.3,4 Tier B The original case series documented psychological symptoms ranging from mild anxiety to severe depression,1 and the review literature adds decreased libido and insomnia.5 Emotional distress was one of the four dominant themes in the forum analysis.2

    The direction of causation is not established. Abdessater and colleagues propose a sequence in which injury produces emotional distress, distress drives sympathetic stimulation, and sympathetic stimulation sustains pelvic floor muscle spasm.5 That is a proposed loop, not a demonstrated one, and it does not establish that distress is the origin. Given that nine in ten patients report distress alongside a symptom that is frightening by nature, distress as consequence is at least as plausible as distress as cause. No study has separated the two. Hypothesis

    Tier C Scrotal or testicular retraction is reported frequently by patients and does not appear in the HFS literature. A mechanism is available in principle, since scrotal position is under autonomic and somatic reflex control, and the proposed sympathetic overactivity in HFS would be expected to affect it. That reasoning is plausible and untested, and it should not be presented to patients as an explanation.

    Key points

    • Around nine in ten patients report psychological distress.
    • Whether distress precedes or follows the physical symptoms has not been determined.
    • Testicular retraction is commonly reported by patients and entirely undocumented in the literature.

    9. What makes symptoms better and worse Tier A

    Symptom variability is one of the more distinctive features of HFS and one of the few for which good numerical data exist. The pattern is consistent: positions and activities that reduce mechanical and sympathetic load improve symptoms, and those that increase it worsen them.

    Table 3 · Reported symptom modifiers (n = 143)
    FactorEffectProportion
    Lying downImproved73.0%
    StretchingImproved44.1%
    MasturbationWorsened75.9%
    StandingWorsened64.5%

    As reported by Niedenfuehr and Stevens.3 Worsening in the standing position is separately documented in the review literature.5

    The positional pattern is real, reproducible across sources, and more informative than a fluctuating symptom usually is. Muscle sympathetic nerve activity rises progressively with orthostatic stress through baroreflex resetting,12 so a symptom sustained by sympathetic outflow to the erectile tissue should worsen on standing and ease when supine. That is what roughly two-thirds and three-quarters of patients respectively describe.3 The observation therefore favors a sympathetically maintained mechanism over the alternatives.

    It does not settle the question. These are self-reported effects with no objective measurement taken in either posture, and standing alters venous drainage and pelvic floor muscle loading at the same moment it alters sympathetic tone, so three candidate explanations change together. A posture-standardized measurement — supine, then standing, with the flaccid state actually measured rather than described — would separate them, and nobody has performed one.

    Key points

    • Lying down improves symptoms for roughly three-quarters of patients; standing worsens them for roughly two-thirds.
    • Masturbation is the most frequently reported aggravating activity.
    • The positional pattern is what a sympathetically maintained mechanism predicts, which makes it one of the few discriminating observations available.
    • Standing alters venous drainage and pelvic floor muscle loading as well as sympathetic tone, so the observation favors that mechanism without confirming it.

    10. Symptoms that are not HFS Established

    Some presentations overlap with HFS at first description but indicate conditions requiring different and sometimes immediate management. This section is not a symptom list. It is an exclusion list.

    Table 4 · Presentations requiring assessment rather than cataloging

    Audible crack or snap with immediate detumescence, swelling and bruisingEmergency care; suspected penile fracture

    Peyronie’s disease, ischemic priapism and vascular erectile dysfunction are the principal differential considerations discussed in the HFS literature.4,5 The remaining entries reflect standard urological practice and are included for patient safety.

    12. Reading the pattern

    Taken together, the list describes a disorder affecting sensation, smooth muscle tone, striated pelvic floor muscle and autonomic regulation at once, in a young population, usually after a mechanical event. The proposed explanation is pathological activation of a pelvic/pudendal-hypogastric reflex, conveying excess sympathetic tone to cavernosal smooth muscle and holding it contracted.7,8 That single mechanism would account for the semi-rigid flaccid state, the impaired erection, and the coldness, since sympathetic tone maintains flaccidity and restricts inflow under normal conditions.9 It accounts less obviously for the sensory loss, and not at all for the Tier C observations.

    Two cautions for anyone using this list. First, no patient has every symptom, and the number of symptoms a patient has is not known to correlate with severity or with outcome — no study has examined that question. Second, the list is a description of a population, not a diagnostic instrument. There is no symptom count that establishes HFS, because no validated diagnostic criteria exist for it.4

    Key points

    • The symptom pattern spans sensory, smooth muscle, striated muscle and autonomic domains simultaneously.
    • The proposed sympathetic mechanism explains the flaccid state, erectile failure and coldness; it explains sensory loss less well.
    • No symptom count diagnoses HFS, and symptom number is not known to predict severity or outcome.
