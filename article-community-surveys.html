Community surveys

A running collection of survey results gathered inside the community. Two so far, with what each can and cannot support.

12 min read · 2 surveys, 307 responses

Contents

    This page collects the results of surveys conducted by the hard flaccid syndrome (HFS) community. Two have been run so far, and both are set out in full below. As others are run, their results will be added here.

    Neither is a published study and neither should be read as one. What they are is the largest structured data anyone has collected on this condition outside the formal literature, and both are larger than any clinically assessed cohort ever described in a paper.

    The final section sets out what these numbers can and cannot support. Most of it applies to any survey run this way, including any added to this page later, and it is worth reading before the tables rather than after.

    1. Symptom survey

    153 respondents. Collected 1–12 August 2024. Results originally posted to r/hardflaccidresearch.

    Only respondents who answered yes to the question — “Does your flaccid penis remain in a firm, semi-rigid state in the absence of sexual desire or arousal?” — were included in the final results.

    That screening question matters, and it is the strongest methodological feature of either survey. It filters on the defining sign rather than on self-diagnosis, which means the sample is at least internally consistent about the one thing the condition is named for.

    2. Onset poll

    154 responses. Collected 6 April – 18 May 2025. Respondents were asked to self-report how they acquired the condition. Results originally posted to r/hardflaccidresearch.

    In the posts promoting the poll, participants were asked to respond only if they currently experience HFS, defined as a perpetually firm and retracted penis. The poll was run on StrawPoll, which limits participants to one response per IP address and blocks VPN use, reducing casual manipulation of the result.

    Taken together, the grouped table puts direct mechanical injury to the penis at 122 of 154 responses — masturbation, enlargement practice, intercourse and other manipulation combined — against 10 who did not know, 3 attributing it to spinal pathology and 3 to drugs. Enlargement practice alone accounts for 47.

    3. How to read these

    Both surveys were run by patients, on patients, without institutional oversight, and they carry the limitations that come with that. The limitations are worth stating precisely rather than as a blanket disclaimer, because they affect different findings differently.

    Nobody verified any diagnosis. Every respondent self-identified. The symptom survey screened on the defining sign, which helps considerably; the onset poll relied on a description in the promoting post, which helps less. An unknown fraction of both samples may have something else.

    Both samples select for the reachable. Respondents were recruited from the online community, so they over-represent men who are still symptomatic, still looking for answers, and still posting. Men who recovered, or who gave up, are largely absent. This inflates symptom frequencies against the true population by an unknown amount.

    One response per IP address is not identity verification. It raises the cost of duplicate voting; it does not prevent it, and it cannot detect a respondent who does not have the condition.

    A checklist can only measure what is on it. The symptom survey reports frequencies for 27 listed items. Symptoms nobody thought to list return zero by construction, and the wording of an item shapes how many people endorse it.

    The two surveys are not comparable to each other. Different populations, nine months apart, different screening. Nothing from one can be cross-tabulated against the other.

    Attributed cause is not established cause. This is the most important caution on this page. The onset poll records what men believe caused their condition, which is not the same as what caused it. People reconstruct a cause after the fact, the reconstruction is shaped by what they have read in the community, and there is reason to think some reports are shaped by embarrassment in one direction or another. The poll is good evidence about beliefs and suggestive evidence about causes.

    With that said, what these surveys can support is not trivial. They establish which symptoms are common within this population and roughly in what order — useful for anyone writing a case definition. They establish that the community’s own account of onset is overwhelmingly mechanical rather than psychological or idiopathic. And they demonstrate that this population can be surveyed at all, at a scale exceeding every clinically assessed cohort in the published literature. The largest such cohort is 88 men, in a conference abstract.

    There is a published comparison available. A cross-sectional survey of 143 self-identified patients, recruited the same way and published in a peer-reviewed journal, is discussed in the literature index. The overlap in what it found is the closest thing to external validation these numbers have.
