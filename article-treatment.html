Treatment: what has been tried, and twelve conjectures

Every intervention reported in the literature, assessed against what it actually did. Then twelve conjectures.

98 min read · 39 references

Contents

    Read this first. Sections 1 to 9 describe published evidence. Section 10 does not: it is original hypothesis generation, every item in it is untested in hard flaccid syndrome, and most of it will turn out to be wrong. Nothing anywhere in this article is a treatment recommendation, and several agents discussed are prescription-only drugs that can cause harm. The purpose here is to make the reasoning visible, not to tell anyone what to take.

    Summary

    • No randomized controlled trial of any treatment for hard flaccid syndrome (HFS) has ever been published. The whole treatment evidence base is four case reports, one uncontrolled survey, one uncontrolled preprint case series and one systematic review.
    • Across the ten individually reported cases in the literature, two achieved complete resolution. In a 143-respondent survey, no treatment produced more than “little to moderate” improvement and none produced a cure.
    • The treatments most often recommended are aimed at striated pelvic floor muscle. The defining sign is contraction of cavernosal smooth muscle. These are different tissues with different innervation, and the mismatch is the most likely explanation for a decade of modest results.
    • The field’s binding constraint is not a lack of drugs. It is the absence of any objective, validated measure of the hard flaccid state, which makes every reported outcome self-assessed and every trial uninterpretable.
    • Unpublished reports, flagged throughout as such, describe sympathetic transmission being interrupted at two separate levels — the hypogastric plexus and the nerve terminal — and failing at both. If accurate, that excludes the simplest version of the prevailing theory.
    • Twelve conjectures follow. The most promising are those acting on the smooth muscle rather than on its nerve supply: Rho-kinase inhibition, which works downstream of every receptor, and the co-transmitter hypothesis, which predicts exactly the pattern of failure reported.

    1. What treatment is aiming at

    A treatment is a claim about a mechanism, whether or not the person prescribing it realizes this. Recommending pelvic floor physical therapy for hard flaccid syndrome asserts that striated muscle tension is doing the damage. Recommending an α-adrenergic antagonist asserts that noradrenaline at α1 receptors is doing it. Recommending an antidepressant asserts something else again. Most of these claims cannot be true at once, and the fact that all three are routinely offered to the same patient is a fair summary of where the field stands.

    Under the working model this site adopts, there are exactly three places a treatment can act. The injury itself — a stretched or compressed nerve, most often the dorsal branch of the pudendal nerve, sustained during vigorous masturbation or penile elongation practice.1,2 The reflex arc that the injury is proposed to have switched on, carrying excess sympathetic outflow through the hypogastric nerve.3 And the effector — cavernosal smooth muscle, contracted, which is what the patient actually feels.12 Every intervention discussed in this article can be sorted into one of those three columns, and sorting them that way explains more about the pattern of success and failure than any other organizing principle.

    Table 1 · The three targets, and what treating each one assumes

    There is a fourth column that does not fit the model at all, and it contains most of what is prescribed in practice: interventions aimed at striated pelvic floor musculature. That mismatch is addressed directly in section 4. It is raised here only to note that the largest volume of treatment in HFS is directed at a tissue that is not the tissue producing the defining sign.

    One further distinction runs through everything below. Symptom relief and disease modification are not the same objective, and treatments that achieve the first are routinely mistaken for treatments that achieve the second. A drug that softens the penis for four hours has not corrected anything. Whether it might, if given long enough or early enough, is a separate and much harder question that no published study in this condition has attempted to answer.

    Key points

    • Every treatment implies a mechanistic claim; incompatible claims are currently offered side by side.
    • Under the working model there are three legitimate targets: the lesion, the reflex, the effector.
    • Most treatment in practice is aimed at a fourth thing — striated pelvic floor muscle — which the defining sign does not implicate.
    • Temporary relief and disease modification are different goals and are consistently conflated.

    2. The complete treatment evidence

    This section is short because the evidence is. It can be stated exhaustively in one table, which is unusual for a condition first described in 2019 and discussed daily by hundreds of people on the internet.

    The 2025 systematic review searched Medline/PubMed, Embase and the Cochrane Library and identified eight studies meeting its inclusion criteria. Of the ten individually reported cases across those studies, two achieved complete symptom resolution: one after physical therapy alone, one after combined therapy with a phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitor (PDE5i), low-intensity shockwave therapy and physical therapy. The authors concluded that treatment usually requires a multimodal approach and that most treatments do not provide a complete cure.5 That is the whole of the formal evidence.

    No randomized trial, no control group, no blinding, and no objective outcome measure appears anywhere in this table.

    Three features of this table deserve to be said plainly rather than left for the reader to notice. First, the two complete resolutions are single unblinded cases in which the patient knew what he was receiving and reported the outcome himself. Second, both resolved patients had short symptom duration — one (btcalvit) was 16 years old at presentation6 — which is precisely the profile in which spontaneous recovery is most plausible, so the treatments cannot be separated from the natural history. Third, the survey with by far the largest sample found the least benefit, which is the direction of discrepancy you expect when case reports are subject to publication bias toward successes.

    The clinical context matters too. A survey of 36 sexual medicine practitioners found limited familiarity with the condition and no consistent management approach.10 Patients are therefore not choosing among well-characterized options. In most cases they are receiving whichever intervention the clinician in front of them happens to know how to deliver.

    What the unpublished reports add

    Provenance warning. What follows is not published evidence. It is a compilation of treatment responses reported by patients and by clinicians treating them, collected outside any study, with no protocol, no dose records, no control, no verification of diagnosis and no objective measure. It cannot be cited, it is subject to every reporting bias there is, and any of it could be wrong. It is used here strictly as hypothesis-generating input. It is included because ignoring it would be worse: a treatment reported to fail repeatedly is information about mechanism, and in a field with six published treatment sources, discarding that information leaves the reasoning poorer than acknowledging it with a warning attached.

    The full compilation appears in the mechanism article. Six items in it bear directly on this article, and three of them contradict what a reader would otherwise infer from the published literature alone.

    The first two rows are the consequential ones, and they should be read together. Botulinum toxin interrupts sympathetic transmission at the nerve terminal inside the target tissue. A hypogastric plexus block interrupts the same pathway far upstream, before it reaches the pelvis. These are different procedures, performed by different specialists, failing at two ends of one pathway. Neither report is reliable on its own. Together they are the strongest signal in this article, and section 10 is organized around them.

    Key points

    • The whole treatment literature is four case reports, one survey, one preprint case series and one systematic review.
    • Two of ten reported cases resolved completely; both had short duration, and neither was controlled.
    • The largest study found the smallest effect, which is what publication bias toward successful cases predicts.
    • Unpublished reports, flagged as such, add one pattern the literature does not contain: sympathetic interruption reportedly fails at both the plexus and the nerve terminal.
    • Specialist familiarity with the condition is low, so treatment selection is driven largely by clinician availability.

    3. Reading the survey correctly

    The Niedenfuehr and Stevens survey is the only dataset in which more than a handful of patients rated more than one treatment, so it carries disproportionate weight in discussions of what works. It is worth reading carefully, because the headline finding is routinely inverted.4

    The study recruited 143 self-identified respondents online between 9 May and 9 June 2023. Efficacy was rated on a Patient Global Impression of Change scale from 1 to 7, on which higher scores indicate greater improvement, and satisfaction on a separate eleven-point scale.

    On the separate satisfaction scale, PDE5 inhibitors again scored highest at 4.8 ± 2.6 and nerve blocks lowest at 1.9 ± 1.5. No respondent was completely satisfied with any treatment.

    “PDE5 inhibitors scored highest” is frequently reported as though it meant PDE5 inhibitors help. Read the number instead of the ranking. A mean of 2.6 on a 1–7 scale sits below the midpoint. The gap between the best and worst treatment is 1.2 scale points, which is small relative to the standard deviations, and the ordering of the bottom eight is essentially noise. What the table actually shows is nine treatments clustered near “no improvement,” one of which is slightly less bad than the others. The authors’ own conclusion was that current therapies generally do not benefit most patients and that new strategies are needed.4

    The methodological limitations are severe and the authors acknowledge them. Participants self-identified as having HFS with no clinical verification, so an unknown fraction may have had something else. Recruitment through social media selects for the most symptomatic and the least satisfied. Treatments were self-selected rather than assigned, so the most severely affected patients accumulate the most treatments, pulling every score downward. Dose, duration, quality of delivery and time since onset were not controlled. And per-treatment sample sizes are not distributed evenly, so the categories are not equally precise.

    None of that makes the survey worthless. It makes it good evidence for one claim and weak evidence for another. It is good evidence that no currently available treatment reliably produces large improvement in a chronic, self-identified HFS population. It is weak evidence about the relative ranking of specific treatments, and it says nothing at all about what any of them would do if given early, at an adequate dose, to correctly diagnosed patients.

    Key points

    • Every treatment in the survey scored below the scale midpoint for improvement.
    • “PDE5i ranked first” means least ineffective, not effective.
    • Self-selection, absent diagnostic verification and uncontrolled dosing make the between-treatment ranking unreliable.
    • The survey establishes that nothing currently reliably works in chronic disease. It does not establish that nothing could.

    4. Physical therapy: most recommended, least justified

    Pelvic floor physical therapy is the most frequently offered treatment for hard flaccid syndrome and appears in both reported complete resolutions.5,6,8 It also scored 1.8 out of 7 in the largest survey.4 Reconciling those two facts requires being precise about what the therapy can plausibly do.

    The mechanistic problem is straightforward and, as far as it goes, decisive. The pelvic floor is striated muscle under somatic control through the pudendal nerve. Cavernosal erectile tissue is smooth muscle under autonomic control through the cavernous nerves and the hypogastric outflow.12 A striated muscle cannot set the resting tone of a smooth muscle it does not innervate. Manual release of the levator ani, however skillfully performed, has no direct route to the contractile state of the corpora cavernosa. Whatever benefit physical therapy produces in HFS, it is not produced by relaxing the tissue that generates the defining sign.

    Established That physical therapy has real value in conditions where the target genuinely is striated muscle is not in question. In a randomized multicenter trial in women with interstitial cystitis / painful bladder syndrome and pelvic floor tenderness, ten sessions of myofascial physical therapy produced moderate or marked improvement in 59% of participants compared with 26% receiving global therapeutic massage.32 That is a genuine effect against an active comparator. It is also a different disease, a different tissue and a different endpoint.

    Hypothesis There are three routes by which physical therapy could still help someone with HFS, none of which requires it to be treating the cause. It can treat genuinely comorbid myofascial pain, which is common and which contributes to the total symptom burden even if it did not initiate anything. It can reduce guarding, protective posture and provocative behavior through education and graded exposure, which is the explicit content of the biopsychosocial protocol that reported 85% improvement at three months.7 And it provides sustained clinical contact with someone who takes the complaint seriously, which is not a trivial intervention in a condition where most patients have been dismissed.

    The practical consequence is a distinction worth holding onto. Physical therapy is reasonable adjunctive care with a plausible symptomatic rationale and a poor causal one. Presenting it as a cure, which happens routinely, sets up a specific and avoidable harm: when months of therapy do not resolve the hard flaccid state, patients frequently conclude that they failed the treatment rather than that the treatment was aimed at the wrong tissue.

    Key points

    • Pelvic floor muscle is striated and somatically innervated; cavernosal muscle is smooth and autonomically innervated. Therapy directed at one does not act on the other.
    • Myofascial physical therapy has genuine randomized evidence in urological pelvic pain, but for a different tissue and a different endpoint.
    • It remains reasonable adjunctive care for comorbid myofascial pain, guarding and behavior change.
    • Framing it as curative produces predictable harm when it does not cure.

    5. Oral drugs

    PDE5 inhibitors

    Sildenafil, tadalafil and their relatives inhibit the breakdown of cyclic GMP, the second messenger through which nitric oxide relaxes cavernosal smooth muscle.12 They amplify an existing signal rather than generating one, which is why they require sexual stimulation to work.

    The evidence in HFS is contradictory in an informative way. In the original four-case series, PDE5 inhibitors were ineffective.1 In the survey they were the highest-rated of nine treatments, at 2.6 out of 7.4 In the one case with a durable two-year outcome they were part of the regimen, given as tadalafil 5 mg daily.8 The most economical reading is that PDE5 inhibitors partially address the erectile dysfunction that accompanies HFS — which is what they are designed to do — without touching the flaccid-state rigidity, and that patients rating them highest are rating that partial benefit.

    Hypothesis There is a second and more interesting rationale for daily rather than on-demand dosing, developed further in section 10. Persistently contracted cavernosal tissue is poorly perfused tissue, and in an animal model cavernous neurotomy produced hypoxia followed by fibrosis in the corpus cavernosum.34 If chronic HFS carries a comparable risk, then a drug that intermittently increases oxygenation may matter for reasons that have nothing to do with how it makes an erection.

    α-adrenergic antagonists

    This is the class the prevailing theory points to most directly. If the hard flaccid state is sympathetically driven contraction mediated by noradrenaline, then blocking the receptor should relieve it. The AUANews commentary proposing the pelvic/pudendal-hypogastric reflex named α-blockers including doxazosin as a therapeutic implication of the model.3 No study reporting on that implication has been published in the seven years since.

    Established The receptor biology is well characterized and supports the attempt. Human corpus cavernosum expresses α1a, α1b and α1d adrenoceptor messenger RNA, with predominant expression of the α1a subtype.13 α-adrenergic blockade with phentolamine increases the efficacy of vasodilators in cavernosal tissue.14 The target exists and is druggable.

    What is missing is any report of what happens when patients take these drugs. The Niedenfuehr survey did not include α-blockers as a category, so even uncontrolled self-report is unavailable. This is the single largest gap between what the leading theory predicts and what anyone has checked, and section 10 returns to it, because the version of the experiment that has been proposed is not the version most likely to be informative.

    Neuropathic pain agents

    Gabapentin, pregabalin, duloxetine and tricyclic antidepressants are standard for neuropathic pain, and HFS following a nerve stretch injury is a reasonable setting for them. Their limits should be stated in numbers. In a meta-analysis of 229 studies, the number needed to treat for 50% pain relief was 6.4 (95% CI 5.2–8.4) for serotonin– noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors, 7.7 (6.5–9.4) for pregabalin and 7.2 (5.9–9.21) for gabapentin; tricyclics had the lowest number needed to treat among first-line agents, and the authors described trial outcomes overall as modest.31

    A number needed to treat of seven means six of every seven patients get no meaningful pain relief. That is the realistic expectation, and it is for pain — the one symptom these drugs target. They have no mechanism by which to reduce cavernosal smooth muscle tone. In the HFS survey, nerve pain medications scored 1.4 out of 7, the joint lowest of any category.4 That is consistent with their being used to treat a symptom that, in most respondents, is not the dominant one.

    Muscle relaxants, anxiolytics and antidepressants

    Cyclobenzaprine, baclofen, tizanidine and diazepam act on striated muscle tone through central mechanisms. They scored 1.5 out of 7.4 The mechanistic objection is the same as for pelvic floor therapy: these agents reduce somatic muscle tone, and the defining sign is not somatic muscle tone. Anxiolytics and antidepressants appear in the systematic review as reported management,5 and treating comorbid anxiety or depression is worth doing on its own merits, since both are common in this population.1,2 Neither should be presented as treatment for the underlying condition.

    Key points

    • PDE5 inhibitors probably help the accompanying erectile dysfunction and not the flaccid rigidity; daily dosing has a separate tissue-preservation rationale that has never been tested here.
    • α-adrenergic antagonists are what the prevailing theory predicts should work, and no one has published what happens when they are given.
    • Neuropathic agents treat pain with a number needed to treat around seven and have no route to smooth muscle tone.
    • Muscle relaxants act on the wrong muscle type. Psychiatric treatment is worth doing for its own indication, not as treatment for HFS.

    6. Injections and blocks

    Intracavernosal phentolamine

    Phentolamine occupies a strange position: it is the only intervention reported to abolish the hard flaccid state, and it is also the weakest evidence in the entire literature. The observation that intracavernosal phentolamine temporarily resolves the hard flaccid state and induces erection is offered as support for the reflex model.3 It is reported without a sample size, dose, protocol, control or objective measure.

    The interpretive difficulty is that phentolamine produces erection in men without HFS too, so an erection following injection cannot by itself distinguish a normalized resting tone from a drug doing exactly what it does in anyone. The counterargument is genuinely stronger than that objection suggests and is set out at length in the article on the reflex model. In brief: cavernosal smooth muscle is autonomically controlled, so if the defining sign is contraction of that smooth muscle then an autonomic mechanism is very hard to avoid, and if parasympathetic-dependent relaxation can still be produced pharmacologically then the sympathetic side is the plausible location of the fault. That is a reasonable argument by elimination. It is not the same thing as a demonstration, and the missing experiment — a measured tone endpoint before and after injection, against saline, in patients and controls — remains straightforward to perform and undone.

    Nerve blocks

    Nerve blocks scored 1.6 out of 7 for efficacy and lowest of all treatments for satisfaction at 1.9 out of 11.4 Two different procedures are usually conflated under the term, and they test different propositions.

    A pudendal nerve block anesthetizes the somatic afferent supply. If it abolishes pain, the pain is transmitted through that nerve, which is diagnostically useful. It should not be expected to abolish the hard flaccid state, since the pudendal nerve does not supply cavernosal smooth muscle. A superior hypogastric plexus block targets the sympathetic outflow to the pelvis at the L5–S1 level and is therefore aimed at the exact anatomical funnel the reflex model proposes. In chronic pelvic pain generally, this block provides pain relief but the evidence supporting it is low grade, and reviewers recommend it as an alternative rather than a primary therapy.27 Its use in HFS has not been formally reported, and the accounts that circulate among patients are unpublished and cannot be cited here.

    Hypothesis If sympathetic blockade at the hypogastric plexus genuinely fails to relieve the hard flaccid state — and this remains unverified — that would be the most mechanistically important negative result available, because it would mean the state cannot be purely neurogenic at the time it is treated. The argument is developed in the article on mechanism conjectures. It is raised here because it determines what an effective treatment would have to look like: something acting on the smooth muscle itself rather than on the nerve supplying it.

    Botulinum toxin

    Established Intracavernosal botulinum toxin A has been studied in refractory erectile dysfunction in randomized placebo-controlled trials. A trial comparing 50 and 100 unit doses against saline in vasculogenic ED unresponsive to PDE5 inhibitors or trimix found a durable effect lasting at least three to six months, with roughly 40% of patients able to resume sexual activity and roughly 60% still unable to complete intercourse.23 A second double-blind randomized placebo-controlled trial in 70 patients with ED refractory to PDE5 inhibitors reported comparable findings.24 Neither study involved HFS, and the population — men with vascular disease — is close to the opposite of the HFS population, in whom penile hemodynamics are normal.9

    The reason it was worth trying in HFS comes from a different disease. Raynaud’s phenomenon is an episodic, cold-provoked, sympathetically-mediated vasospasm of a peripheral vascular bed, and botulinum toxin has been examined there in systematic reviews with reported symptomatic benefit, on evidence the reviewers describe as limited.26 A persistently contracted, cold, sympathetically-driven vascular bed at the end of the body is a close enough parallel that the attempt made sense.

    In hard flaccid syndrome there is no published report of botulinum toxin at all. A PubMed search combining the condition with botulinum or Botox returns nothing. It has, however, reportedly been given to HFS patients on several occasions without success — an unpublished observation carrying every limitation set out in section 2.

    That is a more useful piece of information than a positive report of the same weight would be, and section 10 treats it as the pivot of the whole argument. The reason is specific to what botulinum toxin does. It is the only available way to switch off adrenergic transmission inside the erectile tissue itself, downstream of every nerve, plexus and ganglion. If that fails, the failure cannot be explained away by saying the drug did not reach the target.

    Key points

    • Phentolamine is the only reported intervention that abolishes the hard flaccid state, on evidence with no sample size, control or measurement.
    • Pudendal blocks and hypogastric plexus blocks test different propositions and should not be described interchangeably.
    • A well-documented failure of hypogastric sympathetic blockade would be the most informative negative result in the field.
    • Intracavernosal botulinum toxin has randomized safety and efficacy data in a different ED population, and has never been tried in HFS.

    7. Devices, procedures and surgery

    Low-intensity shockwave therapy

    Shockwave therapy appears in the one HFS case with durable two-year resolution, given as six sessions at an energy flux density of 0.13 mJ/mm², frequency 3 Hz, and 3,600 total shocks distributed across the penis, alongside daily tadalafil and ten to twelve weeks of physical therapy.8 It scored 1.6 out of 7 in the survey.4 The American Urological Association classifies low-intensity shockwave therapy for erectile dysfunction as investigational, noting that randomized sham-controlled trials do not clearly indicate that benefits outweigh risks and burdens.33

    There is a further problem specific to HFS. The rationale for shockwave in ED is neovascularization in ischemic tissue. Men with HFS have normal peak systolic velocity, normal end-diastolic velocity and no elevated echogenicity on B-mode scanning.9 A treatment that works by improving blood supply is being applied to a population whose blood supply has been measured and found normal.

    Nerve decompression surgery

    Established Pudendal nerve decompression has randomized evidence in pudendal neuralgia. In a trial of 32 patients with chronic perineal pain who had responded temporarily to blocks at the ischial spine and in Alcock’s canal, 16 were randomized to surgery and 16 to non-surgical management. At three months, 50% of the surgical group were improved against 6.2% of controls (p = 0.0155); at twelve months, 71.4% against 13.3% (p = 0.0025). No complications were encountered.28

    Hypothesis A closer precedent exists. In a retrospective series of eight women with persistent genital arousal disorder treated by neurolysis of the dorsal branch of the pudendal nerve, seven of eight had complete resolution of arousal symptoms and six of seven reported pain reduction at follow-up beyond 26 weeks.29 This is the same nerve branch implicated in HFS, operated on for a condition that the 2021 and 2026 consensus documents place in the same diagnostic family as HFS.11 It is the strongest surgical signal anywhere adjacent to this condition.

    The qualifications are heavy. Eight patients, retrospective, unblinded, one surgeon, one sex, and a different presenting symptom. Selection was by response to diagnostic block, which is a specific and demanding criterion that most HFS patients have never been tested against. And decompression treats compression: in HFS the proposed mechanism is a stretch injury, which is a different lesion and not obviously corrected by releasing surrounding tissue. No series of decompression in HFS has been published.

    Lumbar spine surgery

    Lumbar discectomy appears once in the systematic review as reported management.5 The rationale comes from a conference abstract reporting lumbosacral annular tears in 76% of 21 men with HFS who underwent magnetic resonance imaging, in a study without a control group — a critical omission, since annular tears are common in asymptomatic adults. Spinal surgery for HFS on that basis is not supportable. Where a patient has independent neurological indications for spinal surgery, those indications stand on their own.

    Platelet-rich plasma and stem cells

    Established The AUA guideline classifies intracavernosal stem cell therapy for erectile dysfunction as investigational and states that platelet-rich plasma should not be offered outside an institutional review board-approved research protocol.33 That is for erectile dysfunction, where the target — damaged vascular and smooth muscle tissue — at least matches the proposed mechanism of the therapy. In HFS the tissue has been shown to be structurally normal.9 These are also among the most expensive interventions offered to this population, sold directly to patients who have been told nothing else exists.

    Key points

    • Shockwave therapy works, if it works, by improving perfusion in ischemic tissue. HFS penile hemodynamics are normal.
    • Pudendal decompression has randomized support in pudendal neuralgia, and dorsal branch neurolysis has an eight-patient series in PGAD with striking results — the closest surgical precedent, and still very thin.
    • The annular tear finding lacked a control group and cannot justify spinal surgery.
    • PRP and stem cell therapy are investigational for ED and are being sold for a condition in which the tissue is structurally intact.

    8. Interventions with no mechanistic route

    A short section, because these can be dealt with briefly, and because dealing with them briefly is itself the honest treatment of them. Diet and nutrition changes scored 1.6 out of 7 and anti-inflammatory medications 1.5.4 Supplements, elimination diets, gut protocols, abstinence regimens, stretching routines and posture correction have no published evidence in HFS and no proposed pathway from the intervention to cavernosal smooth muscle tone.

    This is not a claim that they are harmful. General health measures are reasonable for general health reasons, and a patient who sleeps better and exercises sensibly will cope better with a chronic condition. The claim is narrower: none of them has a mechanism by which it could relieve sustained smooth muscle contraction, and treating them as candidate cures costs money, time and — the largest cost — the patient’s estimate of how much control he has over the outcome. Repeated failure of interventions that were never plausible is read by most people as personal failure rather than as evidence that the interventions were wrong.

    Key points

    • Dietary, supplement, abstinence and postural interventions have no evidence and no mechanism in HFS.
    • General health measures remain worth doing for general health reasons.
    • The main cost of implausible treatments is not financial; it is the false attribution of their failure to the patient.

    9. The measurement problem

    Everything above shares one defect, and it is the defect that has to be fixed before anything else can improve. There is no objective measure of the hard flaccid state.

    Consider what the outcome measure was in each study. In the case reports, whether the patient said the rigidity had regressed.6,7,8 In the survey, a number from 1 to 7 chosen by the respondent.4 In the phentolamine observation, whether an erection was observed.3 Not one study in this literature has measured the thing the condition is named for.

    The consequences compound. Without a measure there can be no blinding worth the name, because a patient who knows he received an injection and is asked whether his penis feels softer will answer differently from one who does not. Without a measure there is no way to detect a small real effect, and small real effects are exactly what an early field needs to find in order to know which direction to keep walking. Without a measure the natural history is unknown, so improvement cannot be separated from time. Without a measure there is no way to establish that two patients in two clinics have the same severity, which means multicenter work is impossible. And without a measure, no regulator will approve anything, because there is no endpoint to approve it against.

    Speculative Several candidate measures exist and none has been validated in this condition. Penile durometry or tonometry could quantify resting rigidity directly, and is the most obvious approach, though no instrument has been standardized for this purpose. Color Doppler ultrasonography in the flaccid state can characterize cavernosal artery flow and has already been used in HFS to establish that it is normal,9 so a posture-standardized version — supine and then standing, given that 64.5% of patients report worsening on standing — would test the sympathetic prediction directly and could be done with equipment already present in every sexual medicine clinic. Cavernosal electromyography exists in research settings but is poorly standardized and invasive.

    Shear-wave elastography is the most promising of the group and the least discussed. It measures tissue stiffness non-invasively and quantitatively, which is close to a direct measurement of what the hard flaccid state is. It has already been applied to penile tissue for a related purpose: a pilot study used penile shear-wave elastography to predict which men with erectile dysfunction unresponsive to PDE5 inhibitors would respond to intracavernosal botulinum toxin.38 That study is small, in a different population, and reported as a pilot. Its importance here is narrower and solid: the technique exists, it has been applied to the penis, and it produces a number.

    Two of these I would fund first. Shear-wave elastography, because it is the only candidate that measures the defining sign directly rather than a correlate of it, and because the instrument already sits in radiology departments. And the standing-versus-supine Doppler protocol, because it requires no new technology at all and directly interrogates the central prediction of the prevailing model. Both are the rare studies that would be informative whichever way they came out.

    Key points

    • No study has ever objectively measured the hard flaccid state. Every reported outcome is self-assessed.
    • This makes blinding ineffective, small effects undetectable, natural history unknown and multicenter work impossible.
    • Candidate measures include durometry, posture-standardized flaccid-state Doppler, cavernosal electromyography and shear-wave elastography. None is validated in HFS, though elastography has been applied to penile tissue elsewhere.
    • Solving measurement is prerequisite to every conjecture in the next section.

    10. Twelve conjectures

    Everything from here is speculative. None of these conjectures has been tested in hard flaccid syndrome. They are stated precisely so that they can be refuted, not because they are likely to be right. Several involve prescription-only drugs with serious risks. Nothing here should be used to select, request or avoid a treatment.

    The conjectures below follow from one organizing idea, developed at length in the mechanism article: that HFS is probably not one lesion but two compartments — an initiating peripheral injury and a maintaining process that becomes progressively independent of it. If that is right, the single most important variable in any treatment decision is not which drug but how long the illness has lasted, and the field’s habit of pooling patients regardless of duration may be concealing effects that exist.

    One reported observation does more work here than any published study, and it should be stated before the conjectures rather than buried among them. Sympathetic transmission to the erectile tissue has reportedly been interrupted at two levels — upstream at the superior hypogastric plexus, and at the nerve terminal itself with intracavernosal botulinum toxin — and reportedly failed at both. Neither report is published and either could be wrong. But if both are right, they exclude the simplest version of the prevailing theory: that the penis is hard because noradrenaline is continuously arriving at it. Something else is holding the smooth muscle contracted by the time anyone treats it. The conjectures are ordered by how well each survives that conclusion, which promotes the two that act on the smooth muscle rather than on its nerve supply (III and IV) and demotes those that act upstream (II, VIII, IX).

    Conjecture I · Stratify by duration, in every study, without exception Speculative

    Claim. Time since onset is the largest single determinant of treatment response in HFS, larger than the choice of treatment, and pooling patients across durations makes real effects disappear.

    Reasoning. If an initiating peripheral lesion hands off over months to a maintaining process in the effector tissue or the spinal cord, then a peripherally directed treatment given at four months and the same treatment given at four years are two different experiments. Averaging them yields a null. Both reported complete resolutions in the literature involved short-duration illness, one in a 16-year-old (btcalvit) at presentation.6,8 The 88-man hemodynamic cohort had a mean symptom duration of 14 ± 20 months, and that standard deviation is larger than the mean, which tells you the population is not homogeneous.9 The survey, which found nothing works, sampled a chronic online population.4

    Test. Report every outcome stratified into bands — under six months, six to twenty-four months, over twenty-four months. This costs nothing. It requires no new drug, no new equipment and no new patients, only the discipline to record and report a variable that is already being collected. It should be a condition of publication in this field.

    Assessment

    • Free to implement and immediately actionable.
    • If wrong, costs nothing; if right, it explains most of the field’s negative results.
    • Priority: Highest.

    Conjecture II · Subtype-selective α1A blockade rather than generic α-blockade Speculative

    Claim. If α-adrenergic blockade is to be tested in HFS, an α1A-selective antagonist is the correct probe, and a non-selective one is likely to produce a false negative.

    Reasoning. Human corpus cavernosum expresses α1a, α1b and α1d adrenoceptor mRNA with predominant expression of the α1a subtype.13 The α1-antagonists differ substantially in subtype selectivity.15 Doxazosin, the agent named in the reflex commentary,3 is not subtype-selective; its dose is limited by orthostatic hypotension arising from α1B blockade in systemic vasculature, which caps receptor occupancy at the cavernosal target. Silodosin is markedly α1A-selective and correspondingly better tolerated at the vascular level.15 The prediction is direct: if noradrenaline at α1 is transducing the sympathetic drive, silodosin should outperform doxazosin at equivalent tolerability.

    What its failure would mean. This is the conjecture’s real value. If a properly dosed, duration-stratified trial of an α1A-selective antagonist produces nothing, then α1-mediated noradrenergic contraction is not what is holding the smooth muscle contracted — and the entire field can stop looking there. The prevailing theory has named this drug class for seven years without anyone reporting the result.3 A negative here is worth more than most positives elsewhere.

    Tempered expectation. Two things argue against a large effect before the trial begins. Unpublished reports describe α-blockers reducing the state in some patients and not others — mixed rather than null, which is the pattern of a drug acting on a contributing mechanism rather than the controlling one. And if intracavernosal botulinum toxin genuinely fails, as Conjecture VII discusses, then removing noradrenaline from the synapse entirely changes nothing, and blocking the receptor that noradrenaline acts on should be expected to fare no better. The two interventions test nearly the same proposition from opposite sides.

    Why it is still worth doing. Nearly the same proposition is not the same proposition. Receptor blockade also covers circulating catecholamines and any non-neuronal source of noradrenaline, neither of which botulinum toxin touches. If denervation supersensitivity has upregulated α1A receptors, blocking them is the intervention that addresses it and silencing terminals is not. And the drugs are approved, generic and cheap, which makes this the least expensive way to close a question the prevailing theory has left open since 2023.

    Caveat. Silodosin causes retrograde or absent ejaculation in a large proportion of men. In a population already distressed about sexual function, that adverse effect is not incidental and would need to be disclosed and counted as a primary tolerability outcome, not a footnote.

    Assessment

    • Uses approved, inexpensive, widely available drugs.
    • Directly tests the treatment implication of the prevailing theory, which remains unreported.
    • Reported botulinum toxin failure predicts a modest result at best; unpublished reports of α-blockers describe mixed responses.
    • Still worth doing, because it covers circulating catecholamines and upregulated receptors, which terminal silencing does not.
    • Priority: High. Demoted from highest by the botulinum reports.

    Conjecture III · Target the co-transmitter, not only the transmitter Speculative

    Claim. If neuropeptide Y rather than noradrenaline is sustaining the contraction, then every α-blocker will fail, botulinum toxin will fail, and both failures will be misread as evidence against a sympathetic mechanism when they are evidence about which sympathetic mechanism.

    Reasoning. Sympathetic vasoconstrictor neurons release noradrenaline and neuropeptide Y from different vesicle populations. In isolated blood vessels, botulinum neurotoxin A significantly reduced noradrenaline-mediated contraction — noradrenergic responses to 1 Hz stimulation in uterine arteries were almost abolished and responses at 10 Hz reduced by 50 to 60% — while sustained contractions mediated by neuropeptide Y were not affected at all.25 Neuropeptide Y transmission is characteristically recruited at high firing frequencies and produces slow, prolonged contraction, which is a closer match to a state that persists for years than fast adrenergic signaling is.

    The prediction has already been tested, accidentally. This conjecture was originally offered as an explanation waiting for a pattern to explain. The pattern appears to exist. Intracavernosal botulinum toxin is reported to fail in HFS, and botulinum toxin is the one intervention that removes noradrenaline release while leaving neuropeptide Y release intact.25 That is not a general failure of sympathetic treatment. It is a failure with a specific shape, and the shape matches. The observation is unpublished and could be wrong, but if it holds, this stops being the most speculative item in this section and becomes one of the two best-supported.

    Test. The diagnostic version is available now: a patient in whom the hard flaccid state resists both adequate α1A blockade and intracavernosal botulinum toxin, but still responds to phentolamine or to a direct smooth muscle relaxant, is telling you the signal is not arriving through α1 and is not carried by noradrenaline. The therapeutic version is not available. Selective neuropeptide Y Y1 receptor antagonists have no clinical formulation, which converts this conjecture from a trial into a drug development case. That is worth stating plainly rather than treating as a disappointment: identifying a target that lacks a drug is a more productive position than continuing to test drugs against a target that may not be there.

    Assessment

    • Explains a specific pattern of treatment failure rather than treatment failure in general.
    • The pattern its prediction requires — noradrenaline removed, nothing changes — is what the botulinum reports describe.
    • No clinically available agent exists, so it cannot be tested therapeutically today.
    • Priority: High, promoted. Explanatory value now exceeds most items here.

    Conjecture IV · Rho-kinase inhibition, as the discriminating experiment Speculative

    Claim. Inhibiting Rho-kinase should relieve the hard flaccid state even where receptor-level blockade does not, because it acts downstream of every receptor.

    Reasoning. Established The RhoA/Rho-kinase pathway maintains cavernosal smooth muscle contraction by calcium sensitization: Rho-kinase inhibits myosin light chain phosphatase, so the smooth muscle stays contracted at a given calcium concentration rather than requiring more calcium.18,37 Pharmacological antagonism of Rho-kinase with Y-27632 raised intracavernosal pressure and initiated erection in rats through a nitric oxide-independent pathway.16 That last detail is what makes this the discriminating experiment. A pathway that produces relaxation without needing nitric oxide, and without needing the adrenergic receptor to be unoccupied, bypasses both of the places where HFS treatment currently fails.

    A mechanism of calcium sensitization also explains something the receptor model struggles with: how a smooth muscle stays contracted for years without a correspondingly sustained signal. It does not need one. It has been re-tuned.

    The failed interventions point here. Both reported failures — plexus blockade and intracavernosal botulinum toxin — act on the delivery of a signal to the smooth muscle. Neither touches what that muscle does with the calcium it already has. If a pathway can be interrupted at the plexus and again at the nerve terminal without the penis softening, the remaining candidate is a contractile apparatus that no longer depends on the pathway. Rho-kinase inhibition is the only conjecture here that acts at that level, which is why it moves to the front of the queue by elimination rather than by enthusiasm.

    Availability. Fasudil is a Rho-kinase inhibitor in clinical use for cerebral vasospasm after subarachnoid hemorrhage, with an established clinical safety record in that indication,20 and the class has been extensively reviewed for wider therapeutic potential.19 Its limitation is systemic hypotension, which is exactly the problem Conjecture V is designed to solve.

    What its failure would mean. If relaxing the smooth muscle at the level of myosin light chain phosphatase does not soften the penis, then the hard flaccid state is not primarily sustained smooth muscle contraction, and the first assumption underlying this entire site is wrong. There is no more consequential experiment available.

    Assessment

    • Acts downstream of every receptor and every transmitter, so it tests the contraction rather than a particular signal to it.
    • The one level neither of the reportedly failed interventions reached.
    • Human data are entirely absent for this indication; the erectile evidence is rodent.
    • Systemic hypotension is the binding constraint and motivates topical delivery.
    • Priority: Highest. The leading therapeutic candidate in this article.

    Conjecture V · Deliver smooth muscle relaxants topically, not systemically Speculative

    Claim. The route of administration, not the choice of agent, is the limiting factor for every smooth muscle relaxant considered in HFS, and a topical formulation removes that limit.

    Reasoning. The same property that makes these drugs useful — they relax vascular smooth muscle — makes them dangerous systemically, and the maximum tolerated oral dose is set by blood pressure in the rest of the body rather than by anything happening in the penis. A topical route confines the drug to the target, permits repeated on-demand use, and is acceptable to patients who will not self-inject.

    Two lines of evidence say this is feasible rather than merely desirable. Established Topical alprostadil cream produced dose-related improvement in erectile function in its phase II program,22 which demonstrates that a vasoactive agent applied to penile skin reaches erectile tissue at a pharmacologically active concentration. And in the specific case of Rho-kinase inhibition, it has already been done in animals: topical Y-27632 applied either to the tunica albuginea or to the glans and surrounding skin produced a marked increase in erectile response in intact and castrated rats, both with and without stimulation of the autonomic innervation.17 The authors specifically noted that high doses reduced systemic blood pressure and that clinical value would depend on dosing to avoid that.

    The phrase “with and without stimulation of the autonomic innervation” is the important one. An agent that relaxes cavernosal muscle without requiring intact neural input is an agent that could work in a condition where the neural input is the problem.

    Objections. Penile skin permeability to a given molecule is compound-specific and cannot be assumed from alprostadil. Systemic absorption through the glans is real and would have to be measured, not presumed absent. Partner transfer is a genuine safety issue for any topical genital preparation. And rodent erectile pharmacology has an unimpressive record of translating to humans.

    Assessment

    • Solves the dose-limiting problem shared by every agent in Conjectures II, III and IV.
    • Feasibility supported by human data for one vasoactive agent and animal data for the specific class of interest.
    • Formulation and absorption work is required before any trial.
    • Priority: High, as an enabling step rather than a treatment in itself.

    Conjecture VI · Local warming as a pharmacological adjunct Speculative

    Claim. Sustained local warming of the penis should shift cavernosal smooth muscle toward relaxation, and could plausibly be combined with a topical agent in a single preparation.

    Reasoning. The rationale is not that warmth is soothing. It is that the enzymatic step governing smooth muscle relaxation is unusually temperature sensitive. In a study of the mechanisms by which temperature affects excitation–contraction coupling in smooth muscle, the rate of relaxation was found to be very temperature-sensitive, in contrast to calcium entry and release, which were largely temperature-insensitive; myosin light chain phosphatase activity was identified as the predominant mechanism through which temperature affects relaxation.21 Myosin light chain phosphatase is the same enzyme that Rho-kinase inhibits. If cooling slows the phosphatase and favors maintained force, warming should do the opposite. There is a clinical observation that fits: patients report the hard flaccid state worsening in cold conditions, and the glans is described as cold in the original symptom catalogs.2

    The objections are serious and must be stated with the conjecture. The temperature study found species-dependent results — cooling increased force in rat ureter and decreased it in guinea pig ureter — so the direction of the effect is not universal across smooth muscles and has never been measured in human cavernosal tissue.21 Surface warming of a few degrees may not change the temperature of deep cavernosal tissue meaningfully, since the corpora are perfused by blood at core temperature. Any benefit might be entirely explained by cutaneous vasodilation, which is not the same target. And the cold glans reported in HFS is plausibly a consequence of reduced flow rather than a cause of contraction, which reverses the direction of the argument.

    Test. This is cheap to falsify. Apply a controlled thermal stimulus and measure the hard flaccid state before and after with whichever objective measure section 9 produces. If warming does nothing measurable, discard the idea. The more interesting version is combination: a preparation that warms and delivers a relaxant at once, on the reasoning that a warmer tissue bed both increases dermal absorption and biases the contractile apparatus in the same direction as the drug. That is two speculative steps stacked, and should be labeled as such.

    Assessment

    • Rests on a real and specific enzymatic mechanism rather than on comfort.
    • Direction of the temperature effect is species-dependent and unmeasured in human erectile tissue.
    • Cheap, non-invasive and rapidly falsifiable once a measure exists.
    • Priority: Moderate as an adjunct. Low as a monotherapy.

    Conjecture VII · Botulinum toxin has been tried, and its failure is the most useful result available Speculative

    Claim. Intracavernosal botulinum toxin is reported to have failed in HFS. If that is accurate, the hard flaccid state is not maintained by ongoing noradrenaline release from sympathetic terminals in the erectile tissue — and three other conjectures in this article have to be reweighted accordingly.

    Why this particular failure carries weight. Most negative results in this field are ambiguous because the intervention might not have reached the target. Botulinum toxin does not have that escape route. It is injected directly into the tissue that is contracted. It cleaves SNAP-25 and impairs vesicular release at the terminal, and in isolated vessels this significantly reduced noradrenaline-mediated contraction — noradrenergic responses to 1 Hz stimulation in uterine arteries were almost abolished.25 The technique has randomized placebo-controlled trials behind it in refractory erectile dysfunction, with a duration of action of at least three to six months.23,24 So the procedure works, in that tissue, for that long. It is the closest thing available to switching off local sympathetic transmission and waiting to see what happens.

    Combined with the block reports, this becomes a convergence. Superior hypogastric plexus blockade is reported to fail as well. That is an interruption of the same pathway several segments upstream, performed by different clinicians using a different technique. Two interruptions of one pathway at opposite ends, both failing, is a much stronger argument than either alone, because the failure modes are not shared: a plexus block can miss anatomically, an intracavernosal injection cannot miss the corpora.

    What survives, if this is right. Three explanations remain, and they are the three most promising items in this article rather than a dead end.

    Co-transmitter dominance. Botulinum toxin attenuates noradrenaline release but leaves neuropeptide Y-mediated contraction entirely unaffected.25 A treatment that removes noradrenaline and changes nothing is precisely what the neuropeptide Y hypothesis predicts. This upgrades Conjecture III from an idea to the explanation with the most direct supporting observation in this article.

    Effector autonomy. If the smooth muscle is held contracted by calcium sensitization rather than by ongoing transmitter, then removing the transmitter accomplishes nothing, because the contractile apparatus has been re-tuned and no longer requires a sustained signal.18,37 This makes Conjecture IV the leading candidate: Rho-kinase inhibition acts downstream of every receptor and every transmitter, which is the one place neither of the failed interventions reached.

    Denervation supersensitivity. There is an uncomfortable third possibility worth stating. Botulinum toxin is a chemical denervation, and denervated smooth muscle upregulates receptors. In a tissue already receiving abnormal sympathetic input, an intervention that silences terminals for three to six months could in principle leave the smooth muscle more responsive afterward, not less. Nobody has measured this in HFS, and it is a reason for caution about repeating the attempt rather than a reason to consider the question closed.

    What would overturn all of this. The report is unpublished and carries no dose, no technique, no patient count, no symptom duration and no measurement. A single properly documented case — confirmed intracavernosal delivery, adequate dose, an objective tone measure before and after — would be worth more than the entire body of anecdote, whichever way it came out. Until someone performs it, this conjecture is reasoning from an observation that could simply be wrong, and every conclusion drawn from it inherits that.

    Assessment

    • The only intervention that switches off adrenergic transmission inside the target tissue, so its reported failure cannot be attributed to poor delivery.
    • Converges with reported hypogastric block failure: two interruptions of one pathway, at opposite ends, both failing.
    • Leaves three live explanations — co-transmitter dominance, effector autonomy, denervation supersensitivity — and promotes Conjectures III and IV.
    • Rests entirely on unpublished report; one documented case with a measurement would settle it.
    • Priority: Not as a treatment. Highest as evidence.

    Conjecture VIII · Sympathetic blockade, done early and repeated Speculative

    Claim. Superior hypogastric plexus blockade has probably been tested in the wrong patients at the wrong time, and its reported failure in chronic disease says less than it appears to.

    Reasoning. The superior hypogastric plexus is the anatomical funnel for the mechanism the prevailing model proposes: sympathetic outflow to the pelvis converges there before reaching the erectile tissue. That makes it the natural test site. In chronic pelvic pain generally the technique produces pain relief on low-grade evidence and is recommended as an alternative rather than a primary therapy.27 If the two-compartment model is right, then a block performed years into the illness targets a compartment that is no longer in charge, and a single diagnostic block is in any case a poor test of whether repeated blockade could break a self-sustaining loop.

    Test. Duration-stratified, per Conjecture I, and preferably a series rather than one injection. The primary endpoint should be an objective tone measure and not pain, since pain and rigidity may respond differently and conflating them has already muddied the phentolamine evidence. A saline-controlled design is achievable here because the patient cannot easily distinguish the injections.

    The diagnostic use is separable from the therapeutic one. Even a block that provides no lasting benefit localizes the mechanism, and localization is currently worth more than relief. A block that transiently softens the penis proves the state is neurally maintained at that moment. A block that does nothing, with confirmed needle placement and confirmed sympathetic interruption, proves the opposite. Both results are publishable and neither has been published.

    Assessment

    • Targets the anatomical funnel of the prevailing model directly.
    • Existing negative impressions come from unpublished accounts in chronic patients.
    • Its diagnostic value is independent of its therapeutic value.
    • Priority: High for diagnosis, uncertain for treatment.

    Conjecture IX · Treat the nerve when a block says it is the nerve Speculative

    Claim. Surgical treatment of the pudendal nerve should be considered in HFS only under the selection criterion that made it work elsewhere — a positive response to diagnostic block — and that criterion has essentially never been applied in this population.

    Reasoning. The randomized evidence for pudendal decompression comes from patients selected by temporary response to blocks at the ischial spine and in Alcock’s canal, and in that group 71.4% were improved at twelve months against 13.3% of controls.28 The selection criterion is not incidental to the result; it is what made the trial work. Separately, neurolysis of the dorsal branch of the pudendal nerve in eight women with persistent genital arousal disorder produced complete resolution of arousal symptoms in seven, at follow-up beyond 26 weeks.29 That is the same nerve branch implicated in HFS and a condition placed in the same diagnostic family by the ISSWSH consensus.11

    The honest counterweight. Eight retrospective patients of one surgeon, all women, with a different presenting symptom, is not a basis for operating on anyone with HFS. Decompression relieves compression; the proposed HFS lesion is stretch, which is a different injury that surgical release does not obviously correct. And surgery on a nerve that is already injured can make matters permanently worse.

    And the reports from HFS itself are bad. Pudendal nerve decompression is described as overwhelmingly unsuccessful in this condition, on the unpublished evidence of section 2. Whether those operations were performed on block-positive patients is unrecorded, so this does not refute the selection argument. But it does mean the procedure has already been attempted here without the results seen in pudendal neuralgia, and anyone proposing it now is proposing to repeat something that has reportedly not worked. It also sits consistently with the two other failures: if the maintaining process is no longer in the peripheral nerve, releasing that nerve should not help, and reportedly it does not.

    Assessment

    • The strongest surgical precedent adjacent to HFS, in the same nerve branch and the same diagnostic family.
    • Reportedly attempted in HFS already and overwhelmingly unsuccessful, though not in patients selected by block response.
    • Irreversible, and the proposed lesion is stretch rather than compression.
    • Priority: Low, and lowered further by the reports. Not a treatment offering.

    Conjecture X · Non-invasive neuromodulation of the afferent limb Speculative

    Claim. If an abnormal reflex is maintained by afferent traffic in the dorsal nerve of the penis, stimulating that nerve at gating parameters should suppress the state while stimulation continues.

    Reasoning. The reflex model requires an afferent limb, and the dorsal nerve is it. Modulating a reflex from its input side is a standard neurological approach, and unlike surgery it is reversible, cheap and non-destructive. A pilot study of dorsal genital nerve neuromodulation in persistent genital arousal disorder enrolled 12 women and analyzed 9, using surface electrodes at 20 Hz and 200 μs for 30 minutes daily over three weeks. Symptom severity fell significantly in weeks 2 and 3 against baseline; three participants experienced relief lasting up to 60 minutes, two reported no change, one reported worsening, six were willing to continue, and no adverse events occurred.30

    Honest reading of that result. Nine women, no control group, an unblinded self-reported outcome, and effects the authors themselves describe as modest. One participant got worse. This is a signal, not a treatment, and it is in a different condition and a different sex. What makes it worth adapting is the risk profile: surface electrodes, no incision, nothing irreversible, and an effect that appears and disappears with the stimulation, which is exactly the pattern that would let a patient serve as his own control.

    Assessment

    • Directly modulates the afferent limb the reflex model requires.
    • Non-invasive and reversible, with a pilot signal in a related condition.
    • Evidence is nine unblinded patients with modest effects, one of whom worsened.
    • Priority: Moderate, and the best fit for the n-of-1 design in Conjecture XII.

    Conjecture XI · Prevent secondary tissue change while the primary problem is unsolved Speculative

    Claim. Chronically contracted cavernosal tissue is chronically hypoxic tissue, and the field should be trying to prevent structural change in parallel with trying to cure the condition, rather than sequentially.

    Reasoning. In an animal model, cavernous neurotomy produced hypoxia and subsequent fibrosis in the corpus cavernosum.34 Erectile tissue depends on intermittent oxygenation during nocturnal erections to maintain its smooth muscle content, and a penis that cannot fully relax may not be getting it. Against this, the 88-man cohort found no elevated echogenicity on B-mode scanning,9 which is reassuring but not decisive: B-mode echogenicity is a coarse measure of fibrosis and mean symptom duration in that cohort was 14 months.

    Implication. Daily low-dose PDE5 inhibition is the only widely available intervention with a plausible tissue-preservation rationale, independent of whether it helps the hard flaccid state at all. This reframes the drug that patients already rate highest4 as something other than a disappointing symptomatic treatment. Whether it actually preserves tissue in this condition is unknown and would require imaging endpoints over years.

    The claim that must not be overstated. There is no evidence that HFS causes fibrosis. The available structural data suggest it does not, at least early. This conjecture is a hedge against a risk that has not been demonstrated, and it is offered as such.

    Assessment

    • Addresses a complication rather than the condition.
    • The one available intervention is already in wide use for the wrong stated reason.
    • The underlying risk is plausible but unproven, and existing imaging argues against it.
    • Priority: Moderate, and low cost to act on.

    Conjecture XII · Run n-of-1 randomized crossover trials Speculative

    Claim. The parallel-group randomized controlled trial is the wrong instrument for hard flaccid syndrome, and waiting for one is why nothing has been established in seven years. The n-of-1 randomized crossover design fits this condition almost exactly.

    Reasoning. In an n-of-1 trial, a single patient is randomly allocated to alternating periods of treatment and control, with the sequence blinded and the outcome measured repeatedly. The design is appropriate when the condition is chronic and stable, when treatment effects appear and disappear quickly, and when between-patient variability makes group trials inefficient.35,36 HFS satisfies all three. It is also rare enough, and scattered enough, that assembling an adequately powered parallel-group trial in any single center is unrealistic — while a network of individually randomized patients can be aggregated afterward.

    What it requires. An objective, repeatable outcome measure, which is section 9’s problem, and a drug with a rapid onset and offset, which is what makes Conjectures IV, V and X better candidates for this design than surgery or botulinum toxin. A patient serving as his own control also eliminates the largest confounder in this field: the natural fluctuation of symptoms with time, activity and posture, which is what makes uncontrolled case reports so unreliable.

    The obstacle is not scientific. It is that no one is funding trials in this condition at all, in any design, which is the deeper problem all twelve of these conjectures run into.

    Assessment

    • Fits the condition’s rarity, chronicity, fluctuation and rapid drug response.
    • Removes natural-history confounding by using each patient as his own control.
    • Blocked by the absence of an objective outcome measure and by the absence of funding.
    • Priority: Highest among design conjectures.

    Key points

    • Three conjectures isolate consecutive steps in one pathway: α1A blockade tests the receptor, botulinum toxin tests transmitter release onto that receptor, Rho-kinase inhibition tests the contractile apparatus downstream of both.
    • The middle step has reportedly already been tested and failed, which is why Rho-kinase inhibition is now the leading candidate and the co-transmitter hypothesis the leading explanation.
    • Interventions aimed upstream of the smooth muscle — plexus blockade, nerve decompression — have all reportedly failed, consistently with each other.
    • Two conjectures cost almost nothing and could begin immediately: stratify by duration, and use n-of-1 designs.
    • Every one of them depends on an objective measure of the hard flaccid state that does not yet exist.

    11. A rational order of investigation

    This section is written for researchers and clinicians, and it is deliberately not a treatment algorithm for patients. Its purpose is to say what order the outstanding questions should be answered in, given that resources for this condition are close to zero.

    Stages 1 to 4 could be completed by a single motivated center within a year and require no new drug.

    The ordering principle is that cheap discriminating experiments come before expensive ones, and that measurement precedes intervention. Stage 4 is placed high despite being a single patient because so much of section 10 now rests on an unverified report of botulinum toxin failure; one properly documented case would either confirm the reorganization or undo it, and few interventions offer that much information for that little cost. It is worth noticing how much of this list is not blocked by science. Stages 1 through 5 use existing equipment, existing approved drugs and patients who are already presenting to clinics. What is missing is that someone decides to do it.

    Key points

    • Measurement comes before intervention; cheap discriminating tests come before expensive ones.
    • Verifying the reported botulinum toxin failure is a single-patient study and would confirm or overturn most of section 10.
    • The first five stages require no new drug and no new technology.
    • The binding constraint on this field is attention and funding, not scientific difficulty.

    12. What not to do

    Four cautions follow from everything above, and they are the only part of this article that comes close to practical guidance. They are stated as things to avoid rather than things to do, because avoiding harm is the one area where the evidence is adequate.

    Do not re-injure. The one point on which every account of this condition agrees is that it begins with mechanical trauma to a stretched penis.1,2,5 Continuing the practice that caused it, or resuming it during a period of improvement, is the one clearly modifiable risk factor in the condition.

    Do not buy irreversible or unregulated interventions. Regenerative therapies are classified as investigational or experimental for erectile dysfunction by the AUA,33 and HFS tissue has been shown to be structurally normal,9 so the rationale is weaker here than in the indication where they are already unproven. Surgery on a nerve that is already injured is not reversible if it goes wrong.

    Do not treat treatment failure as personal failure. Most of what is offered for HFS is aimed at striated pelvic floor muscle, and the defining sign is smooth muscle contraction. When such a treatment does not work, the most likely explanation is the target, not the effort.

    Do not mistake the absence of a cure for the absence of anything worth doing. Pain can be treated on its own terms. Erectile dysfunction can be treated on its own terms. Comorbid anxiety and depression are common in this population1,2 and are treatable independent of anything happening in the penis. Treating what is treatable is not the same as giving up on the rest.

    Key points

    • Avoiding re-injury is the only clearly modifiable risk factor.
    • Irreversible and unregulated interventions have a weaker rationale here than in the indications where they are already unproven.
    • Failure of a treatment aimed at the wrong tissue is information about the treatment.
    • Pain, erectile dysfunction and psychiatric comorbidity are each treatable on their own terms.
