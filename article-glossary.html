    1. Cells, tissues and signals

    Everything downstream depends on these. The single most consequential distinction in the whole vocabulary — smooth muscle versus skeletal muscle — is in this section.

    Neuron
    A nerve cell. It receives signals, integrates them, and passes an electrical impulse along to the next cell. The nervous system is built from billions of them arranged into circuits.
    Every proposed mechanism is ultimately a claim about which neurons are firing more than they should.
    Axon (nerve fiber)
    The long projection of a neuron that carries the impulse away from the cell body toward its target. A named “nerve” is a bundle of many axons wrapped in connective tissue, often carrying traffic in both directions.
    A stretch injury to the penis is, at cellular level, an injury to axons — which is why the resulting problem is not confined to the spot that was stretched.
    Myelin
    A fatty insulating sheath around many axons that lets impulses travel much faster. Damage to myelin alone slows or blocks conduction without destroying the axon.
    Whether an injury damaged myelin only or the axon itself determines how completely recovery is possible. See neurapraxia and axonotmesis in section 8.
    Synapse
    The junction where one neuron communicates with another cell. The signal crosses as chemical messengers released from the terminal and detected on the other side.
    Sensitization — the process proposed to make HFS persist — is a change in how efficiently particular synapses transmit.
    Neurotransmitter
    The chemical messenger released at a synapse or at a nerve terminal onto a target tissue. Different nerves use different transmitters, and one nerve may release several at once.
    Which transmitter is carrying the contractile signal determines which drugs could block it. See section 10.
    Co-transmission
    The release of more than one signaling molecule from the same nerve terminal. Sympathetic vasoconstrictor nerves release noradrenaline together with neuropeptide Y and ATP, from different vesicle populations, in proportions that shift with how rapidly the neuron is firing.24
    A drug that blocks one transmitter leaves the others working. This is the most economical explanation for a treatment that silences adrenergic signaling and changes nothing. Hypothesis
    Receptor
    A protein on a cell that a transmitter or drug binds to, producing an effect. Receptors come in subtypes, and a drug that blocks one subtype may leave others untouched.
    The variable response to α-blockers in HFS is most simply read as evidence that more than one receptor type is involved.
    Agonist and antagonist
    An agonist binds a receptor and activates it. An antagonist binds and blocks it without activating. Phentolamine is an α-adrenergic antagonist; noradrenaline is an α-adrenergic agonist.
    Nearly every drug discussed on this site is one or the other, and knowing which clarifies what it can and cannot do.
    Smooth muscle
    Involuntary muscle found in blood vessels, gut, bladder and erectile tissue. It has no striped appearance because its filaments are not arranged into sarcomeres, it is controlled by the autonomic nervous system and by hormones rather than by conscious command, and its energy consumption is low enough to sustain forceful contraction for long periods without fatiguing.14
    This is the tissue that produces the hard flaccid state. Its capacity to hold contraction indefinitely without tiring is why the sign can persist for years.
    Skeletal (striated) muscle
    Voluntary muscle attached to the skeleton, activated directly by somatic motor nerves at neuromuscular junctions. It is striped under a microscope and it fatigues.14
    The pelvic floor is made of this.15 It is a different tissue from erectile smooth muscle, with different nerves and different control, and it cannot set the resting tone of the smooth muscle inside the penis.
    Gap junction and connexin43
    Channels directly connecting adjacent cells so that ions and small molecules pass between them. Corporal smooth muscle cells are coupled by gap junctions built from the protein connexin43, which coordinates contraction and relaxation across a sparsely innervated tissue.16
    This coupling means a signal reaching a few cells can recruit a whole region — the basis for the conjecture that hourglassing reflects a segmental band of raised tone.
    Endothelium
    The single-cell lining of blood vessels and of the sinusoidal spaces in erectile tissue. It produces nitric oxide, the signal that relaxes smooth muscle.11,12
    If endothelial function is impaired, the tissue loses relaxation capacity as well as gaining contraction — which would make the problem harder to reverse pharmacologically.
    Connective tissue and collagen
    The structural material binding and supporting everything else — tendon, ligament, the dermis of skin, vessel walls, and the fibrous sheaths wrapping organs. Collagen is its principal protein and supplies tensile strength.
    The tunica albuginea is connective tissue, and its inelasticity is what allows pressure to build during erection.11 How readily connective tissue stretches before it is damaged is therefore relevant to a condition thought to begin with a stretch injury.
    Hypoxia
    A state in which tissue receives less oxygen than it needs. Erectile tissue depends on intermittent oxygenation, and in an animal model cavernous neurotomy produced hypoxia followed by fibrosis in the corpus cavernosum.19
    If cavernosal smooth muscle is held contracted, perfusion falls, and the concern is that prolonged hypoxia leads to fibrosis. Against this, the largest assessed cohort found no fibrosis on B-mode imaging. Hypothesis
    Fibrosis
    Replacement of functional tissue with collagen scar. It reduces elasticity and is generally not reversible.
    Cavernosal hypoxia raises transforming growth factor β1 and drives collagen deposition in animal models,19 which is the proposed route by which long-standing HFS could become structural rather than functional.
    Joint hypermobility
    Range of joint movement beyond what is normal for a person’s age, sex and population. It is common, frequently benign, and assessed with standardized scoring systems. It can occur on its own, as a hypermobility spectrum disorder, or as one feature of a heritable connective tissue disorder.
    Unstudied. It is proposed on this site as a candidate susceptibility factor: tissue that stretches further before resisting might exceed its own tolerance at lower applied force, which would help explain why the same activity injures one man and not another. No study has measured hypermobility in an HFS cohort. Hypothesis
    Ehlers-Danlos syndromes (EDS)
    A clinically and genetically heterogeneous group of heritable connective tissue disorders characterized by joint hypermobility, skin hyperextensibility and tissue fragility. The 2017 international classification recognizes 13 subtypes; the hypermobile type is the one most often encountered.33
    Two features make it worth asking about. Tissue fragility would mean injury at lower force. And hypermobile EDS carries a strong association with autonomic dysfunction — among 102 adults with hypermobile EDS or hypermobility spectrum disorder undergoing head-up tilt testing, 48% met criteria for postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome, 25.5% had orthostatic intolerance and 3.9% had hypotension.34 Tissue that injures more readily plus an autonomic system that regulates less well would map onto both halves of the proposed mechanism. It has never been studied in HFS, and hypermobility is common enough in the general population that prevalence alone would prove nothing without matched controls. Hypothesis

    Key points

    • Erectile tissue is smooth muscle: involuntary, autonomically controlled, able to hold contraction without fatigue.
    • The pelvic floor is skeletal muscle: a different tissue with different innervation.
    • Gap junctions let corporal smooth muscle behave as coordinated regions rather than isolated cells.

    2. How the nervous system is organized

    Central nervous system (CNS)
    The brain and spinal cord. Signals are integrated here before outgoing commands are issued.
    If the maintaining problem in HFS is central sensitization, the lesion that matters is in the spinal cord rather than in the penis — which would explain why treatments applied to the penis fail.
    Peripheral nervous system (PNS)
    Everything outside the brain and spinal cord: the nerves running to and from organs, muscles and skin, together with their ganglia.
    The initiating injury in HFS is a peripheral one. Whether the problem stays peripheral is the central open question of the condition.
    Somatic nervous system
    The division carrying conscious sensation from skin and muscle, and voluntary motor commands to skeletal muscle.
    The pudendal nerve is somatic. It carries the sensation of the penis, and it supplies the pelvic floor — but it does not control erectile smooth muscle.
    Autonomic nervous system
    The division controlling smooth muscle, cardiac muscle and glands automatically, without conscious involvement. It has two branches, sympathetic and parasympathetic, which usually oppose each other.
    Erectile tissue is autonomic territory. You cannot contract or relax it deliberately, which is why “relaxing harder” does not resolve the hard flaccid state.
    Sympathetic nervous system
    The autonomic branch associated with alertness, exertion and threat. Its outflow leaves the spinal cord from the thoracic and upper lumbar segments. In the penis it maintains contraction of erectile smooth muscle, which is what keeps the organ flaccid at rest.11,12
    The core hypothesis of HFS is that this system’s output to the penis is pathologically elevated and does not switch off.
    Parasympathetic nervous system
    The autonomic branch associated with rest and restorative function. Its pelvic outflow leaves the sacral spinal cord. In the penis it produces the smooth muscle relaxation that permits erection.11,12
    Erection requires this system to overcome sympathetic tone. If sympathetic tone is abnormally high, erection is harder to achieve and less complete.
    Sympathetic tone
    The continuous background level of sympathetic activity, as distinct from bursts. Tone can be raised or lowered without any conscious event occurring.
    The hard flaccid state is best described not as a spasm but as an abnormally high resting tone that never returns to baseline.
    Preganglionic and postganglionic neurons
    Autonomic commands travel in two stages. A preganglionic neuron runs from the spinal cord to a relay station called a ganglion; a postganglionic neuron runs from there to the target organ.
    This two-stage arrangement matters for nerve blocks: where a block is placed determines whether it interrupts the first stage, the second, or neither.
    Ganglion
    A cluster of nerve cell bodies outside the central nervous system, functioning as a relay or processing station.
    Several conjectures place the critical abnormality inside a ganglion rather than in a nerve trunk, which would explain why operating on the nerve trunk does not help.
    Dorsal root ganglion (DRG)
    The ganglion housing the cell bodies of sensory neurons for a given spinal segment, sitting just outside the spinal cord.
    After peripheral nerve injury, sympathetic axons have been shown to sprout into the DRG and form basket-like structures around sensory neurons, so that sympathetic activity can then directly excite them.17 If that occurs here, it would tie the sensory and sympathetic abnormalities together in one place.
    Dorsal horn
    The region at the back of the spinal cord’s grey matter where incoming sensory fibers first make contact with central neurons. It is a processing station, not a relay — signals are amplified, suppressed or redirected here.
    This is where central sensitization is understood to occur.20
    Spinal segment and spinal level
    The spinal cord is organized in segments, each serving a body region and named by vertebral level: thoracic (T), lumbar (L), sacral (S).
    Which segment a nerve block targets is not a technicality. The reported difference in outcome between blocks at L1 and at L2–L4 is one of the few clues available about where the pathological traffic runs.
    Cauda equina
    The bundle of lumbar, sacral and coccygeal nerve roots descending below the end of the spinal cord, named for its resemblance to a horse’s tail. Compression here affects several roots at once.
    One of the five anatomical regions in the diagnostic algorithm applied to HFS, and the level at which annular tears and Tarlov cysts are proposed to act.1 Hypothesis
    Thoracolumbar outflow
    The origin of sympathetic fibers, from roughly the lower thoracic through upper lumbar segments. For the bladder and pelvic organs this is usually given as T11–L2.21
    This is the source of the sympathetic traffic that the leading hypothesis holds responsible.
    Sacral outflow
    The origin of pelvic parasympathetic fibers and of the somatic pudendal nerve, from segments S2 to S4.13,21
    Erection and penile sensation both depend on this level, which is why sacral pathology can produce genital symptoms without any injury to the penis itself.
    Sympathetic trunk (paravertebral chain)
    A chain of interconnected ganglia running the length of the spine on each side. Fibers can travel up or down within it before leaving to reach their target.
    Because fibers travel within the chain, a block at one lumbar level can affect traffic destined for structures supplied from a different level — relevant to interpreting why blocks at some levels reportedly work and others do not.

    Key points

    • Somatic nerves carry sensation and control skeletal muscle; autonomic nerves control smooth muscle automatically.
    • Sympathetic outflow keeps the penis flaccid; parasympathetic outflow permits erection.
    • Sympathetic fibers leave the cord at thoracolumbar levels; pelvic parasympathetic and pudendal fibers leave at S2–S4.
    • Autonomic signals relay through ganglia, which matters for where a block can interrupt them.

    3. The nerves and plexuses that matter

    A plexus is a network where nerves from several sources mix and redistribute. Three of them and about six named nerves account for nearly all the anatomy discussed on this site.

    Pudendal nerve
    The main somatic nerve of the perineum, arising from the ventral rami of S2–S4. It carries sensation from the external genitalia and perineum and supplies motor fibers to the external anal and urethral sphincters and the perineal muscles. Its branches are the inferior rectal nerve, the perineal nerve and the dorsal nerve of the penis.13
    It is the sensory route from the injured organ, and the standard model proposes that abnormal traffic in its territory triggers the reflex. Notably, pudendal blocks and pudendal decompression surgery both reportedly fail to resolve the hard flaccid state.
    Dorsal nerve of the penis
    The terminal branch of the pudendal nerve, running along the top of the penis to supply sensation to the shaft and glans.13,22 Sources differ on whether it carries any motor fibers.
    This is the structure most exposed to a stretching or bending load, and the most commonly proposed site of the initiating injury. No imaging or histological study has confirmed injury to it in an HFS patient.
    Alcock’s canal (pudendal canal)
    A fascial tunnel on the inner wall of the ischium carrying the pudendal nerve and internal pudendal vessels. It is one of two classical sites of pudendal nerve entrapment; the other is between the sacrospinous and sacrotuberous ligaments at the ischial spine.13
    These two sites are where diagnostic pudendal blocks are placed. Response to blocks at both was the selection criterion in the only randomized trial of pudendal decompression,44 and that criterion is almost never applied before decompression is considered in HFS.
    Cavernous nerves
    The autonomic nerves running from the pelvic plexus into the erectile bodies, carrying the parasympathetic signals that produce erection and sympathetic signals that maintain flaccidity.11,12
    These are the final delivery route to the erectile smooth muscle. Any theory of excess sympathetic tone is a claim about traffic in these nerves.
    Pelvic splanchnic nerves (pelvic nerves, nervi erigentes)
    The parasympathetic nerves arising from S2–S4 that supply the pelvic organs, including the erectile tissue and the bladder detrusor muscle.11,21
    These carry the erection signal. They are also occasionally proposed as an alternative site of the initiating injury.
    Hypogastric nerve
    The paired nerve carrying sympathetic fibers down from the superior hypogastric plexus into the pelvis. In the bladder it mediates storage; in the penis it contributes to maintaining flaccidity.21
    This is the nerve named in the prevailing hypothesis: excess sympathetic tone is proposed to travel it to the erectile smooth muscle.1
    Superior hypogastric plexus
    A sympathetic nerve network lying in front of the lower aorta and sacral promontory, from which the hypogastric nerves descend. It is the principal sympathetic contributor to the pelvic plexus.18
    It is the anatomical funnel for the proposed mechanism, which makes a block placed here the direct test of that mechanism. Reports that such blocks usually fail are the single most awkward observation in the field.
    Inferior hypogastric plexus (pelvic plexus)
    The final autonomic relay to the pelvic organs, formed from the hypogastric nerves, the pelvic splanchnic nerves, and the sacral splanchnic nerves, together with afferent fibers from the viscera.18
    Everything sympathetic and parasympathetic destined for the penis passes through here. Its dual sympathetic supply is the basis for the conjecture that a superior hypogastric plexus block leaves an alternative route intact.
    Sacral splanchnic nerves
    Sympathetic branches from the sacral portion of the sympathetic trunk that run into the inferior hypogastric plexus, supplementing the input arriving from above.18
    If pathological traffic uses this route, blocks placed higher would be expected to fail while blocks on the lumbar chain might succeed — matching the reported pattern, though the anatomy describes this as the smaller of the two routes.
    Genitofemoral nerve
    A nerve arising from L1–L2 whose genital branch supplies the cremaster muscle and skin of the scrotum.
    Scrotal and testicular retraction depend on efferent supply at a different spinal level and by a different route than the erectile tissue. That predicts the two can be dissociated — and a reported case in which an L2 block reduced scrotal retraction without changing the hard flaccid state is exactly such a dissociation.

    Key points

    • The pudendal nerve is somatic and sensory; its dorsal branch supplies the penis.
    • The cavernous nerves are the autonomic delivery route to erectile tissue.
    • The inferior hypogastric plexus receives sympathetic input by two separate routes.
    • Scrotal retraction and the hard flaccid state have different efferent supplies and can dissociate.

    4. Reflexes

    Reflex arc
    A fixed circuit: sensory input travels to the spinal cord, is processed there without involving conscious thought, and produces an automatic output. The knee-jerk is the familiar example.
    The prevailing model of HFS is a reflex model. Understanding what a reflex is makes the claim assessable rather than mysterious.
    Somato-visceral (somato-autonomic) reflex
    A reflex in which somatic sensory input produces a change in autonomic output. Stimulation of somatic afferents has been shown to evoke reflex changes in sympathetic activity and thereby in organ function, demonstrated across the gastrointestinal tract, bladder, adrenal medulla, heart and blood vessels.4
    This is the established principle that makes the HFS hypothesis physiologically possible: it provides a route by which a sensory injury could alter smooth muscle tone.
    Pelvic/pudendal-hypogastric reflex
    The specific reflex proposed by Goldstein and Komisaruk: afferent input in pudendal or pelvic territory drives sustained sympathetic outflow through the hypogastric nerve to the erectile smooth muscle, which remains contracted.1
    This is the prevailing explanation of the condition. It is a hypothesis. The reflex has been named and described but not demonstrated in a patient. Hypothesis
    Dartos reflex
    Contraction of the dartos, a layer of smooth muscle in the scrotal wall, which wrinkles and tightens the scrotum. Being smooth muscle, it is under autonomic control.
    Sustained sympathetic overactivity would be expected to keep the dartos contracted, producing the scrotal tightening patients describe.
    Cremasteric reflex
    Elevation of the testis by contraction of the cremaster, a skeletal muscle supplied by the genital branch of the genitofemoral nerve.
    Testicular retraction may involve both this skeletal reflex and dartos smooth muscle tone. The two have different nerves, which is worth remembering when a treatment affects one and not the other.
    Guarding reflex
    Automatic contraction of the external urethral sphincter, via the pudendal nerve, to maintain continence as bladder pressure rises.21
    An example of reflex somatic guarding in the pelvis. Pelvic floor tightening in HFS is plausibly a similar reflex response to abnormal afferent input, rather than the cause of anything.

    Key points

    • A reflex converts sensory input into automatic output through the spinal cord.
    • Somato-visceral reflexes convert somatic sensory input into altered autonomic output — an established phenomenon.
    • The pelvic/pudendal-hypogastric reflex is a specific proposed instance of this, not a demonstrated one.

    5. The organs

    Perineum
    The region between the scrotum and the anus, containing the roots of the erectile bodies, the perineal muscles and the pudendal neurovascular bundle as it emerges from Alcock’s canal.13
    The commonest site of referred pain in this condition, and the site of blunt trauma in the minority of cases that begin that way.
    Corpora cavernosa
    The paired cylinders of erectile tissue running the length of the penis that produce rigidity. Inside them, blood-filled spaces called sinusoids are separated by trabeculae of smooth muscle.11,12
    This is where the hard flaccid state occurs. The smooth muscle in question is distributed throughout the tissue, not a discrete muscle that could be released.
    Tunica albuginea
    The tough fibrous sheath enclosing the corpora cavernosa. Its inelasticity is what allows pressure to build during erection.11
    A lesion of the tunica produces deformity that worsens with erection, as in Peyronie’s disease.9 Hourglassing that resolves at full erection therefore cannot be tunical — an argument by elimination that constrains what the deformity can be.
    Peyronie’s disease
    Fibrous plaque formation within the tunica albuginea, producing curvature, indentation, shortening or pain. It is a distinct condition with its own clinical guideline.9
    The most important structural differential. Peyronie’s produces a palpable plaque and deformity that appears on erection; HFS produces rigidity at rest with no plaque. The two can be told apart on examination, and confusing them delays both.
    Corpus spongiosum and glans
    The third erectile body, surrounding the urethra and expanding at the tip to form the glans. It has no enclosing tunica, so it fills but does not become rigid.11
    Because it lacks a tunica, the glans depends on flow rather than on pressure trapping. Under restricted inflow it would fail to engorge while the tunica-enclosed corpora still feel firm — which is the “soft glans with firm shaft” pattern described in the case literature.8
    Suspensory ligament and Buck’s fascia
    The suspensory ligament anchors the base of the penis to the pubic symphysis and sets the erect angle; dividing it is an established lengthening procedure that releases the concealed portion forward without altering erectile tissue.51 Buck’s fascia is the deep fascial layer enclosing the corpora, separating the superficial from the deep dorsal vein.
    Both are connective tissue with no erectile smooth muscle and no autonomic supply to the corpora, so neither can set resting tone. Buck’s fascia matters for a practical reason: it is the plane that decides which dorsal vein you can see.
    Scrotum and dartos
    The sac containing the testes, whose wall includes the dartos, a sheet of smooth muscle that adjusts scrotal position and surface area.
    Smooth muscle again, and therefore autonomic. Scrotal retraction fits a picture of generalized pelvic sympathetic overactivity.
    Bladder, bladder neck and internal urethral sphincter
    The bladder stores urine; the bladder neck and internal sphincter are smooth muscle at its outlet. During storage, sympathetic fibers traveling in the hypogastric nerve relax the detrusor through β3 receptors and contract the urethral smooth muscle through α1 receptors.21
    The same sympathetic outflow implicated in the penis also closes the bladder outlet. Excess of it predicts hesitancy, weak stream and incomplete emptying — and reduced urinary stream is reported by 72.7% of surveyed patients.6
    External urethral sphincter
    Skeletal muscle at the urethra under somatic control via the pudendal nerve, allowing voluntary continence.21
    Distinguishing the internal (smooth, sympathetic) from the external (skeletal, somatic) sphincter clarifies which urinary symptoms could plausibly follow from autonomic overactivity and which could not.
    Pelvic floor
    The musculofascial sheet at the base of the pelvis, principally the levator ani complex — pubococcygeus, puborectalis and iliococcygeus — plus coccygeus. These are skeletal muscles, roughly two-thirds slow-twitch fibers, innervated principally by the pudendal nerve.15
    Hypertonicity here is reported by 85.3% of surveyed patients6 and explains much of the pain and urinary picture, but as skeletal muscle it cannot set the tone of the smooth muscle inside the penis.

    Key points

    • The corpora cavernosa produce rigidity; the glans has no tunica and depends on flow.
    • The tunica’s inelasticity is why hourglassing that resolves with erection cannot be a tunical lesion.
    • The bladder outlet is closed by the same sympathetic outflow that maintains penile flaccidity.
    • The internal sphincter is smooth muscle; the external sphincter and pelvic floor are skeletal.

    6. Blood supply, drainage and congestion

    Penile state is set by two variables: how much blood gets in, and how much gets out. Smooth muscle tone governs both. This section covers the plumbing, and then the congestion concepts that patients frequently encounter on imaging reports — including one piece of terminology that the vascular societies have formally retired.

    Internal pudendal artery
    A branch of the internal iliac artery supplying the perineum and external genitalia. After giving off a perineal branch it continues as the common penile artery.30
    It runs alongside the pudendal nerve through the same anatomical corridor, which is why blunt perineal trauma can plausibly involve both the nerve and the vessel.
    Common penile artery and its branches
    The continuation of the internal pudendal artery, dividing into three: the cavernosal artery, the dorsal artery, and the bulbourethral artery. The cavernosal artery is responsible for tumescence of the corpus cavernosum; the dorsal artery is responsible for engorgement of the glans.30
    The division of labor matters. Shaft rigidity and glans engorgement have different arterial supplies, so they can fail independently.
    Cavernosal (deep) artery
    The artery running inside each corpus cavernosum, delivering the blood that produces erection.11,30
    The vessel measured on Doppler. Normal peak systolic velocity in 98% of reported HFS cases2 means this artery is doing its job. Reduced flow through contracted tissue is a consequence of smooth muscle state, not arterial disease — an important distinction, because it changes what would count as a treatment.
    Helicine arteries
    The small coiled terminal branches of the cavernosal artery that open directly into the sinusoids. They are coiled and constricted at rest and straighten and dilate when smooth muscle relaxes.11
    These are the taps. Sustained smooth muscle contraction keeps them closed, which reduces perfusion — the most direct explanation for a cold penis with intact arteries.
    Circumflex veins
    Veins running circumferentially around the corpora, receiving the emissary veins that pierce the tunica albuginea and draining into the deep dorsal vein.11,24
    Visible prominence of these vessels is among the most commonly reported observations in HFS. Nobody has measured their caliber in patients against controls, so the observation is common and uninterpreted.
    Dorsal artery of the penis
    Runs along the top of the penis beneath Buck’s fascia, supplying the glans and the skin.30
    Glans engorgement depends on this vessel rather than on cavernosal filling, which is part of why the glans can stay soft and cool while the shaft feels firm.8
    Subtunical venular plexus and emissary veins
    The small veins draining the sinusoids sit immediately beneath the tunica albuginea, forming the subtunical venular plexus. From there, emissary veins pierce the tunica to reach the larger drainage systems outside it.11,30
    This is the trapping apparatus. Expanding sinusoids squeeze these venules against the tunica, which is what holds blood in during erection. If the tissue becomes less compliant, the squeeze becomes less effective and erections become attainable but not maintainable.
    Superficial dorsal vein
    The vein lying above Buck’s fascia, draining the penile skin and prepuce into the external pudendal veins.11,24 It is the only dorsal vein visible from the outside; the deep dorsal vein lies beneath the fascia.
    When a patient says his dorsal vein looks enlarged, this is the vein in question. A Doppler report describing the deep dorsal vein is describing something else entirely.
    The three venous systems
    Drainage is described in three layers. The superficial system lies in the dartos fascia and consists of the superficial dorsal vein, draining to the external pudendal vein. The intermediate system lies beneath Buck’s fascia and comprises the circumflex and deep dorsal veins. The deep system consists of the cavernosal and crural veins. The intermediate and deep systems drain toward the prostatic venous plexus and the internal pudendal vein.30
    The superficial dorsal vein is the one visible through the skin. When patients describe prominent or engorged veins, this is usually the vessel they are looking at — and it is the layer furthest from the erectile mechanism, so its appearance says little about what the corpora are doing.
    Deep dorsal vein and the prostatic venous plexus
    The deep dorsal vein is the main drainage channel from the erectile bodies. It runs back beneath the pubic bone into the prostatic venous plexus, also called the plexus of Santorini, which connects onward to the internal iliac venous system.30
    This is the anatomical bridge between the penis and the pelvis. It is the route by which a venous problem sitting in the pelvis could, in principle, produce symptoms in the penis — and therefore the reason pelvic venous findings in HFS patients are worth taking seriously rather than dismissing.
    Internal iliac vein
    The main venous trunk draining the pelvic organs, receiving the internal pudendal vein among others. It is the vessel most often implicated in pelvic venous disorders.32
    If pelvic venous reflux contributes to any HFS symptoms, this is the most likely level at which it operates.
    Penile Mondor’s disease
    Superficial thrombophlebitis of a penile vein, most often the superficial dorsal vein, presenting as a palpable cord that may be tender and sometimes following prolonged or vigorous sexual activity. Usually self-limiting.55
    A specific differential worth knowing. A thrombosed vein feels like a firm cord; a merely prominent vein is soft and compressible. The distinction is made on examination, not by appearance.
    Pampiniform plexus and varicocele
    The pampiniform plexus is the network of veins draining the testis. A varicocele is abnormal dilation of that plexus, more common on the left, and it is the best-established venous disorder of the male genital tract.
    Worth distinguishing carefully from HFS. Scrotal symptoms in HFS are attributed to sustained dartos smooth muscle tone, not to venous dilation, and a varicocele is a separate finding with its own assessment.
    Raynaud’s phenomenon
    Episodic vasospasm of small arteries, usually in the digits, provoked by cold or stress and producing color change, numbness and pain.
    Relevant twice over. It is the closest common disorder to a sympathetically-mediated vasospasm of a peripheral vascular bed, which is why botulinum toxin evidence there has been read across to erectile tissue;56 and it appears as a pre-existing condition in at least one documented HFS case. Whether that is coincidence is unknown. Hypothesis
    Venous valve incompetence and reflux
    Veins contain valves that keep blood moving toward the heart. When valves fail, blood flows backward — reflux — and pools in the vessels below, which dilate. Incompetence of the internal iliac vein, the gonadal vein, or a combination is the proposed mechanism of pelvic venous disorders.32
    This is the mechanism behind the “pelvic congestion” findings patients report. Whether reflux found on imaging is producing symptoms is a separate question from whether it is present.
    Capacitance vessels and venous pooling
    Veins hold most of the body’s blood volume and can expand and contract, which is why they are called capacitance vessels. Standing shifts a substantial volume into the veins of the legs and pelvis by gravity.5
    Standing does two things at once: it pools blood downward, and it raises sympathetic outflow through the baroreflex.5 This site attributes the orthostatic worsening reported by 64.5% of patients6 to the sympathetic increase, since the symptom in question is the contracted state itself. Pooling is occurring simultaneously and may contribute to the vascular symptoms specifically.
    Cavernosal veno-occlusive dysfunction (venous leak)
    Failure of the trapping mechanism, so that blood drains out as fast as it enters and rigidity cannot be sustained. It is assessed on Doppler by persistently elevated end-diastolic velocity despite adequate arterial inflow.30
    A candidate explanation for erections that begin adequately and then fade, and for incomplete filling. It would be expected to appear later in the illness if fibrosis reduces the compliance that trapping depends on.19 Hypothesis
    Peak systolic velocity, end-diastolic velocity, resistive index
    The three standard Doppler measures. Peak systolic velocity reflects arterial inflow; end-diastolic velocity and the resistive index derived from both reflect how well the veno-occlusive mechanism is working.30
    Peak systolic velocity is reported normal in 98% of HFS cases.2 That is an arterial measure. It does not exclude a venous contribution, and the venous measures have not been systematically reported in this population.
    Pelvic venous disorders — and the terminology that was retired
    Symptoms attributed to abnormal pelvic venous flow. The historical names for these presentations have been formally set aside. An international working group convened by the American Vein & Lymphatic Society introduced the Symptoms–Varices–Pathophysiology (SVP) classification, with three domains: symptoms, varices, and pathophysiology, the last covering anatomic, hemodynamic and etiologic features. That group stated that the terms pelvic congestion syndrome, May-Thurner syndrome and nutcracker syndrome are inaccurate, fail to capture the relationship between pelvic symptoms and their underlying physiology, and should no longer be used.31
    Patients with HFS frequently report positive findings for “pelvic congestion syndrome.” Two things follow. First, the label itself is outdated, and a report using it is not describing a discrete disease so much as a pattern of venous findings. Second, and more important, the caution that applies to the diagnosis in general applies here with full force: dilated pelvic veins are common in people without symptoms, and their presence alongside symptoms does not establish that they are causally related.32 Without a matched control group, prevalence of these findings among HFS patients cannot support a conclusion.

    Key points

    • The cavernosal artery fills the shaft; the dorsal artery supplies the glans; they can fail independently.
    • Rigidity depends on emissary veins and the subtunical plexus being squeezed against the tunica, not on inflow alone.
    • The deep dorsal vein drains into the prostatic plexus, which is the anatomical link between penile and pelvic venous systems.
    • Normal peak systolic velocity is an arterial measure and does not exclude a venous contribution.
    • “Pelvic congestion syndrome” has been formally retired as a term, and dilated pelvic veins are common in people without symptoms.

    7. The processes

    Flaccidity
    The resting state of the penis — and not a passive one. It is actively maintained by continuous sympathetic outflow, with noradrenaline acting on α-adrenoceptors to keep cavernosal smooth muscle contracted, sinusoids collapsed and inflow low.11,12
    This is the most useful single fact in the vocabulary. If flaccidity is an active contraction, then an abnormally firm flaccid penis is most economically explained as too much of the normal process rather than as something exotic.
    Erection
    A relaxation event. Parasympathetic activity leads to nitric oxide release, smooth muscle relaxation, opening of the sinusoids, and a sharp rise in arterial inflow.11,12
    Erection and the hard flaccid state are opposite states of the same tissue. A drug that produces erection has not thereby normalized resting tone — the distinction at the heart of the phentolamine debate.
    Erectile dysfunction (ED)
    Persistent inability to achieve or maintain an erection sufficient for satisfactory sexual performance. It is a symptom with many causes — vascular, neurogenic, hormonal, pharmacological and psychological — and has its own clinical guideline.40
    Reported by roughly three-quarters of patients and by every published case series, but it is an accompaniment rather than the defining sign. Treating the ED does not treat the flaccid rigidity.
    Veno-occlusion
    The trapping mechanism that sustains rigidity: expanding sinusoids compress the subtunical venules against the tunica, so outflow falls while inflow stays high.11
    Rigidity depends on trapping, not inflow alone. Reduced tissue compliance from fibrosis would impair trapping and produce erections that are attainable but not maintainable.
    Detumescence
    Return to flaccidity: sympathetic tone resumes, smooth muscle contracts, trapped blood drains.
    In HFS the endpoint of detumescence is abnormal. The penis does not return to a soft state but to a firm one.
    Priapism
    A persistent erection unrelated to sexual stimulation. Ischemic priapism is a urological emergency: the trapped blood becomes hypoxic and acidotic, and delay causes permanent damage to cavernosal tissue.57
    Not a feature of HFS, and included because the distinction is a safety matter. The hard flaccid state is a firm flaccid penis, not a sustained erection. An erection lasting more than four hours requires emergency care regardless of any other diagnosis.
    Nocturnal penile tumescence
    Erections occurring during sleep, generated centrally and independently of conscious arousal. They are the mechanism by which cavernosal tissue is regularly reoxygenated.19
    Their loss is documented across the HFS case literature.7,8 It carries two implications: the mechanism operates independently of anxiety, and the tissue is losing its principal reoxygenation.
    Hourglassing
    A patient-described appearance in which the shaft narrows at a point along its length in the flaccid or semi-erect state, giving an hourglass profile. It is not a defined clinical sign and has not been quantified in any peer-reviewed source located for this article.
    Commonly reported and poorly understood. It is worth distinguishing from the indentation caused by a Peyronie’s plaque, which is palpable and appears on erection. Hypothesis
    Ejaculation: emission and expulsion
    Two coordinated phases. Emission is sympathetically driven, moving fluid into the posterior urethra via the vas deferens, seminal vesicles and prostate. Expulsion is a somatic spinal reflex producing rhythmic contraction of perineal muscles. Both are coordinated by lumbar spinal circuitry.10
    Ejaculation is a substantial sympathetic event. That is one reason sexual activity is reported to worsen symptoms in 75.9% of patients,6 and why post-ejaculatory pain is common.
    Micturition
    Urination. Storage is sympathetically maintained through the hypogastric nerve; voiding requires parasympathetic activation of the detrusor via the pelvic nerves and relaxation of the sphincters.21
    A system biased toward storage — which is what excess sympathetic tone produces — gives hesitancy, weak stream and post-void dribbling.
    Defecation and constipation
    Sympathetic activity inhibits gastrointestinal motility; parasympathetic activity promotes it.
    Constipation reported by HFS patients6 is consistent with the same autonomic bias operating on the gut. On this reading it is a parallel consequence rather than a cause of anything.
    Orthostatic sympathetic activation
    Standing up shifts blood into the legs, lowering cardiac filling and blood pressure. The arterial baroreflex responds by raising sympathetic activity, and muscle sympathetic nerve activity increases progressively as orthostatic stress increases.5
    This is the physiological explanation for why symptoms worsen on standing, reported by 64.5% of patients.6 Standing genuinely raises the signal that the hypothesis holds responsible.

    Key points

    • Flaccidity is actively maintained contraction; erection is relaxation.
    • Rigidity depends on venous trapping, not inflow alone.
    • Nocturnal erections reoxygenate the tissue and operate independently of conscious arousal.
    • Standing raises sympathetic outflow, which explains orthostatic worsening.

    8. Nerve injury and what follows

    Stretch injury
    Damage caused by elongating a nerve beyond its elastic limit. Stretch is a recognized cause of axonotmesis, typically preserving the nerve’s outer continuity while disrupting axons inside it.3
    This is the proposed initiating mechanism in most HFS cases, sustained during vigorous masturbation or penile elongation practice. The mechanism is plausible; the lesion has never been demonstrated in a patient.
    Penile elongation practice (jelqing, stretching, clamping, pumping)
    A group of manual and device-based techniques circulated in patient communities and intended to increase penile length or girth by repeated traction, compression or suction. They are distinct from supervised medical penile traction therapy, which is used for a different purpose in Peyronie’s disease.9
    Together with vigorous masturbation, these practices account for most reported HFS onsets.7,8 They are also the only clearly modifiable risk factor in the condition, which is reason enough to name them precisely rather than leave the reader to infer what is meant.
    Edging and gooning (prolonged, repetitive masturbation)
    Terms originating in patient communities rather than in medicine. Edging is the practice of repeatedly approaching orgasm and stopping short of it, sustaining arousal for long periods without ejaculating. Gooning refers to extended sessions of that kind pursued as an end in themselves, often over hours. Neither term appears in the medical literature, and no study has examined session length or frequency as a risk factor for any penile condition.
    What the literature actually records is that most cases begin with vigorous masturbation or penile elongation practice.7,8 Prolonged repetitive sessions differ from that description in exposure profile rather than in kind: the force at any instant is lower, but it is applied for hours, and cumulative loading of a nerve is not the same experiment as one forceful stretch. Two further features are worth naming, neither tested. Repeatedly approaching the emission threshold means repeatedly generating the sympathetic discharge that drives emission10 — the same hypogastric outflow the prevailing theory proposes becomes pathologically activated. And long sessions sustain handling, arousal and engorgement far beyond the duration of ordinary sexual activity. This is also the most direct route by which compulsive use becomes a mechanical exposure, which is the sense in which psychological factors matter here: as a path to the injury, not as its cause. Hypothesis
    Neurapraxia
    The mildest nerve injury: focal loss of myelin or local ischemia causing a conduction block, with all structural layers intact. Complete spontaneous recovery is expected within days to weeks.3
    If HFS were purely neurapraxic it should resolve within weeks. Persistence for years suggests either a more severe injury or a maintaining process that outlives the injury.
    Axonotmesis
    Disruption of axons with the surrounding connective tissue preserved. Commonly caused by crush or stretch. Recovery is possible but slow, with axons regrowing at roughly one millimeter per day.3
    The injury class most consistent with the proposed mechanism. It also predicts a long recovery timescale, which matches reported experience.
    Neurotmesis
    Complete transection of the nerve. Spontaneous recovery does not occur; surgical repair is required.3
    Not consistent with the HFS picture, where sensation is reduced rather than abolished and examination is typically normal.
    Denervation
    Loss of nerve supply to a tissue, whether partial or complete.
    Partial denervation would explain simultaneous numbness and abnormal sensation, and would set up the next entry.
    Denervation supersensitivity
    After a tissue loses its nerve supply, it becomes exaggeratedly responsive to the transmitter it has lost. Two mechanisms contribute: loss of the nerve terminal’s reuptake system, which raises transmitter concentration at the receptor, and slower changes at the target membrane such as increased receptor number.
    A supersensitive effector would respond excessively to circulating catecholamines even when nerve traffic is blocked — a candidate explanation for why sympathetic blocks might fail. Hypothesis
    Ectopic discharge
    Nerve impulses generated somewhere other than the sensory ending — along a damaged axon or in the cell body. After nerve injury, changes in voltage-gated sodium channel expression contribute to such discharge, in both injured neurons and their uninjured neighbors, and in the chronic phase this sustains ongoing abnormal firing.2
    Provides a source of continuing abnormal input that no longer depends on anything happening at the original injury site — which would explain why decompressing the nerve does not help.
    Wallerian degeneration
    The breakdown of the axon distal to a significant injury, clearing the path before regeneration can occur. It does not happen in neurapraxia.3
    Its presence or absence separates injuries that recover in weeks from those requiring months of regrowth.
    Radiculopathy
    Dysfunction of a spinal nerve root — the point where a nerve leaves the spinal cord — producing pain, altered sensation or weakness throughout that root’s territory. Sacral radiculopathy affects the roots serving the pelvis and genitals.
    The same nerve can be damaged at either end. Everything above in this section concerns injury out at the penis; radiculopathy is injury at the root, and it produces symptoms in the same territory without anything being wrong with the organ. It is one of the two less common initiating routes in the working model used across this site, the other being blunt perineal trauma.
    Annular tear (annular fissure)
    A disruption in the annulus fibrosus, the tough outer ring of an intervertebral disc that contains the softer nucleus pulposus. Tears are visible on MRI and are associated with discogenic pain — but they also occur in people with no symptoms at all.
    Goldstein and colleagues obtained lumbar MRI performed within the preceding year from 21 individuals with HFS recruited through an online support group, mean age 28 years (range 19–42). A spine surgeon reviewed sagittal and axial views. Sixteen of 21 (76%) had surgically treatable annular tears, predominantly at L5–S1 and L4–L5, against a prevalence of 19% to 29% reported in asymptomatic populations in prior literature.29 The authors read this as support for sacral radiculopathy triggering excess sympathetic activity. Hypothesis
    The limitations belong beside the figure. It is a congress abstract rather than a full peer-reviewed paper; n = 21; participants were recruited from a support group, which selects for engaged and treatment-seeking patients; there was no concurrent matched control group, so the comparison is to historical figures from separate literature; and imaging was read by a single reader whose reading was not reported as blinded. The finding is hypothesis-generating. It does not establish that annular tears cause HFS, and given how common they are in people without symptoms, base rate alone could account for a substantial share of it.
    Tarlov cyst
    A fluid-filled sac arising on a nerve root sheath, most often in the sacral region. Frequently found incidentally and often asymptomatic.
    Named alongside annular tears as a proposed cauda equina trigger in the five-region model.1 The same base rate caution applies: these are common incidental findings, so their presence in a symptomatic patient is not by itself an explanation.

    Key points

    • Stretch typically produces axonotmesis: axons disrupted, sheath intact, slow recovery.
    • Neurapraxia should resolve in weeks, so persistence implies something else.
    • Denervation can leave the target tissue supersensitive.
    • Injured nerves can generate impulses independently of any ongoing local problem.
    • The same nerve can be injured at the root instead of at the organ, producing genital symptoms with a normal penis.
    • Annular tears and Tarlov cysts are common in people without symptoms, so finding one does not establish that it explains anything.

    9. Sensitization and the language of abnormal sensation

    Central sensitization
    A prolonged increase in the excitability and synaptic efficacy of neurons in central pain pathways, triggered by nociceptor input. It manifests as pain hypersensitivity, including tactile allodynia, secondary hyperalgesia, aftersensations and enhanced temporal summation.20
    The leading candidate for what maintains HFS after the injury has healed. It is also directly testable in patients by quantitative sensory testing, and that study has not been done. Hypothesis
    Wind-up
    Progressive increase in the response of dorsal horn neurons to repeated identical stimuli. It depends on glutamate acting at NMDA receptors and is related to, but not the same as, central sensitization.20
    The NMDA dependence is why an NMDA antagonist is a mechanistically coherent thing to try, and why reported ketamine responses are interesting rather than random.
    Allodynia
    Pain from a stimulus that would not normally be painful, such as light touch or clothing contact.
    Its presence would be positive evidence of central sensitization and is straightforward to test at the bedside.
    Hyperalgesia
    Exaggerated pain from a stimulus that is normally painful.
    Secondary hyperalgesia — spread beyond the injured area — is one of the more specific signs of central rather than peripheral sensitization.20
    Dysesthesia
    An unpleasant abnormal sensation, whether spontaneous or provoked. It is distinct from pain, though often accompanies it.
    Expert consensus places HFS within the genito-pelvic dysesthesia spectrum,2 and HFS-associated pain has been proposed to be dysesthetic rather than a signal of ongoing tissue damage.1
    Paresthesia
    An abnormal sensation that is not unpleasant — tingling, pins and needles, buzzing.
    Commonly reported in and around the penis, and consistent with ectopic discharge in injured afferents.
    Hypoesthesia and anesthesia
    Reduced sensation, and absent sensation, respectively.
    Numbness or sensory loss is reported by 74.8% of surveyed patients.6 It sits oddly beside a classification framed around sensory hyperactivity, and partial deafferentation is the mechanism that would reconcile the two.
    Deafferentation
    Loss of sensory input to a region of the central nervous system. Deafferented central neurons frequently become hyperexcitable.
    Explains how a territory can be simultaneously less sensitive to touch and more prone to spontaneous unpleasant sensation — a combination patients report and which otherwise looks contradictory.
    Neuropathic pain
    Pain arising from damage or disease affecting the somatosensory nervous system, as opposed to pain from tissue injury.
    If HFS pain is neuropathic, it is not a damage signal, and treating it as evidence of ongoing injury is both inaccurate and unhelpful.
    Sympathetically maintained pain
    Pain sustained by sympathetic activity, characteristically relieved by sympathetic blockade. The concept is supported by findings that sympathetic axons sprout into dorsal root ganglia after nerve injury and can then drive sensory neurons directly.17
    Predicts a testable dissociation: a sympathetic block might relieve pain and dysesthesia while leaving the hard flaccid state unchanged. Whether that occurs has not been systematically recorded.
    Persistent genital arousal disorder / genito-pelvic dysesthesia (PGAD/GPD)
    A condition defined by unwanted, intrusive genital sensations occurring without sexual desire and not relieved by orgasm. The nomenclature was widened from “persistent genital arousal disorder” to include genito-pelvic dysesthesia by a fourteen-author consensus panel, on the grounds that patients report burning, itching, pain and pressure as well as arousal-like sensations.35
    A 2026 international consultation places HFS within PGAD/GPD in all genders.36 The classification is disputed by patients, who do not experience the hard flaccid state as arousal. What it gains is a diagnostic framework and a body of adjacent research; what it risks is redescribing a smooth muscle contraction as a sensation. Hypothesis
    The five-region algorithm
    A diagnostic scheme that localizes genital symptoms to one of five anatomical levels: end organ, pelvis and perineum, cauda equina, spinal cord, and brain.35 Each level implies different investigations and different treatments.
    The prevailing theory applies the same algorithm to HFS, proposing that the reflex can be triggered at any of the five regions.1 It is the most useful piece of structure the field has borrowed from elsewhere, and it is why “where is the lesion” is a more tractable question than “what is the cause.”
    Chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CPPS)
    Persistent pelvic pain of at least three to six months without demonstrable infection or other identified local pathology; in men often labeled chronic prostatitis / chronic pelvic pain syndrome. It is a description of a symptom pattern, not a mechanism.
    HFS shares the pelvic pain, the normal investigations and the frequent attribution to pelvic floor dysfunction. It differs in having a defining physical sign that CPPS does not.8 Being absorbed into the CPPS category is the most common way HFS receives a label that does not explain it.
    Complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS)
    A disorder in which pain following an injury is disproportionate to that injury and accompanied by regional autonomic and trophic changes — altered skin temperature and color, sweating, swelling, and later tissue change. Sympathetic involvement is recognized in a subgroup.
    The parallel is close enough to state: a confined region, an initiating injury, persistence out of proportion to it, and autonomic signs including coldness and color change. It is an analogy rather than a diagnosis. No study has applied CRPS criteria to an HFS cohort. Hypothesis
    Post-finasteride syndrome (PFS)
    Sexual, neurological and psychiatric symptoms reported to persist after stopping finasteride. Whether it constitutes a distinct clinical entity remains contested among specialists,52 and proposed mechanisms remain hypothetical.53
    Shares erectile dysfunction, reduced libido and genital sensory change with HFS. It does not share the defining sign: a firm semi-rigid flaccid penis is not a described feature of PFS, and no published case attributes HFS to finasteride.
    Post-SSRI sexual dysfunction (PSSD)
    Sexual dysfunction persisting after discontinuation of a selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor. In 2019 the European Medicines Agency concluded that such dysfunction can be long-lasting after withdrawal and required the product information to say so;54 biological plausibility and symptom profile have been reviewed,58 and incidence remains unquantified.
    Genital numbness is characteristic of PSSD and sensory loss is reported by 74.8% of surveyed HFS patients, so the two can present similarly. The flaccid rigidity again discriminates. Unlike PFS, persistence here has regulatory acknowledgment.
    Neuroinflammation
    Activation of immune cells within the nervous system. Peripheral nerve injury activates spinal microglia, which release mediators including brain-derived neurotrophic factor, producing lasting changes in dorsal horn neurons.12
    A candidate maintenance mechanism, addressing why sensitization that should be reversible instead persists.

    Key points

    • Central sensitization is increased gain in spinal pain circuits, and it is testable.
    • Wind-up depends on NMDA receptors, which is why ketamine is mechanistically relevant.
    • Reduced sensation and abnormal sensation can coexist through partial deafferentation.
    • Sympathetically maintained pain predicts that sympathetic blockade should relieve sensory symptoms specifically.

    10. Molecules, receptors and drug targets

    Noradrenaline (norepinephrine)
    The principal transmitter released by sympathetic nerve terminals onto smooth muscle.11,12
    The molecule at the end of the proposed pathway. Everything upstream is a claim about how much of it is being released, and everything downstream about how the tissue responds.
    α-adrenoceptor
    The receptor subtype through which noradrenaline contracts smooth muscle. α1 receptors predominate in penile and urethral smooth muscle.12,21
    The target of α-blockers. Their partial and variable effect in HFS suggests α-adrenergic transmission is part of the story but not all of it.
    Phentolamine
    A non-selective α-adrenergic antagonist. In isolated cavernosal tissue it suppresses contraction and enhances relaxation produced by other agents.23 It is also an established erectogenic drug in men without HFS.
    The observation that intracavernosal phentolamine relieves the hard flaccid state is the main evidence offered for the prevailing theory.1 Because the drug produces erection in anyone, the observation cannot distinguish “resting tone normalized” from “erection induced.”
    Finasteride and 5α-reductase inhibitors
    Drugs blocking the conversion of testosterone to dihydrotestosterone, used for androgenetic alopecia and benign prostatic hyperplasia.52
    Named as a pre-onset exposure by a small number of patients and the subject of a separate contested syndrome. No mechanism connecting 5α-reductase inhibition to sustained cavernosal contraction has been proposed. See post-finasteride syndrome in section 9.
    SSRIs and SNRIs
    Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors and serotonin–noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors, used mainly for depression and anxiety. Sexual dysfunction during treatment is well established, and persistence after stopping is recognized in European product information.54
    Frequently prescribed to men with HFS for comorbid depression and anxiety, which makes attributing any subsequent change difficult in both directions.
    Neuropeptide Y
    A peptide co-released with noradrenaline from sympathetic nerves. It is present in human penile tissue and modulates noradrenergic constriction of penile small arteries through Y1 and Y2 receptors.24 Constriction of this type characteristically resists α-blockade.
    Offers an explanation for incomplete α-blocker response: if part of the contractile signal is not adrenergic, blocking the adrenergic part cannot fully release it.
    Nitric oxide and cyclic GMP
    Nitric oxide is the signal that relaxes cavernosal smooth muscle. It raises cyclic guanosine monophosphate inside the cell, which drives relaxation and permits erection.11,12
    The relaxation arm of the system. If it is intact, a large enough push can still produce erection even when resting tone is abnormal — which is exactly what complicates the phentolamine evidence.
    Phosphodiesterase type 5 and its inhibitors
    The enzyme that breaks down cyclic GMP. Inhibiting it prolongs the relaxation signal, which is how sildenafil and tadalafil work.12
    These amplify a signal that must first be generated, and they act on tissue that must still be capable of relaxing. That gives two reasons they might help some patients and not others.
    Calcium, calmodulin and myosin light chain kinase
    The core contractile pathway in smooth muscle: calcium binds calmodulin, which activates myosin light chain kinase, which phosphorylates myosin and produces cross-bridge formation.14
    Most drugs that reduce contraction work by reducing calcium entry. That is not the only way a smooth muscle can be held contracted — see the next entry.
    Myosin light chain phosphatase (MLCP)
    The enzyme that strips the phosphate from myosin and so allows smooth muscle to relax. Resting tone is set by the balance between myosin light chain kinase, which switches contraction on, and this phosphatase, which switches it off.14 Rho-kinase inhibits it, which is what calcium sensitization means at molecular level.25
    This is the single point at which the smooth muscle’s default state is decided. An agent that raises its activity, or removes what is suppressing it, would relax the tissue regardless of what the nerves are doing — which is why it is the target of interest once nerve-directed treatments have failed. Hypothesis
    RhoA, Rho-kinase and calcium sensitization
    A parallel pathway that raises the tissue’s sensitivity to whatever calcium is present, by inhibiting the phosphatase that would otherwise switch contraction off. RhoA-mediated calcium sensitization contributes to maintaining the normal flaccid state and can be reversed by Rho-kinase inhibition.25,26
    A tissue with raised calcium sensitivity stays contracted without needing an ongoing nerve signal. If this pathway were upregulated in HFS it would explain why blocking the nerves does not release the smooth muscle, and why α-blockade produces erection without normalizing resting tone. Hypothesis
    Rho-kinase inhibitor
    A drug that blocks Rho-kinase, releasing myosin light chain phosphatase from inhibition and relaxing smooth muscle without requiring nitric oxide. Y-27632 is the standard research compound; in rats it raises intracavernosal pressure and produces erection through a nitric oxide-independent pathway,47 and applied topically to the tunica albuginea or the glans it produces an erectile response with or without stimulation of the autonomic nerves.48 Fasudil is a related compound in clinical use for cerebral vasospasm.49
    Acts downstream of every receptor and every transmitter, so it is the one available way to test whether the smooth muscle itself has been re-tuned rather than merely over-stimulated. Untested in humans for this purpose; systemic blood pressure is the dose-limiting problem. Hypothesis
    α-adrenoceptor antagonist (α-blocker) and subtype selectivity
    Drugs that block α-adrenoceptors and so oppose noradrenaline’s contraction of smooth muscle. They differ in which α1 subtype they prefer: doxazosin is essentially non-selective, tamsulosin intermediate, silodosin markedly α1A-selective.39 Human corpus cavernosum expresses α1a, α1b and α1d, with α1a predominating.38
    Selectivity has a practical consequence. A non-selective drug’s dose is capped by falls in blood pressure caused by α1B blockade elsewhere in the body, which limits how much of the penile target it can occupy. A negative trial of doxazosin would therefore not close a question that a negative trial of silodosin would.
    Alprostadil (prostaglandin E1)
    A synthetic prostaglandin that relaxes cavernosal smooth muscle through a cyclic AMP pathway, independently of nitric oxide. It is the active agent in intracavernosal injection therapy and in a topical cream, which produced dose-related improvement in erectile function in its phase II program.46
    Its mechanism needs neither intact nerves nor nitric oxide, which makes it one of the few agents that could in principle act in a condition where the neural signal is the problem. It has not been reported in HFS. The topical formulation also establishes that a vasoactive drug applied to penile skin reaches erectile tissue at an active concentration. Hypothesis
    Interstitial (pacemaker) cells
    Specialized cells that generate spontaneous rhythmic activity and set baseline tone in smooth muscle organs. Such cells have been described in the lower urinary tract and penis; their pacemaker activity is inhibited by nitric oxide and enhanced by noradrenaline.27
    Because noradrenaline enhances the pacemaker, prolonged sympathetic overactivity could leave the tissue generating its own rhythm — autonomy that would persist after the nerve signal stopped. Hypothesis
    NMDA receptor and glutamate
    Glutamate is the main excitatory transmitter in the central nervous system; the NMDA receptor is the subtype central to wind-up and central sensitization.20 Ketamine is a non-competitive NMDA antagonist.
    Directly connects the reported ketamine responses to a mechanism. It also means those responses, if real, are evidence for a central process.
    Ketamine
    A non-competitive NMDA receptor antagonist used as an anesthetic and, at lower doses, in refractory depression and some chronic pain states. It has pronounced psychoactive effects.
    Reported anecdotally to relieve the hard flaccid state, which is the observation pointing hardest toward a central mechanism. No controlled study exists, and a drug that alters perception is a difficult drug to evaluate by self-report of a symptom judged by touch and appearance. Hypothesis
    Bremelanotide
    A melanocortin receptor agonist administered by injection, approved for hypoactive sexual desire disorder in premenopausal women and acting centrally rather than on penile tissue directly.
    Listed among reported triggers in the first review of the condition, alongside marijuana use.8 Both appear as reported associations without any proposed mechanism, in a review rather than a controlled study.
    Voltage-gated sodium channels
    Channels that generate nerve impulses. Particular subtypes are upregulated after nerve injury and contribute to ectopic discharge.2
    The molecular basis for injured nerves firing on their own, and a rational target for reducing paresthesia and dysesthesia specifically.
    α2δ subunit and gabapentinoids
    Gabapentin and pregabalin bind the α2δ subunit of voltage-gated calcium channels, reducing excitatory transmitter release from sensitized afferents.
    Explains the reported pattern in which gabapentin helps pain but not the hard flaccid state: it dampens afferent transmission without touching sympathetic efferent output.
    Transforming growth factor β1
    A cytokine that drives collagen production. Cavernosal hypoxia increases its expression, with resulting fibrosis, in animal models.19
    The proposed molecular link between chronic contraction, loss of nocturnal erections, and long-term structural change.
    Botulinum toxin
    A protease that cleaves the synaptic protein SNAP-25, preventing vesicular transmitter release.28 Its best-known action is at cholinergic terminals, but SNAP-25 is required for noradrenaline release as well: in isolated blood vessels, botulinum toxin A substantially reduced noradrenaline-mediated contraction while leaving neuropeptide Y-mediated contraction entirely unaffected.37
    Intracavernosal botulinum toxin has reportedly been tried in HFS without success. Because the drug is injected into the target tissue and acts for months, that failure is hard to blame on poor delivery; and because it removes noradrenaline but not neuropeptide Y, it points toward co-transmission rather than toward sympathetic drive in general. Hypothesis

    Key points

    • Noradrenaline acting at α1 receptors contracts the tissue; nitric oxide and cyclic GMP relax it.
    • Contraction can also be sustained by raising calcium sensitivity rather than calcium itself.
    • Not all of the sympathetic contractile signal is adrenergic.
    • Each drug discussed on this site acts at one identifiable point in these pathways.

    11. Investigations and procedures

    Penile color Doppler ultrasound
    Ultrasound with blood-flow imaging, used to assess arterial inflow and venous function, usually after an intracavernosal injection to induce erection. Peak systolic velocity is the main arterial measure.
    Normal peak systolic velocity has been reported in 98% of HFS cases,2 which excludes gross arterial disease and is expected rather than disappointing.
    Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)
    Imaging using magnetic fields rather than radiation, giving detailed views of soft tissue including nerves, discs and spinal structures. Images are acquired in planes — sagittal views cut front to back through the midline, axial views cut across the body.
    MRI of the penis and pelvis is generally unremarkable in HFS; imaging is described as normal in the review literature.8 Its use in this condition has shifted toward the lumbosacral spine instead.
    Lumbar spine MRI
    Imaging of the lower spine, used to assess discs, nerve roots and the cauda equina. Reported HFS workups include it where back pain or radicular symptoms are present.2
    This is the investigation behind the most-discussed imaging finding in the condition — see annular tear and radiculopathy in section 8. The reasoning for ordering it is not obvious without the five-region model: if the reflex can be triggered at the cauda equina rather than at the penis, then a man with genital symptoms and no penile abnormality may have a spinal cause. Whether that is correct is unsettled.
    Nerve block
    Injection of local anesthetic around a nerve or plexus to interrupt conduction temporarily, used both diagnostically and therapeutically.
    Diagnostic blocks are among the few tools that can localize a mechanism in a living patient. Their value depends entirely on confirming that the block actually worked.
    Sympathetic block and block adequacy
    A block aimed at sympathetic structures. Adequacy is normally confirmed objectively — a rise in skin temperature in the corresponding territory, or loss of the sympathogalvanic response.
    This matters more here than almost anywhere. A sympathetic block reported as failing, without documented adequacy, tells you nothing about physiology. Much of what is believed about HFS blocks rests on undocumented procedures.
    Quantitative sensory testing
    Standardized measurement of sensory thresholds across modalities — touch, vibration, temperature, pain — and of phenomena such as temporal summation.
    The single most informative study not yet done in HFS. It would show whether the sensory deficit maps to a nerve distribution, and whether the signatures of central sensitization are present.
    Nocturnal penile tumescence monitoring
    Overnight recording of sleep-related erections, historically used to separate organic from psychogenic erectile dysfunction.
    Would establish objectively how completely nocturnal erections are lost, rather than relying on whether patients notice them.
    Elastography
    An ultrasound technique measuring tissue stiffness. Shear-wave elastography quantifies it directly, and has been applied to penile tissue: a pilot study used it to predict which men with erectile dysfunction would respond to intracavernosal botulinum toxin.41
    Two uses. It could test whether corporal fibrosis develops with disease duration, distinguishing a functional from a structural stage. More immediately, stiffness is close to a direct measurement of the hard flaccid state itself, which makes this the most promising candidate for the objective measure the field lacks. Hypothesis
    Pelvic floor physical therapy and myofascial release
    Manual and exercise-based treatment directed at striated pelvic floor muscle: internal release of tender points, stretching, relaxation training and graded exercise. In a randomized multicenter trial in women with interstitial cystitis / painful bladder syndrome and pelvic floor tenderness, myofascial physical therapy produced moderate or marked improvement in 59% against 26% for global therapeutic massage.45
    The most frequently offered treatment for HFS, aimed at a tissue type that is not the one producing the defining sign. Defensible for comorbid myofascial pain, guarding and behavior change; not a treatment for smooth muscle contraction.
    Low-intensity shockwave therapy (Li-SWT)
    Delivery of low-energy acoustic pulses to tissue, proposed to promote new blood vessel formation. The American Urological Association classifies it as investigational for erectile dysfunction, noting that randomized sham-controlled trials do not clearly show benefits outweighing risks and burdens.40
    It forms part of the one HFS case reporting resolution at two-year follow-up.2 Its rationale is improving perfusion in ischemic tissue, and penile hemodynamics in HFS have been measured and found normal.
    Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) and stem cell therapy
    Injection of concentrated autologous platelets, or of harvested or cultured stem cells, intended to promote tissue repair. The AUA classifies intracavernosal stem cell therapy as investigational and states that PRP should not be offered outside an approved research protocol.40
    Both are marketed to men with HFS, often at high cost. Their proposed mechanism is repair of damaged tissue, and the largest HFS cohort found no structural abnormality to repair.
    Nerve decompression and neurolysis
    Surgical release of a nerve from tissue thought to be compressing it. In pudendal neuralgia, a randomized trial in patients selected by temporary response to diagnostic blocks found 71.4% improved at twelve months against 13.3% of controls.44
    Reportedly attempted in HFS without comparable results, though not in patients selected by block response. Decompression addresses compression; the lesion proposed in HFS is stretch, which is a different injury and not obviously corrected by release.
    Neuromodulation
    Altering nerve activity with applied electrical stimulation, through surface electrodes or an implanted device, at a nerve, ganglion, sacral root or the spinal cord. In a pilot study of dorsal genital nerve stimulation in persistent genital arousal disorder, nine women showed modest reductions in symptom severity, three reporting relief lasting up to an hour.50
    Untested in HFS. Its appeal is that it is reversible and adjustable, so an effect that appears and disappears with the stimulation can be checked against the patient’s own baseline. Hypothesis
    Vacuum erection device
    A cylinder placed over the penis in which negative pressure draws blood in, usually with a constriction ring to retain it.
    It appears on both sides of this condition: listed among triggers for HFS onset,8 and used as a treatment by men who already have it. The pressures involved are why it belongs on the list of things capable of producing a stretch injury.
    Penile durometry and tonometry
    Direct instrumental measurement of penile rigidity or tissue firmness, rather than description of it. No device has been standardized for the flaccid state.
    This is the measurement the field does not have. Every treatment outcome ever reported in HFS is a patient’s own assessment of how firm his penis felt, which is why no trial in this condition can currently be blinded in any way that means anything.
    Intracavernosal injection
    Injection of a vasoactive drug directly into the corpora, used diagnostically and therapeutically. It carries recognized risks including priapism and must be performed under medical supervision.
    The route by which phentolamine was administered in the observation supporting the prevailing theory. It must never be self-administered.

    12. Evidence terminology

    This site qualifies claims constantly, and the qualifications only mean something if the vocabulary is clear. These terms are not medical; they are about how much weight a finding can carry.

    Case report and case series
    A description of one patient, or of a small number. They establish that something can happen and generate hypotheses. They cannot establish frequency or effectiveness.
    Most of the HFS literature is of this type. The 2025 systematic review found eight eligible studies in total, most of them case reports.2
    Cross-sectional survey
    A snapshot of a group at one point in time. It describes that group, not the wider population, particularly when participants recruit themselves.
    The most-quoted HFS figures come from a survey of 143 self-identified patients recruited through social media.6 These are descriptions of a self-selected group, not prevalence estimates.
    Systematic review
    A structured search and synthesis of all studies meeting stated criteria. It is only as strong as the studies it finds.
    The 2025 review could not perform quantitative meta-analysis because reporting was too heterogeneous, and noted a high risk of bias in many included reports.2
    Randomized controlled trial
    Participants are allocated by chance between treatment and comparison, which controls for natural history, expectation and selection. The standard for establishing that a treatment works.
    None has been published for any HFS intervention.2 Every treatment claim about this condition is therefore uncontrolled.
    Congress abstract
    A short summary presented at a conference, usually with limited peer review and without the detail needed to assess methods.
    The lumbosacral annular tear finding — 16 of 21 patients, 76% — is reported in this format.29 It is hypothesis-generating, not established.
    Diagnosis of exclusion
    A diagnosis reached by ruling out alternatives rather than by a positive test.
    HFS is diagnosed this way, because no objective test assists diagnosis and no validated criteria exist.2
    Selection bias
    Distortion arising because who ends up in a study is not representative of who has the condition.
    Recruiting through online support communities selects for persistent, distressing and treatment-refractory cases. Milder or resolved cases are largely invisible to the literature.
    Confounding
    When an apparent relationship between two things is produced by a third.
    The phentolamine observation is the standing example: the drug both relieves the state and induces erection, and the design cannot separate the two.
    Base rate
    How common a finding is in people without the condition. Without it, a finding’s frequency in patients means little.
    Pelvic venous congestion and lumbar imaging abnormalities both occur in asymptomatic people, so their frequency in HFS patients proves nothing without matched controls.
    Blinding, placebo and sham
    Blinding conceals who received the treatment — from participants, from assessors, or from both. A placebo is an inactive substance given for comparison; a sham is its procedural equivalent, the device switched off or the injection given without the drug. Without blinding, the effects of expectation cannot be separated from the effects of the treatment.
    No HFS treatment has ever been given blind. Combined with the absence of any objective measure, this means every reported success is a man who knew he was being treated reporting that he felt better.
    Publication bias
    The tendency for positive results to be written up and published while negative ones are not, which makes a body of literature look more encouraging than the reality underneath it.
    Two of ten individually reported HFS cases resolved completely,2 while the largest survey found no treatment producing more than slight improvement.6 The direction of that discrepancy is exactly what publication bias predicts.
    Regression to the mean and natural history
    Two ways a patient can improve without the treatment doing anything. Regression to the mean is the tendency for extreme measurements to be followed by less extreme ones. Natural history is simply the course a condition takes when left alone. People seek treatment when symptoms are at their worst, so both work in favor of whatever they take next.
    The natural history of HFS is unknown, and both reported complete resolutions occurred in patients with short symptom duration — the point at which spontaneous improvement is most plausible. Neither can be confidently attributed to its treatment.
    Number needed to treat (NNT)
    How many patients must receive a treatment for one additional patient to obtain the defined benefit. Lower is better. For neuropathic pain, the number needed to treat for 50% pain relief is 6.4 for serotonin–noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors, 7.7 for pregabalin and 7.2 for gabapentin.42
    It converts “this drug works” into something honest. A number needed to treat of seven means six of every seven patients get nothing, which is the realistic expectation from the drugs most often prescribed for pain in this condition.
    N-of-1 trial and crossover design
    In a crossover trial each participant receives both treatment and comparison in sequence, serving as their own control. An n-of-1 trial applies that to a single patient, with the order randomized and blinded and the outcome measured repeatedly. The design suits chronic, stable conditions in which drug effects appear and disappear quickly.43
    Probably the right instrument for HFS, which is too rare and too geographically scattered for conventional trials. It requires an objective, repeatable measure, which is precisely what does not yet exist.
    Patient Global Impression of Change (PGIC)
    A single-item scale on which a patient rates how much their condition has changed since starting treatment, typically from 1 to 7. Quick, widely used, and entirely subjective.
    This is the instrument behind the most-quoted treatment figures in HFS. PDE5 inhibitors scored highest at 2.6 ± 1.1 — below the midpoint of the scale.6 “Ranked first” and “effective” are not the same claim.
    Prospective and retrospective
    A prospective study decides what it will measure and then collects it. A retrospective study looks back through records gathered for other purposes. Retrospective work is cheaper and faster, and vulnerable to incomplete records and to selecting cases whose outcome is already known.
    Almost all HFS clinical data are retrospective or self-reported. No prospective cohort has ever been assembled and followed.
    Expert consensus and the Delphi method
    A formal process for producing agreement among specialists, typically through structured rounds of anonymous rating and feedback until positions converge. It produces a defensible statement of what experts currently think. It does not produce new evidence.
    The PGAD/GPD nomenclature within which HFS has been placed was produced this way.35 Consensus documents carry real weight in a field with little data, and it is worth remembering that the weight comes from the authors rather than from measurements.
    Mean, standard deviation, confidence interval and p value
    The mean is the average. The standard deviation describes how widely values are spread around it. A confidence interval gives the range within which the true value plausibly lies. A p value is the probability of observing a result at least this extreme if there were no real effect.
    The spread is often the more informative number here. When the largest HFS cohort reports a mean symptom duration of 14 ± 20 months, a standard deviation larger than the mean is telling you the group is not one population but several.
    Falsifiability
    Whether a claim specifies observations that would show it to be wrong. A model that accommodates every possible outcome forbids none, and predicts nothing.
    Worth applying to any explanation of HFS, including the ones on this site. If a theory cannot say what would refute it, it is not yet doing scientific work.

    Key points

    • Almost the entire HFS literature consists of case reports, case series and self-selected surveys.
    • No randomized controlled trial of any intervention has been published.
    • Findings without a base rate in healthy people cannot support conclusions.
    • A theory that cannot specify what would refute it is not yet testable.
