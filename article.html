What is hard flaccid syndrome?

A condition patients described before medicine did. What the literature establishes, what it proposes, and what is still unknown.

29 min read · 12 references

Contents

    Summary

    • Hard flaccid syndrome (HFS) is an acquired condition in which the penis remains persistently semi-rigid and retracted in the flaccid state, commonly with sensory change, erectile difficulty, pelvic or penile pain, and psychological distress.
    • There are no established diagnostic criteria and no confirmatory test. Diagnosis rests on symptom pattern and exclusion of other pathology.
    • The leading mechanistic proposal is pathological activation of a pelvic/pudendal-hypogastric reflex, in which afferent injury signals drive sustained sympathetic outflow to cavernosal smooth muscle. This is a hypothesis, not an established mechanism.
    • The entire published evidence base is small. A 2025 systematic review identified eight eligible studies; the largest single dataset is a self-selected online survey of 143 respondents.
    • No treatment has demonstrated cure in a controlled trial. Reported outcomes are drawn from case reports and self-report surveys.
    Established replicated or textbook physiology Hypothesis proposed, partially supported Speculative stated without direct evidence Uncertain too little evidence to judge

    Hard flaccid syndrome occupies an unusual position in sexual medicine. The symptom cluster was named and characterized in online patient communities years before it entered the medical literature, and the literature has not yet caught up. Punjani and Mulhall observed in a 2025 editorial that a PubMed search for the term returned 12 results against approximately 410,000 results on a general web search.10 That asymmetry defines the clinical problem. Patients arrive informed, frightened, and carrying material of uneven quality; clinicians frequently encounter the term for the first time in the consultation.

    This article is written for both readers. It states what is supported, labels what is proposed, and does not fill the gaps between them. Where the honest position is that the evidence does not answer a question, the article says so.

    1. Definition and nomenclature Established

    Hard flaccid syndrome describes an acquired, persistent and bothersome state in which the penis is observed in an unwanted semi-rigid condition while flaccid. The descriptive term originated among patients. Its first peer-reviewed appearance was a 2020 case series by Gul and colleagues reporting four men aged 22 to 34, all of whom developed symptoms after trauma during intercourse or vigorous masturbation.1 Those authors noted at the time that the syndrome had not been universally recognized by urologists.

    Six years later, that remains substantially true. The 2025 systematic review by Pang, Feng and Zhang concluded that no objective test assists diagnosis, that the etiology remains unclear, and that diagnosis relies on subjective symptom review together with exclusion of other pathology.2 There is no validated diagnostic instrument, no consensus definition endorsed by a professional body specific to HFS, and no established management guideline.

    One nomenclature development is worth noting. The Fifth International Consultation on Sexual Medicine recommendations on PGAD/GPD, published in 2026, position hard flaccid syndrome within the spectrum of genito-pelvic dysesthesia — a group of conditions characterized by unrelenting, unwanted genital sensation.6 Whether HFS is best classified as a distinct syndrome or as one presentation of a broader dysesthesia spectrum is unresolved, and the two framings carry different implications for investigation.

    Key points

    • HFS is a patient-derived descriptive term that entered the peer-reviewed literature in 2020.
    • No established diagnostic criteria, no confirmatory test, no management guideline.
    • A 2026 expert consensus places HFS within the PGAD/GPD spectrum; this classification is not settled.

    2. Epidemiology and clinical recognition Established

    True prevalence is unknown and probably underreported.10 No population-based study has been performed. The available cohorts are small, and most are recruited from online support communities (Reddit), which selects for patients with persistent symptoms and internet engagement. Reported ages are consistently young: 16 to 42 across the studies pooled in the systematic review,2 a mean of 27.4 years in the largest survey,3 and a mean of 28 years (range 19–42) in an imaging cohort.4

    Clinician familiarity has been measured once. Gryzinski and colleagues surveyed attendees at the 2023 American Urological Association sexual medicine plenary; 36 of 37 attendees completed the survey, of whom 35 were urologists.7 Of these, 64% (23/36) had encountered a patient with HFS. Only 52% considered HFS to represent a legitimate medical condition. Thirteen percent reported treatment success and 57% reported inconsistent results. The authors identified the small sample and survey methodology as the principal limitations. A further consideration is that respondents were self-selected attendees of a subspecialty sexual medicine session, so these figures are unlikely to represent general urological practice and probably overstate familiarity rather than understate it.

    Key points

    • Prevalence is unknown; no population-based data exist.
    • Affected men are consistently young, with reported means of 27 to 28 years.
    • In a subspecialty audience, 64% had seen a case and only 52% regarded HFS as a legitimate condition.
    • Nearly all cohorts are recruited online, which biases toward chronic, treatment-refractory presentations.

    3. Clinical presentation Established

    The symptom pattern is consistent across independent reports. Gul and colleagues described penile sensory change (numbness or coldness), a semi-rigid penis in the flaccid state, reduced frequency of morning and nocturnal erections, and difficulty achieving or maintaining erection, with additional complaints of pain during ejaculation or urination and psychological symptoms ranging from mild anxiety to severe depression.1 The largest dataset, a survey of 143 self-identified patients conducted in 2023, quantifies these frequencies.3

    Two features of the presentation carry mechanistic interest. The first is that symptoms are position-dependent and activity-dependent. In the same survey, lying down improved symptoms for 73.0% of respondents and stretching improved them for 44.1%, while masturbation worsened symptoms in 75.9% and standing worsened them in 64.5%.3 The second is the comorbidity profile: pudendal neuralgia was the most prevalent comorbid condition, reported by 16.9% of respondents.3 The comorbidity profile is consistent with a neurological contribution without discriminating between the mechanisms proposed below. The positional effect does more than that. Smooth muscle sympathetic nerve activity rises progressively with orthostatic stress through baroreflex resetting,12 so a symptom sustained by sympathetic outflow should worsen on standing and ease when supine, which is what these respondents describe.3 That is a prediction the sympathetic account makes and its competitors do not, though self-reported position effects are a weak way to test it.

    Symptom consistency across independent samples is meaningful. It is not, on its own, evidence of a shared mechanism.

    Key points

    • The core presentation is a semi-rigid flaccid penis with sensory change, erectile difficulty and pelvic or penile pain.
    • Psychological distress is reported by roughly nine in ten patients.
    • Symptoms characteristically improve when lying down and worsen with standing and masturbation.
    • All frequency data derive from self-selected, self-reported samples.

    4. The physiology the syndrome disturbs Established

    Understanding the proposed mechanism requires one piece of background, and it is the piece most often missed by patients reading about the condition: flaccidity is not a resting state. It is actively maintained.

    In the flaccid penis, cavernosal smooth muscle is held in tonic contraction by sympathetic outflow. Sympathetic fibers reach the penis via the hypogastric nerve and the pelvic plexus, releasing noradrenaline onto α-adrenoceptors on cavernosal smooth muscle. Contraction keeps arterial inflow low and the sinusoids collapsed. Erection is the reverse process: parasympathetic activity, conveyed through the cavernous nerves, drives nitric oxide release, smooth muscle relaxation, increased arterial inflow, and passive compression of subtunical venules against the tunica albuginea.11

    Somatic innervation runs separately. The dorsal nerve of the penis is the terminal branch of the pudendal nerve and carries sensory information from the penile shaft and glans; the pudendal nerve also supplies the bulbospongiosus and ischiocavernosus muscles.11

    The consequence is straightforward. Any process that increases sympathetic tone to cavernosal smooth muscle will produce a firmer, more retracted flaccid penis and will simultaneously impede the relaxation that erection requires. A single mechanism can therefore account for both the abnormal flaccid state and the erectile difficulty. This is why the sympathetic hypothesis is attractive, and it is the physiological anchor for everything in the next section.

    Key points

    • Flaccidity is actively maintained by tonic sympathetic contraction of cavernosal smooth muscle.
    • Sympathetic supply reaches the penis via the hypogastric nerve and pelvic plexus.
    • The dorsal nerve of the penis is a sensory branch of the pudendal nerve.
    • Excess sympathetic tone would produce both a rigid flaccid state and impaired erection — the two cardinal features of HFS.

    5. Proposed pathophysiology Hypothesis

    Everything in this section is proposal rather than demonstration. No study has directly measured sympathetic outflow to the penis in patients with HFS, and no histological or neurophysiological study has confirmed the site of injury. The reasoning is nonetheless coherent and is the framework within which most current specialist practice operates.

    5.1 The inciting injury

    Reported precipitants cluster around mechanical loading of the erect or semi-erect penis. The systematic review lists aggressive masturbation, intense sexual intercourse and penile trauma among identified causes.2 Penile stretching practices undertaken for enlargement or elongation appear in the case literature: the patient described by Yazar and colleagues had performed self-directed stretching exercises without benefit and with increased pain before presentation.8

    The proposed anatomical targets of such an injury are the dorsal nerve of the penis, the cavernous nerves, or the erectile smooth muscle and tunica themselves. This localization is inference from the mechanism of loading and the resulting symptom pattern. No imaging, electrodiagnostic or histological study has demonstrated injury at any of these sites in a patient with HFS. The specific claim that stretch injures the dorsal nerve should be read as a working hypothesis. Speculative

    An important qualification follows. In the survey of 143 patients, only 58.0% reported that symptoms began after a specific incident or injury; 42.0% reported onset without identifiable trauma.3 A purely traumatic model does not account for that second group. Either HFS is heterogeneous in origin, or the initiating event is sometimes subclinical and unrecognized, or recall is unreliable. The available data do not distinguish among these possibilities.

    5.2 The pelvic/pudendal-hypogastric reflex

    Goldstein and Komisaruk proposed that HFS results from pathological activation of a somato-visceral and/or viscero-visceral reflex, which they termed a pelvic/pudendal-hypogastric reflex.5 In this model, afferent input arising from injured or irritated pudendal or pelvic nerve territory drives sustained efferent sympathetic outflow through the hypogastric nerve to the erectile smooth muscle. The smooth muscle remains contracted. The clinical result is the persistent semi-rigid, retracted, often cool penis, together with impaired erection and genito-pelvic dysesthesia.

    The model’s distinguishing feature is that the reflex can be triggered at any of five anatomical levels, which reframes HFS as a final common pathway rather than a single disease.

    5.3 The lumbosacral contribution

    The most concrete evidence offered in support of the reflex model concerns region three. Goldstein and colleagues obtained lumbar magnetic resonance imaging performed within the preceding year from 21 individuals with HFS recruited through an online support group, mean age 28 years (range 19–42). A spine surgeon reviewed sagittal and axial views. Sixteen of 21 (76%) had surgically treatable annular tears, predominantly at L5–S1 and L4–L5, compared with a prevalence of 19% to 29% reported in asymptomatic populations in the prior literature.4 The systematic review separately notes a case of HFS secondary to lumbar disc protrusion managed definitively by discectomy after sex therapy and pelvic floor physical therapy had failed.2

    The limitations of this finding are substantial and should be stated alongside it. The report is a congress abstract rather than a full peer-reviewed paper. The sample is 21 patients recruited from a support group. There was no concurrent matched control group; the comparison is to historical figures from separate literature. Imaging was reviewed by a single reader, and the reading was not reported as blinded. The finding is hypothesis-generating and warrants a properly controlled study. It does not establish causation.

    5.4 A competing account: the trauma–inflammation cascade

    The systematic review identifies a second hypothesis in which an initial injury triggers neurovascular inflammation, producing pelvic floor muscle spasm that raises intracavernosal pressure during flaccidity and inhibits erection, with the state perpetuated by psychological distress.2 This is not mutually exclusive with the reflex model; inflammation could plausibly serve as the afferent driver. The review authors also raise the possibility of a penis–brain axis analogous to the gut–brain axis described in irritable bowel syndrome, while stating explicitly that this requires further investigation.2 Speculative

    5.5 What the models do not explain

    Two observations sit awkwardly with the current framework. The 42% of patients reporting no inciting event have no accounted-for mechanism. And penile color Doppler ultrasound shows normal peak systolic velocity in 98% of reported cases, so no gross arterial abnormality corresponds to the symptoms.2 A third gap is one of duration rather than observation: the reflex model describes how the state could begin, but not why it persists for years after a peripheral injury would be expected to have healed. No study has followed a cohort long enough to say.

    Key points

    • The leading model proposes that injury-driven afferent input pathologically activates a pelvic/pudendal-hypogastric reflex, producing sustained sympathetic contraction of cavernosal smooth muscle.
    • No study has directly measured penile sympathetic outflow in HFS, and no injury site has been demonstrated by imaging or histology.
    • Annular tears were found in 16/21 (76%) of one small uncontrolled imaging cohort reported as a congress abstract.
    • 42% of patients report no inciting event, which no current model explains.
    • The model accounts for how the state begins but not for why it persists, and no cohort has been followed long enough to say.

    6. Evaluation and differential diagnosis Established

    Because no confirmatory test exists, evaluation serves two purposes: excluding conditions with specific treatment, and documenting a baseline. Reported workups include history and physical examination, baseline hormonal profile, penile color Doppler ultrasound, validated questionnaires including the IIEF-5, IPSS, HADS and NIH-CPSI, and lumbar magnetic resonance imaging where back pain or radicular symptoms are present.2 Results are usually normal. In the case reported by Yazar and colleagues, testosterone, prolactin and thyroid function were normal and Doppler showed mild corporal fibrosis with no other findings.8

    Normal investigations are an expected outcome rather than a negative one. They indicate a functional rather than structural problem, and they exclude the alternatives below.

    Table 5 · Principal differential considerations
    ConditionDistinguishing features
    Peyronie’s disease Palpable tunical plaque and acquired curvature or deformity; plaque is not a feature of HFS
    Ischemic priapism Persistent full rigid erection with pain; a urological emergency requiring immediate care
    Chronic prostatitis / chronic pelvic pain syndrome Overlapping pelvic pain and urinary symptoms; assessed with the NIH-CPSI and may coexist with HFS
    Pudendal neuralgia Neuropathic pain in pudendal distribution; reported as comorbid in 16.9% of surveyed patients3
    Erectile dysfunction of vascular origin Abnormal Doppler parameters; HFS cohorts show normal peak systolic velocity in 98% of cases2
    Lumbosacral radiculopathy Back pain, radicular symptoms or neurological signs; may be causal rather than differential under the reflex model

    Requiring urgent assessment: sudden severe penile pain, an erection lasting more than four hours, an audible crack with immediate detumescence and swelling, inability to pass urine, visible blood in the urine, or new saddle anesthesia, leg weakness or bowel or bladder incontinence. These indicate conditions distinct from HFS that require emergency care.

    Key points

    • Diagnosis is clinical and by exclusion; no objective test confirms HFS.
    • Hormonal studies and Doppler ultrasound are typically normal, which is expected and informative.
    • Peyronie’s disease, ischemic priapism, CPPS, pudendal neuralgia and vascular erectile dysfunction should be considered.
    • Lumbar imaging is reasonable where back or radicular symptoms are present.

    7. Management and reported outcomes Hypothesis

    No randomized controlled trial of any intervention for HFS has been published. All outcome data derive from case reports, small series and self-report surveys, none of which control for natural history, regression to the mean or placebo response. The authors concluded that current therapies generally do not benefit most patients and that better understanding of root causes and new treatment strategies are needed.

    Across the studies pooled in the systematic review, only 20% of patients achieved symptom resolution.2 Individual reports are more encouraging but carry the limitations of single cases. Yazar and colleagues described a 36-year-old man treated with tadalafil 5 mg daily, six sessions of low-intensity shockwave therapy, and physical therapy one to two times weekly over 10 to 12 weeks, with resolution of penile rigidity, restoration of erections adequate for intercourse, and sustained benefit at two-year follow-up.8 A further case series reported two patients.9 Among surveyed practitioners, pelvic floor physical therapy was the intervention most often cited as optimal, named by 11 respondents, generally in combination with medical or behavioral treatment.7

    The consistent recommendation across sources is multimodal, individualized management addressing physical and psychological components together.2 This article makes no treatment recommendation. Choice of therapy is a clinical decision requiring assessment of the individual patient.

    Key points

    • No controlled trial of any HFS treatment has been published.
    • Only 20% of patients across pooled studies achieved symptom resolution.
    • PDE5 inhibitors attracted the highest self-reported satisfaction; no intervention produced cure at group level.
    • Pelvic floor physical therapy is the most frequently favored intervention among specialist practitioners.

    8. Prognosis Uncertain

    Prognostic statements about HFS should be treated with caution because the data to support them do not exist. No prospective cohort with systematic follow-up has been published. What can be said is bounded: sustained resolution has been documented in individual patients at 24 months,8 symptom resolution occurred in 20% of patients across the studies pooled in the systematic review,2 and most survey respondents remained dissatisfied with treatments received.2,3 Whether untreated HFS resolves, persists or progresses is unknown, and any specific recovery timeline offered elsewhere is not derived from published evidence.

    9. What would move the field

    The gaps are well defined, which is itself useful. The systematic review calls for expert consensus guidelines, standardized diagnostic criteria and further basic science work on molecular mechanisms.2 To that list, the structure of the current evidence suggests four additions: a controlled imaging study comparing lumbosacral findings in HFS patients against matched asymptomatic controls with blinded reading; direct neurophysiological assessment of penile sympathetic activity and dorsal nerve function; a prospective cohort recruited at presentation rather than from support communities; and randomized evaluation of the interventions already in widespread use. Until these exist, confident mechanistic claims about HFS — including those in this article’s section five — remain provisional.
