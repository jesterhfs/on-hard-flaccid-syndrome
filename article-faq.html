Common questions

Eighty-one questions, answered as directly as the evidence allows. Where the honest answer is that nobody knows, that is the answer given.

100 min read · 43 references

Contents

    The short answers

    • Hard flaccid syndrome (HFS) is not currently curable in any reliable sense. It has no diagnostic criteria, no confirmatory test, and no treatment demonstrated to work in a controlled trial.
    • The working model on this site is that a stretch injury drives excess sympathetic outflow to penile erectile smooth muscle, which stays contracted. That is a hypothesis, not established fact.
    • Pelvic floor dysfunction, posture, muscle imbalance and psychological stress are not supported as causes. Several are common consequences.
    • Reported cures come almost entirely from patient accounts that cannot be verified, for a condition nobody has yet defined precisely enough to say who had it.
    • Nobody can give a timeline for a cure. The field has eight published studies and no controlled trial of anything.
    Hypothesis proposed and partially supported, not established

    1. How to read these answers

    Two things make an honest FAQ about this condition difficult. The first is that most questions patients ask have not been studied, so a truthful answer is often a description of an absence. The second is that the absence is uncomfortable to read when you are frightened, which creates pressure to fill it with something. This article does not fill it.

    Where evidence exists it is cited. Where a claim rests on the working model rather than on data, that is marked. Where the answer is genuinely unknown, the answer is that it is unknown, and the reason is usually the same: a 2025 systematic review found eight eligible studies in the entire literature, no objective test that assists diagnosis, no validated diagnostic criteria, and no randomized controlled trial of any intervention.2

    One framing assumption runs through everything below. This site treats the hard flaccid state as sustained contraction of penile erectile smooth muscle, driven by excess sympathetic outflow following a peripheral nerve injury — the reflex model proposed by Goldstein and Komisaruk.1 Adopting a model is necessary to answer mechanistic questions at all. It is not the same as the model being proven, and section 9 examines it directly.

    2. What causes it

    Does pelvic floor dysfunction cause HFS?

    Almost certainly not, though it very often accompanies it. The pelvic floor is skeletal muscle under somatic control from the pudendal nerve.14 The hard flaccid state occurs in cavernosal smooth muscle, which is involuntary and autonomically controlled.11,15 A skeletal muscle cannot set the resting tone of a smooth muscle it does not innervate. Pelvic floor abnormality was reported by 85.3% of 143 surveyed patients,3 which is why the association feels causal — but a parallel consequence of the same autonomic disturbance explains the co-occurrence without requiring one to produce the other. An alternative model does place pelvic floor spasm nearer the center of the mechanism, and it has not been excluded.5

    Do muscle imbalances cause HFS?

    There is no published evidence for this, and it faces the same problem as the question above. Skeletal muscle arrangement does not govern autonomic tone in the corpora.11,15 Absence of evidence is not proof the claim is false, but it should not be treated as established while untested.

    Does posture cause HFS?

    No published evidence links posture to HFS. The confusion is understandable, because symptoms genuinely do vary with position: 73.0% of surveyed patients improve lying down and 64.5% worsen standing.3 That is a symptom responding to posture, not posture producing a condition. Standing raises sympathetic outflow through the baroreflex,8 which under the working model is why the state worsens — see section 3.

    Do hip problems cause HFS?

    This is the fourth version of one question, and it gets the same answer for the same reason. No published evidence links hip pathology to HFS, and hip structures are skeletal muscle, bone and joint — none of which set autonomic tone in the corpora.11,15

    One thing does make the question reasonable. Hip pathology genuinely produces groin and pelvic pain, and referred pain in that region is easy to confuse with pelvic-origin symptoms. So a hip problem can coexist with HFS and can account for some of what you are feeling. That is worth having assessed on its own account. It is a different claim from the hip having caused the hard flaccid state.

    Do back problems cause HFS?

    Possibly in a minority, and this is taken more seriously than the questions above. The reflex model proposes that the relevant reflex can be triggered at five levels, one of which is the cauda equina, with lumbosacral annular tears and sacral Tarlov cysts named as candidate triggers.1,9 One imaging study found surgically treatable annular tears in 16 of 21 HFS patients (76%) against 19% to 29% reported in asymptomatic populations, and read this as support for sacral radiculopathy driving excess sympathetic activity.10 That study is a congress abstract with 21 participants recruited from a support group, no matched control group, and a single unblinded reader. Annular tears are common in people without symptoms. It is a lead worth following, not a finding.

    Does a suspensory ligament tear cause HFS?

    No published case links the two, and the anatomy argues against it. The penile suspensory ligament anchors the base of the penis to the pubic symphysis and sets the erect angle. Dividing it is an established lengthening procedure, and the length gained comes from releasing the concealed portion of the shaft forward rather than from any change to the erectile tissue itself.37 It is connective tissue. It contains no erectile smooth muscle and carries no autonomic supply to the corpora cavernosa.

    That matters, because the defining sign of HFS is sustained contraction of cavernosal smooth muscle.1 A torn or stretched ligament changes how the penis is held — a lower erect angle, sometimes a sense of instability at the base — and there is no route by which it could set the resting tone of a tissue it does not innervate.

    The question keeps arising because the exposures overlap. Aggressive stretching, hanging and weight-based lengthening are among the practices most often reported at onset,2,5 and they are also the practices most likely to strain the suspensory apparatus. Two injuries from one activity is not the same as one injury causing the other. If both happened, you would expect both sets of findings — an altered erect angle and a hard flaccid state — rather than one explaining the other. Nobody has imaged the suspensory ligament in an HFS cohort, so this is reasoning from anatomy rather than from data.

    Can pelvic congestion cause HFS?

    Unknown, and the terminology itself needs correcting. An international working group convened by the American Vein & Lymphatic Society introduced the Symptoms–Varices–Pathophysiology classification and stated that “pelvic congestion syndrome,” along with May-Thurner and nutcracker syndromes, are inaccurate terms that should no longer be used.16 More importantly, dilated pelvic veins are common in people without symptoms.17 Finding them alongside HFS does not establish they are related. No study has compared pelvic venous findings in HFS patients against matched controls, so the frequency of these findings among patients cannot support a conclusion either way.

    Do mental health problems cause HFS?

    The evidence does not support it. Psychological distress is reported by 89.5% of surveyed patients,3 and one review proposes a loop in which injury produces distress, distress drives sympathetic stimulation, and that sustains pelvic floor muscle spasm.5 That is a proposed sequence, not a demonstrated one, and no study has separated cause from consequence. Given that the symptom is frightening by nature, distress as a result is at least as plausible as distress as a cause. There is also a specific argument against the psychological reading: loss of nocturnal and morning erections is documented across the case literature,4,5 and sleep-related erections are generated centrally, independently of conscious arousal and independently of anxiety.11 Their absence indicates something other than performance anxiety.

    Where psychology does matter is at the point of exposure. Compulsive masturbation and body-image concerns are plausible routes to sustaining the mechanical injury that starts the process. That is a route to the injury, not a cause of the physiology that follows.

    Is HFS a mind-body connection issue?

    Not in the sense the phrase usually carries. It is generally deployed to mean the problem originates in the mind, and the evidence does not support that. What is true is narrower: sympathetic outflow is modulated by arousal and stress, so mental state can plausibly influence the intensity of a physically driven symptom. Modulating a mechanism is not the same as creating it. Physical examination and imaging in HFS are typically normal,5 and normal investigations are frequently misread as evidence of psychological origin when they are in fact what a functional rather than structural problem would be expected to produce.

    Does Ehlers-Danlos syndrome or hypermobility cause HFS?

    Nobody has studied it, and unlike the questions above this one deserves more than a dismissal. It may be the most promising susceptibility hypothesis anyone has raised for this condition, for two specific reasons.

    Tissue fragility. The Ehlers-Danlos syndromes are a genetically heterogeneous group of heritable connective tissue disorders characterized by joint hypermobility, skin hyperextensibility and tissue fragility, with 13 subtypes recognized in the 2017 international classification.35 If connective tissue is more extensible and more fragile than average, a given stretch would be expected to exceed what the tissue tolerates at lower force. That is a direct candidate answer to the question in the next entry — why the same activity injures one man and not another.

    Autonomic dysfunction. Hypermobile EDS is strongly associated with dysautonomia. Among 102 adults with hypermobile EDS or hypermobility spectrum disorder undergoing head-up tilt testing, 48% met criteria for postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome, 25.5% had orthostatic intolerance and 3.9% had hypotension.36 Set that beside a condition proposed to involve excess sympathetic outflow, in which 64.5% of patients report worsening on standing,3 and the overlap is striking. A pre-existing tendency toward autonomic dysregulation is a plausible amplifier for a disorder of autonomic control.

    Taken together: tissue that injures more readily, paired with an autonomic system that regulates less well. That maps onto both halves of the proposed mechanism at once. Hypothesis

    Now the restraint it deserves. No study has measured hypermobility in an HFS cohort. Ehlers-Danlos syndrome does not appear in the systematic review2 or among the comorbidities recorded in the patient survey.3 And hypermobility is common in the general population, so finding it in HFS patients would prove nothing without a matched control group — the same base rate problem that undermines the annular tear finding. This is a hypothesis worth testing, not an explanation. Practically: if you have diagnosed EDS or marked hypermobility, mention it to your clinician. It costs nothing to say and it is exactly the sort of detail that might turn out to matter.

    Does finasteride cause HFS?

    Not as far as the literature shows, though the question is more reasonable than most. Finasteride is a 5α-reductase inhibitor used for androgenetic alopecia and benign prostatic hyperplasia. A proportion of men report sexual, neurological and psychiatric symptoms that persist after stopping it, described as post-finasteride syndrome (PFS). Whether PFS is a distinct clinical entity remains contested among specialists: a 2025 review in the journal that publishes most HFS work poses exactly that question in its title and does not settle it,38 and the proposed mechanisms remain hypothetical.39

    The overlap is real. Erectile dysfunction, reduced libido, genital sensory change and complaints of penile shrinkage appear in both. What does not overlap is the defining sign: a firm, semi-rigid flaccid penis is not a described feature of PFS, and no published case attributes HFS to finasteride.2

    In this community’s own onset poll, 2 of 154 respondents attributed their condition to finasteride and 1 to an antidepressant, about 1.9% between them (community surveys). That is self-reported attribution collected outside any study, not established cause.

    The honest position: if you took finasteride and then developed HFS, your own case cannot tell you which occurred. Both are conditions with contested mechanisms and overlapping symptoms, and the question is answerable only by a cohort nobody has assembled. Decisions about starting or stopping a prescription belong with the prescriber.

    Do SSRIs cause HFS?

    The same answer, with one difference: persistence after SSRIs has regulatory recognition that PFS does not. Sexual dysfunction during treatment with selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) is well established; what is contested is persistence after stopping. In 2019 the European Medicines Agency’s Pharmacovigilance Risk Assessment Committee concluded that sexual dysfunction associated with SSRIs and serotonin–noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors can be long-lasting after discontinuation, and required the product information for those drugs to say so.41 The condition is described as post-SSRI sexual dysfunction (PSSD). Its biological plausibility and symptom profile have been reviewed,40 and its incidence remains unquantified for reasons that have themselves been examined.43

    The overlap with HFS is narrower than with PFS but sharper. Genital numbness is characteristic of PSSD,40 and sensory loss is reported by 74.8% of surveyed HFS patients.3 A man with genital anesthesia after an SSRI and a man with genital anesthesia after a stretch injury can look similar in a clinic.

    The flaccid rigidity is again the discriminator. It is not a described feature of PSSD, and no published case links SSRIs to HFS.2

    Do pharmaceutical drugs cause HFS?

    No drug has been established as a cause of HFS. Three claims get collapsed into one here, and separating them resolves most of the confusion. Many drugs cause sexual dysfunction while they are being taken, which is uncontroversial and usually reversible on stopping. A smaller number are associated with dysfunction that persists after stopping, and those associations are contested to differing degrees. Neither is the same as causing HFS, which requires producing a flaccid penis that stays firm at rest.

    Why do I have HFS when other people doing the same thing don’t?

    Nobody knows, and it is one of the more conspicuous gaps in the field. No study has examined risk factors, susceptibility or why identical activity injures one person and not another. There is no genetic study, no anatomical comparison, and no prospective work following people who engage in the practices associated with onset. Plausible contributors would include how much force was applied and for how long, individual variation in nerve anatomy, and — under the working model — whether a sensitization process becomes established after the injury rather than settling. All of that is reasoning, not data. Hypothesis

    Two things are worth saying alongside the honest answer. Nothing in the literature supports the idea that susceptibility reflects anything about the person beyond biology. And 42.0% of surveyed patients report no identifiable inciting event at all,3 so the framing of “what did I do differently” does not even apply to a large share of patients.

    What if I don’t know what caused my HFS?

    That is common and it does not exclude the diagnosis. In the largest survey, 58.0% of patients reported symptoms beginning after a specific incident. The remaining 42.0% reported no identifiable trauma.3 Roughly four in ten patients have no inciting event to point to. Whether those cases involve a subclinical injury, a forgotten one, or a genuinely different process is unknown, and it is one of the more important unanswered questions in the condition.

    Key points

    • Pelvic floor dysfunction, posture and muscle imbalance are not supported as causes; the first is a very common companion.
    • Back pathology is a live but weakly supported possibility in a minority.
    • “Pelvic congestion syndrome” is a retired term, and the findings it describes are common in healthy people.
    • Distress is near-universal but its direction has never been established, and lost nocturnal erections argue against a psychological origin.
    • 42% of patients report no inciting event at all.

    3. Why it does what it does

    Why does the hard flaccid state get worse when I stand up?

    Because standing raises sympathetic activity. When you stand, gravity pools blood in the legs and pelvis, cardiac filling falls, and the arterial baroreflex responds by increasing sympathetic outflow — muscle sympathetic nerve activity rises progressively with the degree of orthostatic stress.8 Under the working model, sympathetic outflow is exactly what is holding the erectile smooth muscle contracted,1,11 so more of it means a firmer flaccid state. Lying down reverses the demand and 73.0% of patients report improvement.3 This is ordinary physiology acting on an abnormal system, not a sign of deterioration.

    Why does it get worse with exercise?

    The same reason, more so. Physical exertion is a sympathetic event, and the harder the effort the greater the outflow.8 Direct mechanical loading of the perineum — cycling in particular — adds a second consideration, since blunt perineal trauma is itself a described route to pudendal injury.1 No study has examined exercise in HFS specifically, so the reasoning is mechanistic rather than evidential.

    What causes the hourglassing?

    This is one of the more interesting symptoms, and the answer can be reasoned out partly by elimination. In Peyronie’s disease a fibrous plaque in the tunica albuginea produces indentation that worsens with erection, because inelastic tissue cannot expand while the surrounding tunica does.12 The hourglassing in HFS behaves in the opposite way: present when flaccid or partly erect, gone at full erection. Whatever produces it must therefore be something maximal intracavernosal pressure can overcome — and a tunical lesion is by definition not overcome by pressure. So it is not a tunical problem, and it is pressure-dependent.

    That points to a segment of corporal tissue holding more tone than its neighbors. The substrate exists: corporal smooth muscle is sparsely innervated, and its cells are coupled by gap junctions built from connexin43, which coordinate contraction across the tissue.13 A syncytium coupled that way turns uneven input into a coherent band rather than scattered cell-by-cell contraction. At full erection, nitric oxide-driven relaxation and high pressure override the difference and the waist disappears. This reasoning is set out at greater length in the mechanisms articles. It is a deduction, not a demonstrated finding — nobody has imaged it. Hypothesis

    Why is my penis numb?

    Numbness or sensory loss is reported by 74.8% of surveyed patients,3 and the case literature describes numbness or coldness with decreased sensitivity, particularly at the glans.5 The most direct explanation is partial injury to the dorsal nerve of the penis, the terminal sensory branch of the pudendal nerve.18 Stretch beyond a nerve’s elastic limit is a recognized cause of axonotmesis, in which axons are disrupted while the outer sheath stays intact.19 Fewer working axons means less sensation. This also explains why numbness and unpleasant abnormal sensation can coexist in the same area: partial deafferentation reduces input while leaving central neurons more excitable.20

    Why is my glans soft and cold even when I have an erection?

    This is documented as a characteristic finding — a soft, cold glans during an otherwise adequate erection.5 The reason is structural. The corpora cavernosa are wrapped in the tunica albuginea, a tough sheath that lets pressure build and traps blood, so they can feel firm even at modest volume.11 The glans is part of the corpus spongiosum and has no tunica, so it cannot achieve firmness by trapping. It depends on continuous flow. It is also supplied by a different vessel — the dorsal artery, rather than the cavernosal artery that fills the shaft.31 Restricted inflow therefore shows up in the glans before it shows up in the shaft, which is why the two can dissociate so obviously.

    Why are my penile circumflex veins enlarged?

    Usually because they are visible rather than because they are diseased, but the anatomy is worth knowing. Blood leaves the erectile tissue in layers: small emissary veins pierce the tunica albuginea from the subtunical venular plexus and drain into the circumflex veins, which run circumferentially around the corpora and empty into the deep dorsal vein.11,32 How prominent they look varies with skin thickness, body fat, temperature, arousal and recent activity, and prominent superficial vessels are a common normal finding.

    They are also among the most frequently reported observations in this condition: 77.8% of 153 respondents in the community symptom survey reported vascular changes, described as engorged or spider veins (community surveys). That frequency shows the observation is real and common. It does not show it is pathological, because no study has measured vein caliber in HFS patients against matched controls.

    One thing it is not evidence of is a vascular cause of HFS. In the largest clinically assessed cohort, 88 men, penile Doppler ultrasonography showed normal peak systolic velocity throughout, with abnormal end-diastolic velocity in 2.21 Surface vein prominence and cavernosal veno-occlusive function are different things, and only the second is what Doppler measures.31 Seek assessment if a vein becomes hard, cord-like or tender rather than merely visible — see the next answer.

    Why is my dorsal penile vein enlarged?

    First, which one. Two veins share the name and only one is visible. The superficial dorsal vein lies above Buck’s fascia, drains the penile skin and prepuce, and empties into the external pudendal veins. The deep dorsal vein lies beneath Buck’s fascia, drains the glans and the circumflex veins, and empties into the periprostatic venous plexus.11,32 If you can see it, you are looking at the superficial dorsal vein. A Doppler report describing the deep dorsal vein is not describing what you noticed in the mirror.

    Visible prominence of the superficial vein follows the same variables as the circumflex veins above, is commonly reported in HFS, and has never been measured against controls.

    The reason this question deserves a careful answer is one differential. Penile Mondor’s disease is superficial thrombophlebitis, most often of the superficial dorsal vein, presenting as a palpable cord that may be tender and sometimes following prolonged or vigorous sexual activity.42 It is usually self-limiting, but it is a specific diagnosis with specific management and it is distinguishable on examination: a thrombosed vein feels like a firm cord, while a merely prominent vein is soft and compressible. If that describes yours, have it examined rather than assuming it belongs to HFS.

    A visible dorsal vein is also not evidence of venous leak. Cavernosal veno-occlusive dysfunction is a functional diagnosis made from end-diastolic velocity on Doppler after intracavernosal injection,31 not from the appearance of the skin.

    Is my penis getting smaller? Will I lose length permanently?

    A change in penis shape or size is the single most commonly reported symptom, at 92.3% of 143 surveyed patients.3 The survey groups shortening, retraction, altered contour and altered girth under one heading and the published summary does not separate them, so what drove that figure cannot be determined.

    The mechanism offers some reassurance. Smooth muscle contraction shortens tissue — that is what contraction does. A penis held in sustained contraction is a shorter penis at rest, and that is a state rather than a loss of tissue. No study has measured stretched penile length in HFS patients, before or after, so nothing is established either way. What can be said is that the largest cohort, 88 men, showed no fibrosis on imaging,21 and fibrosis is the mechanism by which length is actually lost in other conditions.

    Why does my penis feel like it isn’t part of my body?

    Patients describe this consistently — hollow, disconnected, unstable, not theirs — and it is one of the hardest symptoms to say out loud to a clinician. No study has examined it in HFS. The available explanation is inference: partial injury to the dorsal nerve reduces sensory input,18,19 and a cortical representation deprived of reliable input would be expected to degrade, making the organ’s place in the body map less stable. That reasoning is several steps long and entirely untested. Hypothesis

    One thing is worth stating plainly. Difficulty describing a symptom is not evidence that it is imaginary, and this one should not be assumed psychological simply because it is strange to articulate. It should not be assumed neurological either. Nobody has investigated it.

    Why do my testicles pull up?

    Scrotal position is controlled by two muscles with different nerves. The dartos is smooth muscle in the scrotal wall under autonomic control, and the cremaster is skeletal muscle supplied by the genital branch of the genitofemoral nerve. Under the working model, sustained sympathetic overactivity would keep the dartos contracted, which is what produces the tight, drawn-up scrotum patients describe.1 The two outputs can be separated: one reported case describes a nerve block that reduced scrotal retraction while leaving the hard flaccid state unchanged, which is what different efferent supplies would predict.

    Is the numbness permanent?

    It depends on how badly the nerve was injured, and nobody has determined which injury class applies in HFS. Neurapraxia — the mildest, involving loss of myelin without axonal damage — recovers completely within days to weeks. Axonotmesis, in which axons are disrupted while the outer sheath stays intact, is the class typically caused by stretch; recovery is possible but slow, with regrowth at roughly one millimeter per day. Neurotmesis, complete transection, does not recover spontaneously.19

    Two qualifications. Symptoms persisting for years are not consistent with pure neurapraxia, which should have resolved. And if a central component contributes to the sensory picture, peripheral regeneration alone would not necessarily restore normal sensation.20 No study has followed sensory recovery in HFS patients over time.

    Is this dangerous? Can it permanently damage my penis?

    The findings do not support the fear, though the fear is close to universal. Physical examination is described as unremarkable, and imaging, Doppler and hormonal studies as normal.5 Peak systolic velocity was normal in 98% of reported cases.2 The largest assessed cohort — 88 men — had normal hemodynamics throughout and no fibrosis on imaging.21 Nothing in the literature describes HFS progressing to tissue death, gangrene or any acutely dangerous state.

    The honest qualifications: no long-term follow-up study exists, so nobody can speak to outcomes over decades, and there is a theoretical concern that chronic reduced oxygenation could drive fibrotic change over time — a pathway demonstrated in animal models but never in an HFS patient.22 Separately, the emergency presentations listed at the end of this article are genuinely dangerous and are not HFS.     Could this be cancer or something serious?

    Nothing in the HFS literature reports any association with malignancy, and imaging in this condition is characteristically normal.5 That said, the reason to be assessed is precisely that HFS is a diagnosis of exclusion — it is what remains after other conditions have been ruled out.2 A new painless lump, blood in the urine or semen, or any progressive change needs evaluation on its own account, regardless of what else you have. That is not because HFS causes those things. It is because those findings need explaining whatever the context.

    Why are all my tests normal?

    Because the problem is functional rather than structural, and your investigations are looking for structure. Examination is described as unremarkable, and imaging, Doppler and hormonal studies as normal.5 Penile color Doppler ultrasound showed normal peak systolic velocity in 98% of reported cases.2 The largest assessed cohort — 88 men — had normal hemodynamics throughout and no fibrosis on imaging.21 Normal results are the expected finding. They exclude vascular disease and structural damage, which is what they are for, and their normality is itself informative rather than a dead end.

    Is this Peyronie’s disease?

    Almost certainly not, and the distinction is worth making precisely because both involve deformity. Peyronie’s disease produces a palpable tunical plaque with acquired curvature or indentation, and it is defined against a published guideline.12 HFS does not produce a plaque. If you have a palpable lump in the shaft with a new curve, that needs assessment for Peyronie’s regardless of anything else, because it has its own management pathway.

    Is HFS the same as chronic pelvic pain syndrome?

    They overlap substantially without being the same thing. Chronic prostatitis / chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CPPS) shares the pelvic pain, the urinary symptoms, the normal investigations and the young male population. It also has something HFS lacks: a validated measuring instrument, the National Institutes of Health Chronic Prostatitis Symptom Index, developed and validated as an outcome measure.26 That instrument appears in reported HFS workups,2 which tells you how closely the two sit.

    What separates them is the defining sign. A penis that stays firm at rest without arousal is not a feature of CPPS, and it is the anchor of HFS. Some clinicians regard HFS as a presentation within the broader pelvic pain family; expert consensus has instead placed it within persistent genital arousal disorder and genito-pelvic dysesthesia.9 Neither classification is settled, and the two conditions can coexist.

    Is HFS a central sensitization disorder?

    Possibly in part, and it is one of the better questions anyone asks about this condition — but the label does not cover the thing that defines it.

    Central sensitization is a prolonged increase in the excitability and synaptic efficacy of neurons in central pain pathways, triggered by nociceptor input. It shows up as pain hypersensitivity: dynamic tactile allodynia, secondary hyperalgesia, aftersensations and enhanced temporal summation.20 Several things about HFS fit that picture. It would explain why symptoms persist long after an injury should have healed. It would explain why interventions aimed at the penis and the pudendal nerve reportedly fail — if the maintaining process has moved into the spinal cord, treating the original site removes nothing. It fits the reported ketamine responses, since ketamine is an NMDA receptor antagonist and central sensitization is NMDA dependent.20 And it accounts for numbness and unpleasant abnormal sensation occurring in the same territory, which otherwise looks contradictory.

    Here is the problem. Central sensitization, as defined, is a pain phenomenon. The defining sign of HFS is not pain — it is a sustained smooth muscle state, and pain is not even universal in this condition. To make central sensitization explain the hard flaccid state you have to propose that the same process occurs in the somato-autonomic limb rather than only the nociceptive one, leaving sympathetic output tonically elevated. That is a further claim, and nobody has demonstrated it. The reported failure of sympathetic blockade points the other way, toward at least part of the maintenance sitting in the effector tissue itself. Hypothesis

    The frustrating part is that this is directly testable and has never been tested. Quantitative sensory testing against matched controls would show whether the signatures of central sensitization are present. It needs no new technology and no new patients beyond a comparison group. It is, in this author’s view, the single most informative study that could be run on HFS today.

    Is HFS complex regional pain syndrome?

    Not as currently defined, though the resemblance is real enough to be worth investigating properly.

    Complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS) is a chronic pain condition, usually affecting a limb after an injury, in which the pain is disproportionate to the inciting event and accompanies sensory, vasomotor, sudomotor and motor or trophic changes. It is diagnosed against the Budapest criteria, which were validated in 113 patients with CRPS type I and 47 with non-CRPS neuropathic pain, retaining sensitivity of 0.99 with specificity improved to 0.68.34

    The parallels are not superficial. Both follow a peripheral injury. Both persist beyond what the injury explains. Both involve proposed sympathetic participation, and sympathetic blockade is attempted in both. Most strikingly, two of the CRPS diagnostic categories are vasomotor changes — temperature asymmetry and skin color change — and both are documented features of HFS, where a cold penis and discoloration are reported.5 Sensory findings in both can include reduced sensation and abnormal sensation together.

    The differences matter more. CRPS requires pain, and pain is neither universal in HFS nor its defining feature. CRPS motor findings involve skeletal muscle — dystonia, tremor, weakness — whereas the central sign in HFS is sustained contraction of visceral smooth muscle, which has no CRPS counterpart. Genital CRPS is not an established entity. And there is a practical obstacle to the comparison: CRPS has validated criteria and HFS has none,2 so no cohort has ever been assessed against the Budapest criteria to see how many would meet them.

    The reasonable position is that CRPS is a useful analogy and an untested hypothesis, not a diagnosis. Applying the label would import an entire framework — and its treatments — on the strength of a family resemblance. Someone should run the comparison. Until somebody does, it remains a question rather than an answer. Hypothesis

    Will it get worse over time?

    Nobody knows. No prospective cohort with systematic follow-up has been published. What can be said is that symptom resolution occurred in only 20% of patients across the studies pooled in the systematic review.2 There is a mechanistic argument for progression — sustained contraction reduces perfusion, loss of nocturnal erections removes the tissue’s main reoxygenation, and cavernosal hypoxia raises transforming growth factor β1 and drives fibrosis in animal models22 — but that chain has never been demonstrated in a patient with HFS, and the 88-man cohort found no fibrosis at a mean symptom duration of 14 months.21 Hypothesis

    Key points

    • Standing and exercise worsen symptoms because both raise sympathetic outflow.
    • Hourglassing that resolves at full erection cannot be a tunical lesion; a segmental tone band fits.
    • Numbness is consistent with partial stretch injury to the dorsal nerve.
    • Normal investigations are the expected result and exclude structural disease.

    4. Whether it can be cured

    Do people get cured of HFS?

    Some people supposedly recover. Whether anyone is “cured” is a harder question than it sounds, and the difficulty is worth understanding rather than dismissing.

    Start with what is documented. Across the studies pooled in the systematic review, 20% of patients achieved symptom resolution.2 Individual reports describe sustained improvement — one patient treated with tadalafil, shockwave therapy and physical therapy remained well at two-year follow-up.6 Another became symptom-free after pelvic floor physical therapy. These are real, and they are also single unblinded cases in which nothing rules out the possibility that the patient would have recovered anyway.

    Now the harder part. The overwhelming majority of reported cures circulate as anecdotal accounts in patient communities, and four problems apply to all of them. First, HFS has no validated diagnostic criteria,2 so nobody can establish that the person who recovered had the same condition as the person reading about it. Second, there is no objective measure of the hard flaccid state, so “cured” means whatever the person reporting it means. Third, the natural history is unknown — without knowing how often HFS resolves on its own, no recovery can be attributed to whatever the person happened to be doing at the time. Fourth, reporting is biased in both directions at once: people who recover often post once and leave, while people who do not recover stay and post for years. Neither group is representative.

    None of this means recovery does not happen. It means that a specific claim of the form “X cured my HFS” carries almost no information about whether X would do anything for you.

    Could this go away on its own?

    Possibly, and the frustrating truth is that nobody knows how often. The natural history of HFS has never been studied — no cohort of untreated patients has been followed. The 20% resolution figure comes from patients across the pooled studies who were receiving something,2 so it cannot be separated from spontaneous recovery. This is not a minor gap. Without knowing how often HFS resolves by itself, no treatment can be credited with anything, which is why the absence of controlled trials matters so much.

    I’ve had this for years. Is it too late for me?

    No evidence establishes a point after which improvement becomes impossible, and no evidence rules one out either. Two considerations pull in opposite directions. There is a mechanistic argument that long-standing disease becomes structural rather than functional, as chronic reduced oxygenation drives fibrotic remodeling — a chain demonstrated in animal models and never in a patient.22 Against that, the 88-man cohort had no fibrosis at a mean symptom duration of 14 months,21 and one published case reports sustained resolution maintained at two-year follow-up.6

    No study has stratified outcomes by symptom duration, which is one of the cheapest analyses the field could run and has not run. Until it does, duration should be treated as an open question rather than a verdict. Hypothesis

    If it improves, can it come back?

    No study has examined relapse. What is known indirectly: masturbation worsens symptoms in 75.9% of surveyed patients,3 and the proposed mechanism begins with a mechanical injury to tissue that would presumably remain susceptible to the same injury afterward.1 On that reasoning, recurrence after renewed exposure would be unsurprising. It remains reasoning. The one case with long follow-up reported sustained benefit without relapse.6

    When will a reliable cure be developed?

    Nobody can give a timeline, and anyone who does is guessing. Consider what would have to happen first. A condition needs diagnostic criteria before you can reliably identify who has it; HFS has none.2 It needs an objective measure before you can tell whether treatment worked; there is none. It needs its mechanism confirmed before treatments can be aimed rationally; the leading mechanism has never been measured in a patient. And it needs controlled trials; not one has been published for any intervention.2

    A 2025 systematic review found eight studies meeting its inclusion criteria;2 counting conference abstracts, correspondence and a preprint as well, the whole literature comes to about twenty items. A 2025 editorial noted that a PubMed search returned 12 results against roughly 410,000 general web results.7 The systematic review’s own conclusion is that expert consensus guidelines, standardized diagnostic criteria and further basic science are all needed.2 Those are the prerequisites, not the cure. What can be said is that the first steps are cheap and nobody has taken them, which is the most frustrating fact about this condition.

    Key points

    • 20% symptom resolution across pooled studies; recovery happens but is not the norm.
    • Anecdotal cures cannot be assessed because the condition has no criteria, no objective measure, and no known natural history.
    • No timeline for a cure can honestly be given; the prerequisites do not yet exist.

    5. Treatments people ask about

    Does pelvic floor physical therapy cure HFS?

    It does not appear to, and the reason follows from the anatomy. Pelvic floor physical therapy treats skeletal muscle under somatic control.14 The defining sign of HFS occurs in smooth muscle under autonomic control.11,15 Treating one tissue should not be expected to resolve a problem in the other. The data are consistent with that: pelvic floor physical therapy scored 3.5 ± 2.4 out of 10 for satisfaction among surveyed patients — better than most options, well short of resolution.3

    Should I go to pelvic floor physical therapy anyway?

    There is a reasonable case for it, provided expectations are set correctly. Pelvic floor abnormality is reported by 85.3% of patients,3 and hypertonic pelvic floor disorders genuinely produce pain and urinary symptoms.14 Among surveyed sexual medicine practitioners, pelvic floor therapy was the intervention most frequently cited as optimal, named by 11 of 36 respondents.23 So it addresses a real component of the illness. It is unlikely to resolve the hard flaccid state itself. Treating the pelvic floor and curing HFS are different goals, and conflating them is what produces disappointment. This is a decision for you and a clinician, not something this article can decide.

    If pelvic floor therapy doesn’t cure it, why do doctors keep recommending it?

    Because in a condition with no diagnostic criteria, no confirmatory test and no management guideline,2 clinicians default to the lowest-risk intervention that plausibly addresses what is in front of them — and what is in front of them looks like a genito-pelvic problem. The survey data show the position they are in: of 36 sexual medicine practitioners, only 52% considered HFS a legitimate medical condition, 13% reported treatment success, 57% reported inconsistent results, and pelvic floor therapy was nonetheless the intervention most frequently named as optimal, by 11 respondents.23 That is what defaulting looks like in aggregate.

    It is worth being fair about this rather than cynical. The recommendation is not entirely bad: pelvic floor abnormality is reported by 85.3% of patients,3 hypertonic pelvic floor disorders genuinely produce pain and urinary symptoms,14 and the intervention is cheap, reversible and carries little risk. Offering a low-risk treatment for a documented component of an illness is defensible medicine when nothing better exists.

    What is not supported is presenting it as treatment for the hard flaccid state itself. That is where the recommendation outruns the evidence, and where patients end up concluding they failed at physical therapy when the therapy was never aimed at the tissue producing their defining symptom.

    Will general physical therapy or physiotherapy cure HFS?

    No evidence supports it as a cure, and the tissue argument applies as it does to pelvic floor work: musculoskeletal physiotherapy treats skeletal muscle, joints and connective tissue, none of which set autonomic tone in the corpora.11,15 The one paper written primarily from a physiotherapy perspective describes a single case managed with a biopsychosocial approach — pain management, therapeutic exercise including pelvic floor re-education, graded exposure to activity, and education — with reported benefit.29 One unblinded case establishes that someone improved. It does not establish that the therapy caused it.

    Will myofascial release cure HFS?

    No study has examined it in HFS. Myofascial techniques target fascia and skeletal muscle, so the same limitation applies — they are not acting on the tissue that produces the hard flaccid state.15 They may plausibly help the pelvic floor and pain components that most patients also have. Expecting more than that is not supported.

    Will strengthening my glutes cure HFS?

    There is no evidence for this and a reason to doubt it. Gluteal strengthening acts on skeletal muscle; the defining sign is smooth muscle contraction.15 The nearest thing to supporting data is a conference abstract describing a 16-year-old (btcalvit) who became symptom-free after pelvic floor physical therapy that included exercises targeting abdominal and gluteal muscles. That is a single unblinded case, in the youngest patient described anywhere in this literature, receiving several interventions at once. It cannot separate the gluteal component from the rest, or from spontaneous recovery.

    Will working out cure HFS?

    No evidence supports exercise as a treatment, and there is a reason it can feel worse rather than better: exertion raises sympathetic outflow, which is the signal the working model holds responsible for the contracted state.8 Activities that load the perineum directly, cycling in particular, carry a further consideration, since blunt perineal trauma is a described route to pudendal injury.1 General fitness is worth maintaining on its own merits. Treating it as therapy for this condition is not supported by anything published.

    Does NoFap cure HFS?

    No evidence supports it, and the mechanism argues against it. If the initiating lesion is a peripheral nerve injury driving a sympathetic reflex,1 then abstinence does not repair an injured axon and does not reverse a sensitized reflex. Nerve regeneration after axonotmesis proceeds at roughly one millimeter per day regardless of sexual behavior.19 One account circulating among patients describes several years of complete abstinence without resolution; that is an anecdote and carries no evidential weight, and it is mentioned only because the opposite anecdote circulates equally freely.

    There is a narrower point that is defensible. Masturbation worsens symptoms in 75.9% of surveyed patients,3 and sexual activity involves substantial sympathetic activation. Avoiding an activity that reliably aggravates your symptoms is sensible. Expecting abstinence itself to be curative is a different claim, and it is not supported.

    Will changing my diet help?

    No study supports any dietary intervention for HFS. In the patient survey, diet and nutrition changes scored 2.8 ± 2.6 out of 10.3 General health measures are worth pursuing on their own merits. There is no evidence that any specific diet affects the mechanism proposed for this condition.

    Will taking supplements cure HFS?

    No supplement has been studied in HFS, and none appears among the interventions cataloged in the systematic review.2 Two practical cautions. Supplements are not regulated to the standard applied to medicines, so contents and doses vary. And some interact with prescription drugs, which matters if you are taking anything for this condition or any other — worth mentioning to whoever prescribes for you.

    Will fixing my gut cure HFS?

    No evidence supports it. Constipation is reported by patients3 and fits the working model as a parallel consequence rather than a cause: sympathetic activity inhibits gastrointestinal motility, so a disturbance producing excess sympathetic outflow to the pelvis would be expected to affect the bowel as well as the penis. Treating bowel symptoms is worth doing because bowel symptoms are worth treating. There is no published basis for expecting it to change the penile symptom.

    Do PDE5 inhibitors work?

    Partially, for some people, and they are the best-rated option in the only survey that measured it — 4.8 ± 2.6 out of 10, ahead of everything else.3 Mechanistically this makes sense: they prolong the nitric oxide relaxation signal, pushing the same tissue in the opposite direction to the one it is stuck in.15 They do not address whatever is driving the contraction, which is presumably why they help without curing. The original case series reported them as ineffective in four patients.4

    Do alpha blockers help?

    This is the most mechanistically direct question in the whole list, which is what makes the answer interesting. If excess α-adrenergic tone is holding the erectile smooth muscle contracted, then blocking α-adrenoceptors should release it.1

    The authors of that theory draw exactly this conclusion and act on it. For patients whose trigger is localized to the end organ, they propose downregulating sympathetic drive using analgesics, anti-inflammatory agents, oral α-blocking agents — naming doxazosin and tamsulosin — and low-intensity shockwave therapy.1 So if you have been prescribed an α-blocker for this, it is not an arbitrary choice; it follows directly from the proposed mechanism.

    What does not exist is any published account of how well it works. Systemic α-adrenergic blockade does not appear among the interventions cataloged in the systematic review,2 and no case series or cohort has reported outcomes. Patients describe mixed results — some improve, others do not — which is exactly the kind of observation that would be worth collecting properly and nobody has.

    Two explanations could account for a partial response, and both are testable. Part of the contractile signal may not be adrenergic at all: sympathetic nerves co-release neuropeptide Y, which is present in human penile tissue and produces constriction that characteristically resists α-blockade.32 Alternatively the contraction may be sustained downstream of the receptor entirely, by RhoA/Rho-kinase raising the tissue’s sensitivity to whatever calcium is present — a pathway that helps maintain normal flaccidity and is not reversed by blocking α-receptors.33 Either would explain why the most obvious drug for the proposed mechanism works only sometimes. Hypothesis

    Is there any medication made specifically for HFS?

    No. Every drug used in this condition is borrowed from another indication — PDE5 inhibitors from erectile dysfunction, gabapentinoids from neuropathic pain, muscle relaxants and anxiolytics from elsewhere again.2 Nothing has been developed for HFS, nothing is licensed for it, and no controlled trial of any agent has been published.2 That is what a condition with a literature this thin looks like pharmacologically.

    What about testosterone?

    Hormonal studies are described as normal in this condition,5 and in the published multimodal case, testosterone, prolactin and thyroid function were all normal.6 There is no basis for treating a hormone level that is not abnormal, and no evidence that testosterone affects the proposed mechanism. If testing shows genuinely low testosterone, that is a separate condition worth addressing on its own terms — not a treatment for HFS.

    What about acupuncture?

    There is no evidence for acupuncture as a treatment for HFS, and there is a specific reason for caution. Thread-embedding acupuncture therapy appears in the systematic review among the reported causes of hard flaccid syndrome.2 That does not make all acupuncture harmful, but any procedure involving needling or implantation in the genital or pelvic region deserves a higher bar in a condition thought to begin with injury to those structures.

    Does marijuana or CBD help?

    No evidence supports either, and marijuana use appears in the review literature among the described triggers for HFS.5 That is a single item in a list of reported precipitants rather than a demonstrated causal link, and it should not be over-read. But it is a poor basis on which to recommend the same substance as a treatment.

    Should I try stem cells or platelet-rich plasma?

    No published evidence supports either in HFS, and there is a structural reason to be skeptical. Regenerative therapies aim to repair damaged tissue. The largest cohort found normal hemodynamics and no fibrosis — which is to say, no gross structural damage to repair.21 If the problem is neural drive or smooth muscle tone rather than tissue loss, regenerating tissue does not address it. These treatments are also marketed aggressively and priced accordingly, which is worth weighing when the evidence base is empty.

    Does shockwave therapy help?

    The evidence is weak. Low-intensity shockwave therapy scored 2.0 ± 2.4 out of 10 in the patient survey, among the lowest of any option.3 It appears as one component of a successful three-treatment case report, which cannot attribute the outcome to any single element.6 The therapy targets vascular tissue, and the largest cohort found vascular function already normal.21

    What about nerve blocks?

    Results reported by patients are mixed and no controlled data exist. There is a methodological point that matters more than it sounds: a sympathetic block is only interpretable if it is confirmed to have worked, normally by a documented rise in skin temperature in the corresponding territory. A block that did not achieve blockade tells you nothing about physiology. Nerve blocks scored 1.9 ± 1.5 out of 10 in the patient survey, the lowest of any listed intervention.3

    What about ketamine?

    There is no published study of ketamine in HFS. There is a coherent mechanistic rationale, which is why the question keeps arising: ketamine is an NMDA receptor antagonist, and central sensitization — the process that might explain why HFS persists after the injury heals — is NMDA dependent.20 A rationale is not evidence. Ketamine also has pronounced psychoactive effects, which makes any self-reported improvement in a symptom assessed by touch and appearance particularly difficult to evaluate.

    What should I try if nothing reliably works?

    This is the question the evidence is least able to answer, and it deserves a direct admission rather than a list. No controlled trial of any intervention has been published,2 so there is no evidence-based sequence to follow. What to try is a judgment made with a clinician who has examined you. It is not something this or any article can settle.

    What can be offered is the reasoning clinicians appear to use. The approach described consistently across sources is multimodal and individualized, addressing physical and psychological components together rather than choosing between them.2 Among options that have been rated by patients, PDE5 inhibitors scored highest at 4.8 ± 2.6 out of 10 and pelvic floor physical therapy second at 3.5 ± 2.4.3 Neither is curative.

    Three principles are defensible when evidence is this thin. Prefer reversible interventions to irreversible ones, because a wrong reversible decision can be undone and a wrong surgical one cannot. Avoid anything that appears in the literature as a trigger for this condition, which includes vacuum devices and penile stretching.5 And change one thing at a time, because trying four things at once guarantees you will not learn which, if any, helped — the same problem that makes the multimodal case reports uninterpretable.

    Key points

    • Pelvic floor therapy addresses a real component but treats the wrong tissue to resolve the defining sign.
    • Clinicians recommend it because it is low-risk and nothing better exists — the error is presenting it as a cure.
    • Abstinence does not repair nerve injury; avoiding aggravating activity is still reasonable.
    • PDE5 inhibitors are the best-rated option and are not curative.
    • With no controlled trials, prefer reversible over irreversible, avoid known triggers, and change one thing at a time.

    6. Surgery and devices

    Does pudendal nerve decompression surgery cure HFS?

    Reported outcomes have been poor, and there is a mechanistic reason to expect that. If the condition is maintained centrally — by a sensitized spinal reflex — or by the effector tissue itself having become autonomously hypercontractile, then releasing pressure at the original injury site removes nothing that is still driving the problem.1,20 No controlled study of decompression in HFS exists. Surgery on a nerve is irreversible and carries its own risk of injury, which raises the standard of evidence that ought to be met before undergoing it.

    Does back surgery cure HFS?

    One case is described in the systematic review in which HFS secondary to lumbar disc protrusion was managed by discectomy after sex therapy and pelvic floor physical therapy had failed.2 That is one patient. Set against it: annular tears are common in asymptomatic people, the 76% figure comes from an uncontrolled congress abstract of 21 patients,10 and spinal surgery is not reversible. A finding on a scan is not by itself a reason to operate, particularly when the same finding appears in a substantial share of people with no symptoms at all.

    Should I get a penile implant?

    Nothing in the literature supports penile implantation for HFS, and there are specific reasons for caution. An implant is an irreversible procedure that involves removing erectile tissue; it treats inability to achieve rigidity. The defining problem in HFS is abnormal resting tone in that same tissue, together with altered sensation and often pain — none of which an implant addresses. Sensory symptoms and dysesthesia would not be expected to improve, and no data exist on how a sensitized system responds to implantation. This is a decision requiring a specialist assessment, and the honest position is that the evidence base for it in this condition is empty.

    Should I do vacuum pump therapy?

    This one has a clear answer available. Vacuum device use appears in the review literature as a described trigger for hard flaccid syndrome.5 Using a device listed among the causes of a condition as a treatment for that same condition requires a strong justification, and none exists. There is no published evidence for vacuum therapy in HFS.

    Key points

    • Pudendal decompression has reportedly failed and is mechanistically unlikely to help if maintenance is central.
    • One discectomy case exists; a scan finding common in healthy people is not by itself grounds for surgery.
    • No evidence supports penile implantation in HFS, and it does not address the defining problem.
    • Vacuum devices appear in the literature as a trigger, not a treatment.

    7. The mental health questions

    Will fixing my mental health cure HFS?

    Unlikely on its own, and the framing contains a trap. If distress is a consequence rather than a cause, then resolving it will not remove the physical mechanism. If distress genuinely does raise sympathetic tone, addressing it may reduce symptom intensity without addressing the underlying lesion. Cognitive therapy scored 2.9 ± 2.4 out of 10 among surveyed patients.3 The trap is that failing to improve psychologically then gets read as personal failure, when the evidence never supported the premise.

    Is treating anxiety worth doing at all?

    On its own merits, yes. Psychological distress affects roughly nine in ten patients,3 and the case literature documents symptoms ranging from mild anxiety to severe depression.4 That is worth treating because distress is worth treating, independently of whether it changes the penile symptoms. The reported management approaches consistently address physical and psychological components together rather than choosing between them.2

    8. Sex, daily life and living with it

    Can I still have sex?

    Nothing in the literature indicates that sexual activity causes lasting harm in HFS. What is documented is that it often makes symptoms temporarily worse: masturbation worsened symptoms in 75.9% of surveyed patients,3 and both arousal and ejaculation involve substantial sympathetic activation, which under the working model is the signal driving the contracted state. Erectile difficulty is reported by 74.1%, so the practical obstacle is often mechanical rather than a matter of permission.3

    No study has examined whether continuing or avoiding sexual activity affects the long-term course. The distinction worth holding is between an activity that provokes symptoms temporarily and one that causes damage — the evidence supports the first and says nothing about the second.

    Will this affect my fertility?

    Nothing in the HFS literature reports an association with infertility. The mechanisms are separate: sperm production occurs in the testes and semen transport during ejaculation is driven by sympathetic activity acting on the vas deferens, seminal vesicles and prostate — none of which is the cavernosal smooth muscle implicated in the hard flaccid state. Painful ejaculation is documented in this condition,5 which is a different problem from impaired fertility.

    That said, no study has measured fertility outcomes in HFS patients, so the honest position is absence of reported association rather than demonstrated safety. If this is a specific concern, semen analysis answers it directly and is a straightforward test to request.

    Should I avoid cycling or sitting for long periods?

    No study has examined either in HFS, so this is reasoning rather than evidence. Blunt perineal trauma is a described route to pudendal nerve injury,1 and cycling loads the perineum directly, which is a reasonable basis for caution in a condition thought to begin with injury to those structures. Prolonged sitting has no equivalent documented link. What is documented is positional variation in general — 73.0% improve lying down and 64.5% worsen standing3 — which suggests position matters to symptom intensity without telling you that any particular position is harmful.

    Am I making it worse by checking it and reading about it constantly?

    No study has examined this in HFS, so nobody can tell you that checking harms you — and nobody should imply that your symptoms are produced by paying attention to them. The physical findings are real and the mechanism proposed for them is physical.1

    What can be said is narrower. Psychological distress affects 89.5% of patients,3 coping was one of four dominant themes patients raised with each other,30 and a review of online support groups across 24 chronic conditions found a possible negative effect on anxiety, particularly on exposure to other members’ difficult experiences.28 Reading about worst cases has a documented cost even where it has benefits. Whether that cost extends to the physical symptom is unknown.

    Key points

    • Sexual activity commonly provokes symptoms temporarily; nothing indicates it causes lasting harm.
    • No association with infertility is reported; semen analysis answers the question directly if it is a concern.
    • Perineal loading such as cycling is worth caution on mechanistic grounds, not on evidence.
    • Nothing supports the idea that attention produces the symptoms; exposure to others’ worst outcomes has a documented cost to anxiety.

    9. About Irwin Goldstein and his theory

    Is Goldstein a scammer?

    No, and the question conflates two separate things worth separating.

    On standing: Irwin Goldstein is a sexual medicine physician who directs a private subspecialty practice, San Diego Sexual Medicine. He is a lead author of the 2021 International Society for the Study of Women’s Sexual Health consensus on persistent genital arousal disorder and genito-pelvic dysesthesia24 and of the 2026 recommendations from the Fifth International Consultation on Sexual Medicine.9 Those are documents produced by international expert panels, not self-published material.

    On cost: private subspecialty care in the United States is expensive, sexual medicine is frequently poorly covered by insurance, and a thorough workup for a condition with no diagnostic test takes considerable clinician time. Those facts explain high fees without implying anything about honesty. Charging a lot for scarce specialist care is not the same as selling something that does not work.

    The legitimate criticism is entirely separate and belongs to the evidence, not the person: the reflex proposal was published in a professional society news publication rather than as peer-reviewed research, and it reports no sample size, protocol, control or objective measure.1 That is a criticism of how a hypothesis was supported. It says nothing about the motives of the person who proposed it.

    Is Goldstein right about HFS?

    Partly unanswerable at present, which is not the same as wrong.

    What is in the theory’s favor: it accounts for the defining sign, the erectile difficulty and the coldness through a single mechanism grounded in established physiology, since sympathetic outflow is what maintains normal flaccidity in the first place.11,15 It explains why examination and imaging are normal.5,21 It gave a condition with no diagnostic framework a structured one to borrow.9,24 And it generated a testable prediction about lumbosacral pathology that someone went and tested.10 Those are the marks of a productive hypothesis.

    The main evidence offered is the response to intracavernosal phentolamine, and it is worth understanding rather than dismissing. Phentolamine blocks α-adrenoceptors. If a man with HFS gets a full erection from it, then the relaxation machinery, the arterial inflow and the blood-trapping mechanism are all demonstrably working25 — which eliminates fibrosis, vascular disease and structural damage as explanations. Since cavernosal tone is set by the autonomic system and the relaxation arm works, the fault must be on the contraction side.11,15 That is a sound deduction, and the 88-man hemodynamic cohort reaches the same conclusion by a different route.21

    What is against it is narrower and more specific. The reflex itself has never been demonstrated, sympathetic outflow to the penis has never been measured in a patient, and the injured structure has never been found by imaging or histology.2 And phentolamine acts at the receptor — the last step in the chain — so it cannot distinguish excess noradrenaline released by a hyperactive reflex from receptor upregulation, impaired reuptake, or a state maintained locally in the tissue. All of those respond to α-blockade identically. The observation supports “the problem is α-adrenergic contraction.” It does not support “a pelvic/pudendal-hypogastric reflex is causing it,” and the reflex is what the theory is about.

    The reasonable position: it is the best available explanation and it is provisional. Both of those are true at once, and treating the best available theory as settled is how fields stop making progress.

    Key points

    • Goldstein’s standing in sexual medicine is established through international consensus panels; high private specialist fees are not evidence of bad faith.
    • The legitimate criticism concerns how the hypothesis was evidenced, not the person.
    • The reflex model is coherent, productive and unproven; its central claims have never been measured in a patient.

    10. Practical questions

    What kind of doctor should I see?

    A physician specializing in sexual medicine is the most likely to have encountered this, which is why the practitioner survey was conducted at a sexual medicine plenary.23 A general urologist is the usual first stop and a reasonable one, since the conditions that must be excluded — Peyronie’s disease, priapism, vascular erectile dysfunction — are urological.

    Beyond that, the reflex model has a practical implication that is easy to miss: if the problem can be triggered at five different anatomical levels, then different patients need different specialists.1,9 Dominant pain and sensory symptoms point toward a pain specialist or neurologist. Back or radicular symptoms point toward a spine specialist. Pelvic floor and urinary symptoms point toward a pelvic floor physical therapist alongside the medical assessment. That is not a recommendation to see all of them; it is the logic behind why one specialty may be unable to help you and another may.

    What should I tell my doctor?

    Two things help more than anything else. See the right kind of clinician. A physician specializing in sexual medicine is the most likely to have encountered this. Even then, expect limits: in a survey of 36 attendees at a 2023 American Urological Association sexual medicine plenary, 64% had seen a patient with HFS and only 52% considered it a legitimate medical condition.23 That sample was drawn from a subspecialty session, so it probably overstates familiarity in general practice rather than understating it.

    Bring the literature. It is short enough to print. The systematic review2 and the original case series4 together give a clinician the clinical picture and the state of the evidence in two documents. This is an unusual dynamic — patients arriving better-read than the clinician they are seeing — and it is easier to manage when both sides expect it. Bringing a paper is not a challenge to anyone’s competence; it is a reasonable response to a condition this thinly studied.

    Arrive with a written history covering onset, the defining sign, morning and nocturnal erections, sensation, pain, urinary and bowel function, what makes symptoms better and worse, everything already tried, back and leg symptoms, and the timing of mood changes relative to physical ones. That last item addresses a question the literature has never answered.

    What tests should I get done?

    That is a decision for a clinician who has examined you, and no test confirms HFS — the systematic review is explicit that no objective test assists diagnosis.2 What can be described is what reported workups have included: history and physical examination, a baseline hormonal profile, penile color Doppler ultrasound, validated questionnaires including the IIEF-5, IPSS, HADS and NIH-CPSI, and lumbar magnetic resonance imaging where back pain or radicular symptoms are present.2

    Understand what those tests are for. They exclude conditions with specific treatments; they do not confirm this one. Expect them to come back normal — examination, imaging, Doppler and hormonal studies are described as normal in this condition,5 peak systolic velocity was normal in 98% of reported cases,2 and the 88-man cohort had normal hemodynamics throughout.21 A normal result is a finding, not a failure.

    My doctor has never heard of it. Does that mean it isn’t real?

    No. It means the condition first appeared in the literature in 20207 and that its whole literature comes to about twenty items, eight of which met the inclusion criteria of a 2025 systematic review.2 A clinician who has not encountered a condition documented that thinly is not being negligent. Unfamiliarity is a fact about the state of the field, not a verdict on whether you are unwell.

    Should I believe what people say about HFS on the internet?

    Treat it as testimony, not evidence, and the distinction matters more here than in most conditions. Almost everything written about HFS online is an anecdote from an anonymous person whose diagnosis cannot be verified — because no diagnostic criteria exist to verify it against.2 There is no objective measure of the hard flaccid state, so claims of improvement mean whatever the person writing them means. And nobody knows the natural history, so no outcome can be attributed to whatever the person was doing at the time.

    This is not speculation about online communities. A scoping review of 14 articles drawn from 3,136 sources found more evidence of poor-quality information and misinformation in online peer support groups than of good-quality information, while also noting that members actively corrected false claims in subsequent posts.27 A mixed-studies review of 100 papers across 24 chronic conditions found consistent benefits for social wellbeing and coping alongside a possible negative effect on anxiety, particularly on exposure to other members’ difficult experiences.28 Both halves are real. Punjani and Mulhall make the same observation about HFS specifically: patient communities provide support while distributing unvetted information.7

    Who do I trust when doctors can’t help and all I have is other patients?

    A workable rule: trust a claim in proportion to what supports it, and be most careful with the confident ones. In a condition with eight published studies,2 certainty is the least reliable signal available. Someone who tells you they know what causes HFS, or that a specific protocol cures it, is claiming more than the entire literature does.

    Patient communities are genuinely good at some things and poor at others, and sorting which is which helps. They are good at practical questions — who to see, what to expect from a procedure, how to describe symptoms — at coping, and at reducing isolation.28 The forum analysis found exactly these functions: patients seeking diagnostic guidance, causal explanation, treatment support and coping strategies from each other,30 which is what forms when no service exists. They are poor as a source of evidence about what works, for the reasons above.

    On clinicians: a doctor who says “I don’t know, and here is what we can exclude” is being more honest than one who is certain, given that only 52% of surveyed specialists regarded the condition as legitimate at all.23 Uncertainty voiced plainly is a sign of competence here, not a lack of it.

    Should I tell my friends and family?

    No study addresses this and it is genuinely personal, so what follows is not a recommendation. Two facts are worth having in view. Psychological distress is reported by 89.5% of patients,3 and coping was one of four dominant themes patients raised with each other.30 Carrying a condition like this without anyone knowing is its own load, separate from the symptoms.

    If you do tell someone, the practical difficulty is usually that they have no framework for it. Handing over something written is often easier than explaining it, and it lets the other person read at their own pace. The systematic review2 works for that purpose, and so does this page — the sections on what causes it and whether it can be cured cover most of what someone close to you would want to know.

    Is there anything I should do immediately?

    The one thing supported across every source is to stop whatever produced the injury — aggressive masturbation, stretching or elongation practices, vacuum devices.4,5 Beyond that, no intervention has been shown to work in a controlled setting.2 Get assessed properly so that the conditions with specific treatments are excluded first.

    Seek emergency care for a rigid painful erection lasting more than four hours, inability to pass urine, sudden severe pain with swelling and bruising, or new saddle numbness, leg weakness, or loss of bladder or bowel control. These indicate conditions distinct from HFS that need immediate attention.

    Key points

    • A sexual medicine specialist is the most likely clinician to know the condition, and even then familiarity is limited.
    • Which specialist helps depends on which symptoms dominate, because the proposed mechanism can start at several anatomical levels.
    • Tests exclude other conditions rather than confirm this one; expect normal results.
    • Bringing the literature is reasonable and short enough to be practical.
    • Online accounts are testimony, not evidence — no criteria to verify a diagnosis, no objective outcome measure, no known natural history.
    • Trust claims in proportion to their support; certainty is the least reliable signal in a field this small.
    • Stopping the provoking activity is the one step supported everywhere.
