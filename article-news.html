    8 August 2026

    This is the day the site was published. From here it will be updated incrementally, as new work on hard flaccid syndrome appears and as my own understanding of it changes, and I intend to review the whole site at least once a year whether or not anything new has come out. If I have anything of my own to report about the condition, I will note it here and on my case report.

    Some recent developments:

    In recorded interviews, Irwin Goldstein has described developing a warming gel applied directly to the penis as a treatment, and using thermal imaging — a forward-looking infrared (FLIR) camera — as a diagnostic aid, on the basis that the hard flaccid penis runs cold.1,2 Neither the gel nor any trial of it has been published.

    The thermal imaging has now been reported, as a conference abstract. Koontz and colleagues, including Goldstein, took 250 thermal images from 12 men with HFS (mean age 36.4 ± 11.1) and 38 without (mean age 49.4 ± 16.8), measuring the glans and mid-shaft supine in both the flaccid and erect states.3 One comparison reached significance: the flaccid glans, at 88.2°F (31.2°C) in the HFS group against 91.4°F (33.0°C) in the controls (p = 0.0003). Flaccid mid-shaft was 94.0°F against 94.6°F, and the erect measurements differed little. In five patients who were treated, flaccid glans temperature rose from 86.6°F to 89.9°F. The authors conclude that FLIR offers an objective physiological measure for both diagnosis and treatment monitoring.

    The usual cautions apply. This is a congress abstract rather than a full paper, the comparison is 12 patients against 38 controls who were thirteen years older on average, and only one of the four measurements separated the groups. The control figure is at least plausible: 91.4°F sits close to an independent baseline of 33.25°C (91.8°F) recorded in nine healthy men under bedding.4 Thermal imaging is not new to urology either, having been examined in erectile dysfunction and Peyronie’s disease.5 What makes this worth watching is narrower and more important: it is the first objective measurement anyone has proposed for this condition, in a literature where every other reported outcome is self-assessed.

    Separately, a preprint from Mendias and Awan reports a retrospective case series of 32 men given a nine-part rehabilitation protocol: class IV laser, dry needling, radial pressure wave shockwave therapy, therapeutic ultrasound, biofeedback, manual therapy, therapeutic exercise, behavioral coaching and oral tadalafil.6 Median Erection Hardness Scale rose from 2 to 4 and median penile satisfaction from 2 to 5 (both p < 0.01), with all 32 reaching an EHS of 3 or more by the end against 8 of 32 at baseline. Comorbidities were common: low back pain in 53%, hip or groin pain in 38%, pelvic floor pain in 31%, urinary symptoms in 28%. The authors read this as support for a model in which musculoskeletal and end-organ problems start the syndrome and the nervous system amplifies it.

    Three things to hold in mind before that result changes anyone’s plans. It is a preprint, so it has not been peer-reviewed. It is retrospective and uncontrolled, with unblinded patient-reported outcomes collected at the clinic that delivered the treatment. And nine interventions were given at once, which makes it impossible to say which, if any, did the work. The outcome measures are also worth reading closely: the Erection Hardness Scale measures erections, and none of the instruments used measures the flaccid rigidity that defines the condition. What the series shows is improvement in the symptoms that accompany HFS. Whether the hard flaccid state itself changed is not something the data can answer. That is still the largest treated cohort anyone has described, and by some distance.
