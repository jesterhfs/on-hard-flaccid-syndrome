Mechanism · Article 06

The pelvic/pudendal-hypogastric reflex

The prevailing theory set out in full, then an honest assessment of the evidence for it.

37 min read · 14 references

    The previous article assembled the parts: smooth muscle that can hold contraction indefinitely, sympathetic outflow arriving through the hypogastric nerve that keeps it contracted, somatic sensation leaving the penis by a separate route through the pudendal nerve, and the established principle that somatic sensory input can reflexively alter autonomic output. Those four facts describe a route. This article is about the claim that the route is taken.

    The claim belongs to Irwin Goldstein and Barry Komisaruk, and it is currently the most developed explanation of HFS in circulation. It is also, as will become clear, supported by considerably less evidence than its prominence suggests.1 Both things should be held at once. A theory can be the best available and still be provisional, and treating the best available theory as settled is how fields stop making progress.

    1. The claim Hypothesis

    Goldstein and Komisaruk propose that hard flaccid syndrome is a consequence of pathological activation of a somato-visceral and/or viscero-visceral reflex, which they name the pelvic/pudendal-hypogastric reflex.1 In their account, HFS is an acquired, persistent, painful and bothersome sexual dysfunction in which the penis is observed in an unwanted persistent semi-rigid flaccid state.1

    The mechanism, stated compactly: afferent input arising in pudendal or pelvic nerve territory drives excessive sympathetic activity through the hypogastric nerve to the erectile smooth muscle, which remains contracted.1 The associated pain is proposed to be a form of genito-pelvic dysesthesia produced by the same pathological reflex activation rather than by ongoing tissue damage.1,3

    Read against the previous article, every step uses established components. Somatic afferent stimulation is known to evoke reflex changes in sympathetic efferent activity and thereby in organ function, demonstrated across multiple organ systems.13 Sympathetic outflow through the hypogastric nerve is what maintains cavernosal smooth muscle in tonic contraction and keeps the penis flaccid.9,10 Smooth muscle can sustain contraction for long periods at low metabolic cost.10 The theory assembles known pieces into an unknown arrangement. That is a legitimate way to build a hypothesis, and it is not the same as demonstrating one.

    Key points

    • The proposal: afferent input pathologically activates a reflex that drives sustained sympathetic outflow to erectile smooth muscle.
    • Pain is attributed to the same reflex activity rather than to continuing tissue injury.
    • Every component is individually established; the specific arrangement is not.

    2. The proposed sequence, step by step Hypothesis

    Set out as stages, with the evidential status of each marked, the proposal looks like this.

    Table 1 · The proposed chain, stage by stage
    Stage What is proposed Status
    1 · Injury Mechanical loading of the erect or semi-erect penis injures tissue in somatic sensory territory Trauma precedes onset in 58.0% of surveyed patients6; the specific injured structure has never been identified Speculative
    2 · Afferent signal Injured or irritated tissue generates sustained afferent traffic in pudendal or pelvic territory Not measured in any HFS patient Speculative
    3 · Reflex Spinal circuits convert that afferent input into increased sympathetic efferent output Somato-visceral reflexes are established physiology13; this particular reflex is named but not demonstrated Hypothesis
    4 · Efferent limb Excess sympathetic outflow travels the hypogastric nerve to the erectile tissue The pathway is established anatomy9; excess flow in HFS has never been measured Hypothesis
    5 · End organ Noradrenaline at α-adrenoceptors holds cavernosal smooth muscle contracted Established mechanism of normal flaccidity9,10 Established
    6 · Presentation Persistent semi-rigid flaccid state, impaired erection, coldness, dysesthesia Matches the documented symptom picture5,6,8 Established

    The pattern is worth noting. The two ends of the chain are well supported. The middle — the part that makes it a theory rather than a description — is where measurement is absent.

    Key points

    • Stages 5 and 6 rest on established physiology and documented symptoms.
    • Stages 2, 3 and 4 have never been measured in a patient with HFS.
    • The injured structure proposed at stage 1 has not been identified by imaging or histology.

    3. Five places it can start Hypothesis

    The theory’s distinguishing feature is that the reflex is proposed to be activatable at any of five anatomical levels, which reframes HFS as a final common pathway rather than a single disease.1 The same five-region framework appears in the consensus documents on PGAD/GPD as a diagnostic algorithm.2,3

    Table 2 · The five proposed trigger regions
    RegionExample pathology cited
    1 · End organ (penis)Injury to the erect penis during intercourse or masturbation1
    2 · Pelvis and perineumPudendal neuropathy following blunt perineal trauma; high-tone pelvic floor dysfunction1
    3 · Cauda equinaLumbosacral annular tear; sacral Tarlov cyst1
    4 · Spinal cordNot specified in the available source material
    5 · BrainNot specified in the available source material

    Regions as described in the originating commentary and mirrored in the PGAD/GPD diagnostic algorithm.1,2,3

    This is the most clinically useful part of the theory. It predicts that two men with identical symptoms may require entirely different investigation, and it explains why a patient whose trigger sits at region three might not improve with treatment directed at region one. It also creates a methodological problem, addressed in section 7.

    Key points

    • The reflex is proposed to be triggerable at end organ, pelvis/perineum, cauda equina, spinal cord or brain.
    • This reframes HFS as a final common pathway rather than one disease.
    • Two of the five regions are not specified in the available source material.

    No study has measured sympathetic outflow to the penis in a patient with HFS. No imaging, electrodiagnostic or histological study has identified the proposed injury. No trial has tested a treatment derived from the theory against a control.5 The systematic review that surveyed this field concluded that no objective test assists diagnosis and that etiology remains unclear.5

    Key points

    • The evidential basis is one confounded pharmacological observation, one small uncontrolled imaging study reported as an abstract, one case, and physiological plausibility.
    • Penile sympathetic outflow has never been measured in HFS.
    • No treatment derived from the theory has been tested against a control.

    5. HFS as a form of PGAD/GPD Consensus

    The second element of the current position is nosological: where HFS belongs among other conditions. Understanding it requires a short detour, because the parent category is unfamiliar to most readers and its name is misleading on first encounter.

    5.1 What PGAD/GPD is

    Persistent genital arousal disorder (PGAD) describes unwanted, unremitting sensations of genital arousal accompanied by significant distress.2 The word arousal is doing unusual work here. It refers to physical genital sensation occurring without desire — not to sexual interest, and not to anything the person wants. In a 2021 consensus document produced by a panel using a modified Delphi method, the International Society for the Study of Women’s Sexual Health broadened the nomenclature to add genito-pelvic dysesthesia (GPD), on the basis that patients’ symptoms extend beyond arousal sensations alone.2 Dysesthesia means an unpleasant abnormal sensation. The combined term PGAD/GPD covers both.

    That same document set out the five-region diagnostic algorithm — end organ, pelvis/perineum, cauda equina, spinal cord, brain — used to localize the source of symptoms.2 The five regions in the HFS theory are not a separate invention; they are this algorithm applied to a new presentation.

    5.2 The extension to HFS

    The Fifth International Consultation on Sexual Medicine recommendations, published in 2026, extend PGAD/GPD to all genders and describe it as a sensory hyperactivity condition involving a broad range of undesired, unrelenting dysesthesias — explicitly including hard flaccid syndrome, alongside genital arousal and sleep-related prolonged erection.3 The document proposes a diagnostic algorithm examining the same five anatomical regions to identify dysesthesia triggers, with concurrent psychological and medical intervention.3

    5.3 What the reclassification means in practice

    Three consequences follow, and they are the reason this is more than a labeling exercise.

    • It supplies an assessment framework where none existed. HFS has no diagnostic criteria of its own.5 Placement within PGAD/GPD imports a structured algorithm developed for a related problem.2,3
    • It reframes symptoms as sensory rather than structural. If the pain and abnormal sensations are dysesthesias generated by reflex activity, then normal imaging is exactly what should be expected, and continued searching for structural damage in the penis is misdirected.1,3
    • It changes who investigates. A cauda equina or spinal trigger implies imaging and specialties that a purely genital framing would never involve.2,4

    Key points

    • PGAD/GPD covers unwanted persistent genital arousal sensations and unpleasant abnormal genital sensation, with distress.
    • The 2021 ISSWSH consensus introduced the combined term and the five-region algorithm; the 2026 ICSM recommendations extend it to all genders and name HFS within it.
    • The practical effect is to import a diagnostic framework, reframe symptoms as sensory, and widen which specialties are involved.

    6. The phentolamine argument

    This is the observation most often cited as direct support for the theory, and the one the patient community argues about. It is also the part of the argument most often handled badly by both sides. Dismissing it as “a drug that causes erections caused an erection” underrates it considerably. Treating it as proof of the reflex overrates it by about the same margin. The useful work is in establishing precisely how much it shows, because the answer is: more than critics allow, and less than the theory needs.

    6.1 What is claimed

    The originating commentary states that intracavernosal injection of phentolamine temporarily resolves the hard flaccid state and induces penile erection, and offers this as support for the hypothesis that HFS results from excessive sympathetic activity producing unrelenting contraction of erectile smooth muscle.1 The logic is direct. If excess α-adrenergic tone holds the tissue contracted, then blocking α-adrenoceptors should release it.

    6.2 What phentolamine actually does

    Phentolamine is a non-selective α-adrenergic receptor antagonist. In isolated cavernosal tissue studied in organ baths, α-adrenergic blockade by phentolamine suppresses contractile responses and enhances relaxation induced by sildenafil, vasoactive intestinal polypeptide and prostaglandin E1, producing synergistic relaxation greater than single agents alone.11 It is a standard component of intracavernosal pharmacotherapy for erectile dysfunction.

    It is also an erectogenic drug in men who do not have HFS. An open-label study of more than 2,000 men with mild to moderate erectile dysfunction reported that oral phentolamine mesylate increased Erectile Function Domain scores on the International Index of Erectile Function, increased rates of successful vaginal penetration, and increased treatment satisfaction, with an adverse event profile consistent with its known pharmacodynamics.12 That is what the drug is for.

    6.3 The deduction, taken seriously

    The common dismissal is that phentolamine produces erections in anyone, so producing one in an HFS patient shows nothing. That objection is weaker than it looks, and following the reasoning properly is worth doing, because the argument being made is not “the drug worked, therefore the theory is right.” It is an argument by elimination about where in the system the fault can possibly sit.

    Start from what is not in dispute. Cavernosal smooth muscle tone is set by autonomic input. Sympathetic outflow releases noradrenaline onto α-adrenoceptors and contracts the tissue, which is what maintains normal flaccidity. Parasympathetic activity drives nitric oxide release, raises cyclic GMP and relaxes it, which is what permits erection.9,10 That is textbook physiology, not hypothesis. It has a consequence that is easy to skip past: whatever is wrong with the resting tone of that tissue is necessarily a problem of autonomic control, of the receptors and machinery the autonomic signal acts on, or of the tissue itself. There is no fourth option. The organ has no voluntary innervation and no other mechanism for setting its own tone.

    Now add what the injection shows. If blocking α-adrenoceptors produces a full erection in a man with HFS, then everything downstream of that blockade is demonstrably working. The smooth muscle is capable of complete relaxation. Nitric oxide and cyclic GMP signaling is intact. Arterial inflow is sufficient. The veno-occlusive mechanism traps blood as it should.9 The tissue is not fibrotic, not mechanically locked, and not structurally incapable of doing what it is being asked to do.

    That eliminates a large class of alternatives. Structural damage, fibrosis, arterial insufficiency, veno-occlusive failure, an intrinsic inability of the smooth muscle to relax — all are ruled out at the moment of testing, because a penis with any of those problems does not become fully erect on α-blockade.

    And then the conclusion follows. The patient has an abnormal resting state; that is the presenting sign and is not in question. If the relaxation apparatus is intact, and the tissue can be shown to relax completely once α-adrenergic input is removed, then what was holding it contracted was α-adrenergic input. The fault is on the contraction side. Given the physiology in the first step, that means the fault is in sympathetic control of the tissue. As a localization argument this is sound, and this article was previously too quick to wave it away.

    It does not stand alone, either. The largest cohort assessed in this condition — 88 men — showed normal peak systolic velocity in all of them, abnormal end-diastolic velocity in only 2 of 88, and no elevated echogenicity on B-mode scanning.14 That is an entirely independent method reaching the same conclusion the phentolamine response points to: the hardware is intact, and the problem is one of control rather than structure. Two different lines of evidence converging on the same localization is not nothing.

    6.4 What the deduction does not establish

    Two things survive, and they are narrower and more specific than a general charge of confounding.

    It does not identify which link in the contraction chain is faulty. This is the serious one. Grant everything above — the fault is α-adrenergically mediated contraction. Phentolamine acts at the adrenoceptor on the smooth muscle cell, which is the last step in the proposed chain.10,11 At least four different problems would produce an identical response to it: excess noradrenaline released by a pathologically active reflex, which is Goldstein and Komisaruk’s specific claim; normal noradrenaline acting on upregulated receptors after partial denervation; impaired local reuptake raising the concentration reaching the receptor; or a state maintained locally within the effector tissue. All four are α-adrenergic. All four are relieved by α-blockade. The observation therefore supports “the problem is α-adrenergic contraction” — a real and useful conclusion — without supporting “a pelvic/pudendal-hypogastric reflex is generating it.” Stages 1 through 4 of Table 1 are bypassed entirely, and the reflex is precisely the part that remains unevidenced.

    It does not measure the magnitude of the tone. Every normal flaccid penis is held flaccid by α-adrenergic tone, and blocking it produces erection in men without HFS too.11,12 The response therefore shows that α-adrenergic tone is present and can be overcome, not that it is abnormally high. That distinction matters less than it first appears — the patient’s abnormal resting state already establishes that something is wrong — but it matters for anyone wanting to quantify the abnormality rather than locate it.

    The endpoint question that the patient community argues about survives too, though for a narrower reason than usually given. Erection tells you the relaxation arm works. Return to a normal soft flaccid state would tell you something more specific and more directly relevant: that pathological resting tone itself had normalized. Both outcomes are informative. The second is the one the theory actually predicts, and the published account does not report it.

    Reporting standard. Separately from all of the above: no sample size, dose, protocol, control condition, blinding or objective measurement is reported, and the claim appears in a professional society news publication rather than peer-reviewed primary research.1 The 2025 systematic review did not identify a study establishing it.5 A sound inference reported without method is still a sound inference; it is simply not yet a finding anyone can check.

    The phentolamine response is good evidence that the fault lies in sympathetic control of cavernosal smooth muscle rather than in the structure of the organ. It is not evidence about which part of the sympathetic pathway is at fault — and that is the part the theory is actually about.

    6.5 What a decisive version would look like

    The objection is not that the experiment is worthless. It is that a good inference has not been converted into a measurement, and that doing so is straightforward. The design below follows from the pharmacology; it is reasoning rather than a published protocol. Reasoning

    Table 4 · Turning the observation into evidence
    ProblemWhat would fix it
    No control group Men without HFS receiving identical doses, assessed identically
    Confounded endpoint Define the endpoint as return to normal flaccid state — measured resting tone or compliance — rather than as erection
    Erectogenic dose masks the question Dose-ranging below the erectogenic threshold. If a sub-erectogenic dose normalizes resting tone in HFS but changes nothing in controls, that is a genuine finding
    Subjective assessment Objective measurement, blinded assessors, saline control
    Says nothing about the source Pair with a measure of sympathetic activity, or with regional blockade at a defined level, to locate the driver

    The third row is the crux. A sub-erectogenic dose that normalizes the flaccid state in HFS but not in controls would be difficult to explain other than by abnormal α-adrenergic tone. That single experiment would move the theory substantially.

    Key points

    • The phentolamine response is stronger evidence than critics usually allow: a full erection on α-blockade shows the relaxation apparatus, arterial inflow and veno-occlusion are all intact, which eliminates structural explanations.
    • Since cavernosal tone is set autonomically and the relaxation arm demonstrably works, the fault is on the contraction side. As a localization argument this is sound.
    • The 88-man hemodynamic cohort reaches the same conclusion independently.
    • What it cannot show is which link is faulty. Excess release from a reflex, receptor upregulation, impaired reuptake and a locally maintained state all respond to α-blockade identically — and the reflex is the part the theory is about.
    • Return to a normal soft state, rather than erection, is the outcome the theory predicts and the account does not report it.

    7. Other weaknesses

    The framework may be difficult to falsify. A reflex triggerable at five levels spanning penis to brain can accommodate almost any finding.1,2 That flexibility is clinically useful and epistemically expensive: a model that explains every outcome forbids none, and a model that forbids nothing cannot be tested by observation. The theory would be strengthened by stating what result would count against it.

    Onset without injury is unaccounted for. In the survey of 143 patients, 58.0% reported that symptoms began after a specific incident; 42.0% reported no identifiable trauma.6 The five-region framework can absorb this by locating those cases at another level, but absorbing an observation is not the same as predicting it.

    The treatment implication was made but never reported on. This is worth stating carefully, because it is easy to get wrong in the theory’s favor and equally easy to get wrong against it. The authors did draw the obvious therapeutic conclusion: for patients whose trigger is localized to region one, the end organ, they propose downregulating sympathetic drive with analgesics, anti-inflammatory agents, oral α-blocking agents — doxazosin and tamsulosin are named — and low-intensity shockwave therapy.1 That is exactly what the mechanism predicts, and it is prescribed on that basis. The five-region framework is not merely diagnostic; it assigns different treatments to different levels.

    What is missing is any account of what happened next. Systemic α-adrenergic blockade does not appear among the interventions cataloged in the 2025 systematic review, whose list runs to PDE5 inhibitors, low-intensity shockwave therapy, pelvic floor physical therapy, biopsychosocial management, anxiolytics and antidepressants, and in one case lumbar discectomy.5 No case series, no cohort, not even a report of a handful of patients.

    That is a sharper criticism than the one it replaces. A prediction that nobody has acted on is merely untested. A prediction that is being acted on clinically, in patients, with no published account of the results, is a missed opportunity of a different order — because the outcome data exist somewhere and would be informative either way. If α-blockade reliably relieved the hard flaccid state, that would substantially strengthen the theory. If it usually did not, that would point toward the fault sitting somewhere other than α-adrenergic drive from a reflex. Patients report both outcomes informally. Nobody has written it down.

    Key points

    • A five-region model accommodates any finding, which makes it hard to falsify.
    • The 42% with no inciting event are absorbed rather than predicted.
    • The theory’s most direct therapeutic prediction — oral α-blockade — is prescribed on its authority but has never been reported on in the literature.

    8. Does the PGAD/GPD classification fit? Open question

    One tension deserves stating, because it is not addressed in the material available. PGAD/GPD is characterized as a sensory hyperactivity condition.3 Yet the sensory complaints that dominate HFS are of sensory loss: penile numbness or sensory loss was reported by 74.8% of surveyed patients,6 and the case literature describes numbness and decreased sensitivity, particularly at the glans.7,8

    The two are not incompatible. Sensory loss and abnormal sensation are both reported in HFS, frequently in the same patient,6,8 and the GPD half of the term refers to unpleasant abnormal sensation rather than to heightened normal sensation.2 But the grouping of a predominantly hypoesthetic presentation within a hyperactivity spectrum is a substantive claim, and the justification for it is not spelled out in the consensus material available. It should be read as a working classification rather than a settled one.

    Key points

    • PGAD/GPD is framed as sensory hyperactivity; HFS is dominated by reported sensory loss.
    • The two are compatible but the grouping is not self-evident and is not justified in detail in the available documents.
    • Treat the classification as provisional.

    9. Where this leaves the theory

    It remains the best available explanation, and that judgment is not faint praise. It accounts for the defining sign, the erectile difficulty and the coldness through a single mechanism grounded in established physiology.9,10 It explains why examination and imaging are normal.5,8 It supplies an assessment framework to a condition that has none.2,3 It generated a testable prediction about lumbosacral pathology and someone went and looked.4 Those are the marks of a productive hypothesis.

    It is also, at present, a hypothesis whose central claims have not been measured in a single patient. The reflex has not been demonstrated. Sympathetic outflow to the penis has not been recorded. The injured structure has not been found. The pharmacological observation offered in support does real work — it locates the fault in sympathetic control rather than in the structure of the organ — but it stops exactly where the theory becomes specific, and a properly designed study could carry it further in an afternoon. The systematic review’s conclusion — that consensus criteria, standardized diagnostics and further basic science are needed — remains the accurate summary.5

    For a patient the practical consequence is narrower than it may appear. The theory is a reasonable basis for investigation and a reasonable thing for a clinician to be working from. It is not yet a reason to accept any specific claim about what is happening in your body as established fact.

    What follows from here

    There is a temptation, having found the best available explanation, to stop. The argument against stopping is contained in section 7. A model that can be triggered at five levels spanning penis to brain accommodates almost any finding, its most direct therapeutic prediction has been prescribed without anyone reporting the results, and its central claim — the reflex itself — has never been measured. Those are not reasons to discard it. They are reasons to hold it loosely and to ask what else could produce the same clinical picture.

    One observation in particular refuses to sit quietly. If excess sympathetic outflow travels the hypogastric nerve to the erectile tissue, then blocking that outflow at the superior hypogastric plexus should abolish the hard flaccid state. Reports suggest it usually does not. Take that seriously and it forces a fork: either the pathological traffic runs by a different route than the one assumed, or the effector tissue has stopped needing the traffic at all and is now sustaining the contraction by itself.

    The next article follows that fork. It sets out fourteen conjectures — four locating the fault in the nervous system, four in the smooth muscle itself, the rest in tissue that has already been remodeled — and asks of each what it would predict, what would refute it, and which experiment would settle it. Everything there is speculative and labeled as such. It is written on the assumption that a field this small needs more candidate explanations rather than fewer, provided each is stated precisely enough to be wrong.
