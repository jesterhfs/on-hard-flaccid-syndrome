Is this what I have?

There is no validated test. What exists instead: the defining sign, what has to be excluded first, and the history to bring to an appointment.

27 min read · 17 references

Contents

    Summary

    • Hard flaccid syndrome (HFS) has no validated diagnostic criteria, no confirmatory test and no scoring instrument. Nobody can self-diagnose it, and this article does not enable it.
    • The defining sign is the hard flaccid state itself: a penis that remains firm and semi-rigid at rest, in the absence of sexual desire or arousal. Without that sign, HFS is unlikely to be the correct label regardless of what else is present.
    • Several conditions in the differential do have formal diagnostic criteria. Those conditions should be excluded by a clinician before HFS is considered.
    • Two of them — acute ischemic priapism and penile fracture — are emergencies. Section 6 lists what requires immediate care.
    • The practical output of this article is section 7: a structured history to bring to an appointment.
    Established replicated or guideline-level Hypothesis proposed, partially supported Speculative stated without direct evidence

    The question in the title is usually asked at two in the morning, several weeks after something went wrong, with a browser full of tabs. It deserves a direct answer, and the direct answer is uncomfortable: this article cannot tell you whether you have hard flaccid syndrome, and neither can any other article or forum thread. Not because the information is being withheld. Because the instrument that would settle it has never been built.

    A 2025 systematic review concluded that no objective test assists diagnosis, that the etiology remains unclear, and that diagnosis relies on subjective symptom review together with exclusion of other pathology.4 There is no validated questionnaire for HFS, no consensus definition endorsed by a professional body, and no imaging or laboratory finding that confirms it.4 Any online tool claiming to diagnose HFS is claiming something the field cannot currently do.

    What follows is the next best thing, and it is not a small thing. You can determine whether your presentation contains the defining sign. You can identify features that point somewhere else. You can rule out the emergencies. And you can arrive at an appointment with a history organized well enough that the clinician’s job becomes straightforward. That is the realistic goal, and it is the goal this article is built around.

    1. The defining sign Established

    Everything starts here. Hard flaccid syndrome is named for a sign, and that sign is the anchor of the whole syndrome: the penis remains in a firm, semi-rigid state at rest, in the absence of sexual desire or arousal. Abdessater and colleagues describe it as a constantly semi-rigid penis in the flaccid state, accompanied by penile engorgement at baseline.5 In the largest survey of patients, 90.9% of 143 respondents reported a rigid penis when flaccid.3,4

    This is the discriminating feature. Many conditions cause erectile difficulty, pain, numbness or urinary change. Very few cause a penis to remain hard while entirely unaroused. If that sign is absent from your presentation, the remaining symptoms may still be real and may still need investigation, but the label is probably wrong, and pursuing it may delay the correct diagnosis.

    Several qualifications matter. The state is described as persistent rather than momentary. It is not an erection: rigidity is partial, arousal is absent, and the glans is frequently soft and cold even when the shaft is firm.5 That dissociation between shaft firmness and glans softness is one of the more distinctive observations in the case literature. Symptoms characteristically vary with position, improving when lying down in 73.0% of surveyed patients and worsening on standing in 64.5%.3,5

    Table 1 · The hard flaccid state, and what it is not
    Consistent with the defining signNot the defining sign
    Firmness at rest that persists over hours or days Brief retraction and firming from cold, exercise or anxiety, resolving within minutes
    Present without sexual desire or arousal Partial tumescence occurring in response to arousal
    Partial rigidity, often with retraction or shortening Full rigid erection — if painful and lasting over four hours, this is an emergency12
    Shaft firm while the glans stays soft and cool5 Uniform engorgement including the glans
    Better lying down, worse standing3 No positional variation at all
    No palpable lump in the shaft A palpable plaque with new curvature — assess for Peyronie’s disease11

    This table is a clinical aid, not a validated instrument. No study has measured the sensitivity or specificity of any feature listed here, because no diagnostic standard exists against which to measure them.4 The left column describes what the literature reports; it does not confirm a diagnosis.

    Key points

    • The hard flaccid state — firmness at rest without arousal — is the defining sign and the anchor of the syndrome.
    • It was reported by 90.9% of 143 surveyed patients and is documented across the case literature.
    • A soft, cool glans alongside a firm shaft is characteristic.
    • Without the defining sign, another explanation should be sought first.

    2. How it began Hypothesis

    The onset history carries weight, though less than is often assumed. Reported precipitants cluster around mechanical loading of the erect or semi-erect penis: aggressive masturbation, intense sexual intercourse and penile trauma are the identified causes in the systematic review.4 Penile stretching undertaken for enlargement or elongation appears in the case literature; the patient described by Yazar and colleagues had performed self-directed stretching exercises before presentation.6 All four patients in the original 2020 case series developed symptoms after trauma during intercourse or vigorous masturbation.1

    The proposed mechanism holds that such an injury pathologically activates a pelvic/pudendal-hypogastric reflex, driving sustained sympathetic outflow to cavernosal smooth muscle and holding it contracted.8,9 That model would explain why an injury produces a persistent state rather than a healing wound. It remains a hypothesis; no study has measured penile sympathetic outflow in HFS, and no injury site has been demonstrated by imaging or histology.4

    Here is the qualification that matters for self-assessment. In the survey of 143 patients, 58.0% reported that symptoms began after a specific incident or injury. The remaining 42.0% reported onset with no identifiable trauma.3 A clear inciting event therefore supports the picture, but its absence does not exclude HFS.

    Key points

    • Reported triggers cluster around stretching or mechanical loading of the erect penis.
    • 58.0% of surveyed patients report a specific inciting incident; 42.0% report none.
    • Absence of a remembered injury does not rule out HFS, and its presence does not confirm it.

    3. Does the wider pattern fit? Established

    Beyond the defining sign, HFS presents as a cluster spanning several systems at once. Seven symptoms have published frequencies, drawn from the same survey of 143 self-identified patients.3,4

    Table 2 · Quantified symptom frequencies (n = 143)
    SymptomFrequency
    Change in penis shape or size92.3%
    Rigid penis when flaccid90.9%
    Psychological distress, anxiety or depression89.5%
    Weak or overactive pelvic muscles85.3%
    Penile numbness or sensory loss74.8%
    Erectile difficulty74.1%
    Decreased urinary stream72.7%

    Respondents were self-identified and recruited through social media, which selects for persistent and distressing presentations. These are descriptions of a self-selected population, not prevalence estimates.3

    Two structural cautions apply to using this list. First, no symptom count diagnoses HFS, because no validated criteria exist to be met.4 Counting matches is a natural instinct and it produces a false sense of resolution in both directions. Second, no study has examined whether the number of symptoms correlates with severity or with outcome, so a longer list is not known to mean a worse condition.

    The supporting features documented in the case literature include coldness or numbness with decreased sensitivity, particularly at the glans; a soft, cold glans during erection; reduced frequency of morning and nocturnal erections; penile and perineal pain during urination or ejaculation; and reduced urinary flow.1,5 Emotional distress is near-universal and was one of four dominant themes in a thematic analysis of 12 online forum discussions containing 6,150 comments.2

    Key points

    • HFS presents as a multi-system cluster, not a single symptom.
    • Six of the seven quantified symptoms occur in more than 70% of surveyed patients.
    • No symptom count establishes or excludes the diagnosis.

    4. What has to be excluded first Established

    This is the part of self-assessment that carries real consequence, and it deserves more attention than it usually receives. Several conditions in the differential have formal, published diagnostic criteria — which is precisely what HFS lacks. A diagnosis of HFS made before those conditions are excluded is a diagnosis made in the wrong order.

    Table 3 · Conditions to exclude, and how they are formally defined
    Condition Defining features Criteria or instrument
    Acute ischemic priapism Persistent, typically painful erection unrelated to arousal; a urological emergency AUA/SMSNA guideline12
    Peyronie’s disease Palpable tunical plaque with acquired curvature, indentation or shortening AUA guideline11
    Pudendal neuralgia Pain in pudendal nerve territory, worse sitting, not waking the patient at night, no objective sensory loss on examination, relieved by anesthetic pudendal nerve block Nantes criteria13
    Chronic prostatitis / chronic pelvic pain syndrome Pelvic pain with urinary and quality-of-life impact, scored across three domains NIH-CPSI15
    Erectile dysfunction of vascular origin Graded erectile difficulty; abnormal Doppler parameters IIEF-514 with penile Doppler
    Hard flaccid syndrome Hard flaccid state at rest with supporting symptom cluster None published4

    The final row is the point of the table. Every condition above HFS can be defined against a published standard. HFS cannot, which is why it remains a diagnosis of exclusion.4

    The Nantes criteria for pudendal neuralgia are worth reading closely by anyone whose dominant symptom is pain. They require pain in the anatomical territory of the pudendal nerve, worsened by sitting, not waking the patient at night, without objective sensory loss on clinical examination, and relieved by an anesthetic pudendal nerve block.13 Pudendal neuralgia was also the most frequently reported comorbid condition in the HFS survey, at 16.9%,3 so the two are not mutually exclusive. Both can be present.

    Key points

    • Peyronie’s disease, ischemic priapism, pudendal neuralgia, CPPS and vascular erectile dysfunction all have published diagnostic standards.
    • HFS has none, which is why it is diagnosed only after the others are excluded.
    • Comorbidity is common; excluding a condition as the primary diagnosis does not exclude it as a coexisting one.

    5. Features that argue against HFS

    Some findings should redirect attention rather than be absorbed into an HFS narrative. None of these individually excludes HFS, and several can coexist with it, but each warrants specific assessment in its own right.

    • A palpable lump or plaque in the shaft, with new curvature or indentation. This is the presentation of Peyronie’s disease, a distinct condition with its own guideline-based management.11
    • A full, rigid, sustained erection. The hard flaccid state is partial rigidity without arousal. A sustained full erection is a different phenomenon entirely, and beyond four hours it is an emergency.12
    • Pain that is clearly worse on sitting and relieved on standing. This is the pattern described in the Nantes criteria and points toward pudendal neuralgia; HFS symptoms are more often reported as worse standing and better lying down.3,13
    • Fever, discharge, or pain on passing urine of recent onset. Infection requires assessment and treatment; it is not part of the syndrome.
    • Neurological symptoms in the legs, saddle numbness, or new bladder or bowel incontinence. These require emergency assessment for cauda equina syndrome.
    • Gradual onset over years in an older man with cardiovascular risk factors, without the hard flaccid state. This is a more typical picture for vascular erectile dysfunction, assessed with the IIEF-5 and Doppler studies.14

    Key points

    • A palpable plaque, a sustained full erection, or sitting-dominant pain each point elsewhere.
    • Infective and neurological features are never explained by HFS and need their own assessment.
    • Redirection is not dismissal; some of these conditions coexist with HFS.

    6. Stop reading and seek care Established

    Emergency care, now: a rigid painful erection lasting more than four hours;12 an audible crack or snap with immediate loss of the erection, swelling and bruising; inability to pass urine; new saddle numbness, leg weakness, or loss of bladder or bowel control.

    Same-day or urgent assessment: visible blood in the urine or semen; fever with pelvic or perineal pain; progressive or persistent painful discoloration of the penis; a new painless lump.

    Acute ischemic priapism is the most important of these to recognize, because it is time-critical and because it is the one condition on the list that patients sometimes mistake for a severe hard flaccid state. The two are not similar on examination. Priapism involves a rigid, usually painful erection; the hard flaccid state involves partial firmness without arousal. If there is genuine uncertainty about which is present, that uncertainty is itself a reason to be assessed the same day.12

    7. Who to see, and what to bring

    If you are trying to establish whether you have this, a physician specializing in sexual medicine is the best first choice, because that is where the condition is most likely to have been encountered before. A general urologist is a reasonable alternative and is the usual route in practice, since the conditions that have to be excluded first are urological. Familiarity is not guaranteed even in the subspecialty: at a 2023 sexual medicine session, 64% of 36 attendees had seen a patient with HFS and only 52% regarded it as a legitimate condition.7 Which specialty is right beyond that depends on which symptoms dominate, and the common questions set out that reasoning.

    This is the practical output. A clinician who has not encountered HFS before — which is common — can still assess a well-documented history efficiently. The table below organizes what is worth recording in advance. It is not a diagnostic instrument and produces no score.

    Table 4 · A structured history to prepare
    Domain What to record Why it matters
    Onset Date of first symptom; any specific event immediately preceding it; the interval between event and symptom A mechanical precipitant is reported by 58.0% of patients3
    The defining sign Whether the penis is firm at rest without arousal; how long it persists; whether the glans stays soft This is the anchor of the syndrome5
    Erections Morning and nocturnal erections — present, reduced, absent, and since when; rigidity compared with before Nocturnal erections operate independently of conscious arousal16
    Sensation Location and type of change: numbness, coldness, tingling, unpleasant sensation, loss of erogenous feeling Sensory change affects 74.8% of surveyed patients3
    Pain Site, character, timing; relation to sitting, standing, urination and ejaculation Distinguishes pudendal neuralgia patterns13
    Urinary and bowel Stream strength, hesitancy, urgency, incomplete emptying, constipation Reduced stream affects 72.7%3
    Modifiers What reliably improves and worsens symptoms, including position 73.0% improve lying down; 64.5% worsen standing3
    Prior treatment Everything tried, dose and duration, and the effect of each Avoids repetition of ineffective treatment
    Back and legs Back pain, sciatica, leg symptoms, prior spinal injury Lumbosacral pathology is under investigation as a trigger10
    Mood Anxiety, low mood, sleep, and their timing relative to symptom onset Reported by 89.5%; direction of causation is unresolved3,5

    Recording the timing of mood symptoms relative to physical symptoms is genuinely useful, because whether distress precedes or follows the physical presentation has not been established in any study.5 Your own timeline is real data on a question the literature has not answered.

    One caution about symptom diaries. Recording daily is useful for a few weeks before an appointment. Recording indefinitely, several times a day, tends to become the checking behavior that patients describe as part of the problem rather than part of the assessment. Set an end date when you start.

    Key points

    • Prepare a written history across onset, the defining sign, erections, sensation, pain, urinary and bowel function, modifiers, prior treatment, back symptoms and mood.
    • Timing of mood symptoms relative to physical ones addresses a genuinely open question.
    • Keep symptom recording time-limited.

    8. What the assessment will involve Established

    Reported workups include history and physical examination, baseline hormonal profile, penile color Doppler ultrasound, validated questionnaires including the IIEF-5, IPSS, HADS and NIH-CPSI, and lumbar magnetic resonance imaging where back pain or radicular symptoms are present.4 The questionnaires used are validated for the conditions they were built for — the IIEF-5 for erectile dysfunction,14 the NIH-CPSI for chronic prostatitis15 — not for HFS.

    Expect the results to be normal. Physical examination in HFS is described as unremarkable, with possible firmness at the base of the penis and flaccidity of the glans; imaging, Doppler studies and blood tests including hormonal studies are described as normal.5 Penile color Doppler ultrasound showed normal peak systolic velocity in 98% of reported cases.4 A normal result is not a failed investigation. It excludes structural and vascular disease, which is what it is for.

    One practical matter. The survey figures given in section 7 were drawn from a subspecialty audience, 35 of the 36 attendees being urologists, so they likely overstate familiarity in general practice rather than understate it.7 The implication is that bringing the published literature to an appointment is reasonable, and that being met with unfamiliarity is not evidence your symptoms are imagined. It reflects a field in which the condition first appeared in the literature in 2020.1,17

    Key points

    • Assessment centers on history, examination, hormonal studies, Doppler ultrasound and questionnaires, with lumbar imaging where indicated.
    • Normal results are expected and exclude structural disease.
    • Clinician unfamiliarity is common and is not a judgment on the reality of the symptoms.

    9. Why self-diagnosis fails in both directions

    The risk of concluding too readily that this is HFS is that a condition with specific treatment goes unaddressed. Peyronie’s disease, infection and lumbosacral pathology all have management pathways that a self-applied HFS label delays.11

    The opposite risk is real too. Punjani and Mulhall noted in a 2025 editorial that a PubMed search for the term returned 12 results against roughly 410,000 general web results, that patient-driven communities provide support while also distributing unvetted information, and that true prevalence remains unknown and likely underreported.17 A presentation that does not match a clinician’s expectations is not thereby psychological in origin. The physical findings in HFS are described as unremarkable precisely because the proposed mechanism is functional rather than structural.5,8

    The honest position for a patient reading this is narrower than either extreme. Your presentation may be consistent with what has been described as HFS. Consistency is not confirmation, and confirmation is not currently available to anyone.4

    Key points

    • Premature self-labeling risks missing a treatable condition.
    • Unfamiliarity or normal investigations do not make symptoms psychological.
    • Consistency with published descriptions is the most that self-assessment can establish.

    Important

    This article summarizes published literature and cannot diagnose any condition. It is not medical advice and does not establish a clinician–patient relationship. No validated self-assessment tool for hard flaccid syndrome exists, and nothing here substitutes for examination by a qualified clinician. Seek emergency care for a rigid painful erection lasting more than four hours, inability to pass urine, sudden severe pain with swelling and bruising, or new saddle numbness, leg weakness or loss of bladder or bowel control.
