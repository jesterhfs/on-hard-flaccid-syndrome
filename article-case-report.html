Case report

My own case, recorded in full — onset, every investigation, every treatment attempted, and what each one did or did not do.

11 min read · one case, 44 months

Contents

    This site argues in several places that one properly documented case is worth more to this condition than any volume of forum discussion, and that the community has produced almost none. This is my own — the case of Jesterhfs — set out in full: what happened, what was tested, what was tried, and what each attempt did or did not do.

    It is a single case. It establishes nothing on its own. It is recorded here because an undocumented case establishes less than that, and because several claims made elsewhere on this site — particularly about the failure of sympathetic blockade — trace back to this record and should be checkable against it.

    Before onset

    Attended the University of Michigan, studying philosophy and computer science. Did not graduate, because of this condition. Not in employment, education, or training (NEET) since onset. Never been in a relationship, a virgin, and not seeking a relationship because of this condition. Chronically online for years before onset, and for most of adolescence — the internet was the primary social environment rather than an adjunct to one.

    Medications before onset. Minoxidil and finasteride, both taken for hair loss. Discontinued at onset.

    Medical history before onset. Palmar and plantar hyperhidrosis. Postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS), which resolved after roughly a year. Raynaud’s phenomenon.

    Symptoms

    Nineteen symptoms, present in varying combination since onset.

    • Hard flaccid penis; the flaccid penis remains in a firm, semi-rigid state in the absence of sexual desire or arousal
    • Difficulty achieving or maintaining an erection
    • Vascular changes to the penis (engorged veins, spider veins)
    • Loss of erogenous sensation to the penis, specifically the territory innervated by the dorsal branch of the pudendal nerve
    • Loss of tactile feeling to the penis — temperature, pressure, vibration, texture — in the same territory
    • Penis feels “hollow,” “disconnected,” or unstable, as if it were not part of the body
    • No spontaneous erections
    • No morning erections
    • Hourglassing of the penis in the flaccid and semi-erect states
    • “Long flaccid”: the penis is more extended than it should be, feeling either firm or like a balloon filled with water
    • Tilt of the penis to one side while flaccid
    • Cold feeling in the glans and shaft
    • Discoloration of the skin of the penis
    • Scrotal and testicular retraction
    • Tight erections
    • Pain in and around the penis
    • Dysesthesia — an unpleasant, abnormal sense of touch in or around the penis
    • Paresthesia — tingling or pins and needles in or around the penis
    • Urinary issues: post-void dribbling, dual streams

    Points of note

    Six features of this case seem worth isolating, with the caveat attaching to all of them: this is one patient, and nothing below can be generalized from a single record.

    One. Sympathetic blockade failed at every level attempted. A pudendal block, sympathetic blocks at L2 and L1, and a superior hypogastric plexus block were all performed, and none relieved the hard flaccid state. The superior hypogastric plexus is the anatomical funnel through which the prevailing theory routes the entire proposed mechanism. This case is the primary source for that observation as it is used in the mechanism conjectures and the treatment article, and it should be weighed as what it is: one patient, with no objective measurement taken before or after any block.

    Two. The L2 block did something. It produced transient reduction in scrotal and testicular retraction without touching the hard flaccid state. That dissociation is interesting: it suggests the block worked, and that what it reached was not what maintains the defining sign.

    Three. Alpha blockers produced a slight, real, intolerable response. Terazosin and doxazosin both slightly reduced the hard flaccid state and improved voiding; alfuzosin did nothing. Both were stopped for side effects before any conclusion could be drawn about sustained use. A partial response to α-adrenergic blockade is consistent with adrenergic involvement, and consistent with adrenergic transmission being only part of what holds the tissue contracted.

    Four. Two disorders of autonomic and vascular regulation preceded onset. Raynaud’s phenomenon and POTS were both present before the injury. Whether that is coincidence or predisposition is unknown and cannot be determined from one case. It is recorded because nobody has looked for such a pattern, and it would be worth asking about in a cohort.

    Five. Finasteride exposure preceded onset. Taken for hair loss and stopped before onset. Recorded for completeness. No causal claim is made or implied; it appears here because omitting a pre-onset drug exposure from a case report would be a reporting failure.

    Six. Fifteen months from onset to diagnosis, and eighteen investigations to get there. Almost everything came back unremarkable, which is the expected result in this condition and is itself the finding: structural imaging, urinalysis, blood work and penile Doppler were all normal across four years.
