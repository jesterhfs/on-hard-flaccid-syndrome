Some thoughts

Just some thoughts on the condition and the community.

8 August 2026 · 46 min read · 41 thoughts

I am leaving the community for a while. There are things in my own life that need more attention than this does, and I have run out of ways to pretend otherwise.

It is also a concession. I do not think there is much else I can do about this condition right now, so before I go, I just want to get some thoughts off my chest, for posterity’s sake — about the condition, about the community, about what I think should be done instead, etc.

Some of it is unkind. Most of it applies to me as much as to anyone.

  1. HFS is probably curable, or at least treatable. Nothing we currently know lets us say so definitively, however.
  2. HFS is a condition of the internet age. It first appeared on penis enlargement forums, and it was named on penis enlargement forums. This was not a coincidence. The internet, and the social, psychological, and sexual environment it created, both gave rise to the condition and made it recognizable.
  3. The entire published literature on HFS is about twenty papers. That is a few hours of reading, at most. I’m convinced almost nobody in the community has done it — or has read anything beyond it.
  4. Almost everything known about HFS comes from anonymous strangers posting anecdotes on internet forums. That is the weakest form of evidence there is, and it is essentially our entire foundation.
  5. Doctors cannot help us, so we’re left with each other — the blind leading the blind, except the blind are also clinically retarded.
  6. At any given time, about three to four members of the community are well-read enough to discuss the condition and physiology competently. Hundreds of people, both inside and outside the community, depend on those three to four.
  7. In many cases, this condition cannot be discussed offline. Not with friends, rarely with family, barely with doctors even. So the HFS Reddit and Discord communities are not inputs among many — for most members, they are the only places their beliefs about their own bodies ever get tested. No other health-related community has this much unchecked influence over its members, because no other subject — barring a few exceptions — is this unspeakable everywhere else.
  8. Neither medicine nor this community has ever defined “cured” or “recovered” for HFS. Nobody can be “cured” or “recovered” of a condition that has no definition of “cured” or “recovered.” The condition itself barely has a definition. As the terms are used now, they can mean anything from every symptom disappearing to simply living a normal life again. Two people who claim “I am cured” or “I am recovered” may be claiming completely different things.
  9. Some “cure” posters lie, some exaggerate small or temporary improvements, some never had the condition, and some may have genuinely recovered. Nobody can tell these apart, because the posts are anonymous and unverified. The result is a perpetual state of confusion, misinformation, and psychological harm inflicted upon members of the community.
  10. The community’s default response to an anonymous “cure” claim is belief. That is the wrong default for any claim from any stranger on the internet. For this condition, it is disastrous.
  11. Almost no “cure” claim has ever been substantiated.
  12. In the community’s entire history, one person (btcalvit) got his reported recovery medically documented. One. Judge every other claim against that number.
  13. This community has a serious survivorship bias problem. Attention goes to the few who reportedly “recovered,” never to the many who did the same things and did not. The community’s picture of what “works” is drawn entirely from one side of the record.
  14. A verified recovery would be one of the most valuable things this community could ever produce. Anyone who genuinely cured themselves of a condition this destructive and this obscure would have strong reasons to document it and prove it. Yet the typical “cure” story offers little evidence, answers few questions, and the original poster never returns. Other explanations exist, but without verification, these claims are impossible to tell apart from claims where there was never a “cure” to prove.
  15. The community often assumes that people who stop posting are recovered, rather than considering that they may have stopped out of despair, exhaustion, acceptance, or worse. When someone stops posting, the only thing it proves is that they stopped posting.
  16. A “cure” post can be written to push members toward a procedure, a potentially risky one, that the author will not or cannot try himself. That is guinea-pigging — an attempt to convince some poor bastard to go and find out what happens. I suspect a lot of people have fallen for this without realizing it, and the community has no defense against it, because it believes everything by default.
  17. Anyone selling a cure for a rare, poorly understood condition like HFS — supplements, routines, e-books, coaching, etc. — is knowingly exploiting desperate people. There is no innocent version of this. The correct response is ridicule and ostracism.
  18. Much of the community believes almost anything. Not because they are stupid per se, but because they are desperate, and desperation makes belief easy and desirable, and sometimes even necessary for survival.
  19. This community treats comforting lies as acceptable. That tolerance rests on one assumption: that the people here are too fragile for accurate — or near accurate — information. Nobody would say that sentence out loud, but that is what the behavior implies. The people being protected from the truth are the ones who ultimately pay the price later.
  20. Attention in this community goes to two kinds of posts: doom and “cures.” It follows that the community rewards two kinds of people: black-pilled doomers and hopium addicts.
  21. How confident someone sounds matters more here than whether they are right. So the community naturally elevates its most confident members, and confidence and accuracy are not the same trait. Often they are opposites: the people who understand least have the least reason to doubt themselves.
  22. Most members do not understand the topics they discuss. They discuss them daily anyway, at length, with confidence.
  23. The standard way of learning about HFS here is to ask an influential member, “Is this true?” They provide an answer, and that answer is then repeated ad nauseam until it becomes accepted as fact.
  24. A claim repeated often enough becomes community knowledge. At least half of what the community “knows” about HFS is simply what it has heard the most.
  25. Most members want answers handed to them, so all the actual work falls on only a handful of people.
  26. Almost no doctors take this condition seriously or intentionally study it. The community’s response to the few who do is to attack or discredit them.
  27. The community demands from doctors a seriousness it does not practice itself.
  28. Pelvic floor physical therapy has little to no logical basis as a treatment for HFS; more specifically, the hard flaccid state. It is what doctors prescribe when they do not know what else to do. Years of promoting it have cost sufferers time and money and have taught everyone — patients, doctors, and anyone who searches the condition — that HFS is something that it is not. That mistaken picture is now one of the biggest obstacles to seeing what it actually is.
  29. Nobody fails at treating this condition, because nothing has been shown to work beyond a reasonable doubt. When a treatment fails, the fault is the treatment’s, not the patient’s. “Try harder” assumes something works if tried hard enough. Nothing has earned that assumption, and defending it anyway is dogma — a belief maintained against the evidence because the community needs it to be true.
  30. Some members most certainly lie about how they got the condition, because the truth embarrasses or scares them. You can usually spot it: the more elaborate or convoluted the onset story, or the more nonsensical the attributed cause, the more likely the real cause is the obvious one. Individually, these lies are understandable. Together, they create the impression that HFS can be caused by virtually anything under the sun.
  31. Every member seems to have his own theory about HFS, and with it, his own red herring to chase.
  32. Enormous effort goes into alternative explanations for the condition, not because the evidence points to them per se, but because they are easier to live with than the likely truth. Whole theories exist in this community for essentially one purpose: to avoid saying “I injured my penis” or “I injured a nerve in my penis” or “I have a rare, poorly understood penis condition.”
  33. Denial is one of the community’s defining traits. It often shapes what we believe, what we investigate, what we tell ourselves and each other, and what we tell our doctors.
  34. Community discussion produces next to nothing, because most of it is either complaining or theorizing about things nobody understands. None of it is helpful, substantive, or productive.
  35. The community has never decided what it is. In my opinion, it is a waiting room, or a kind of purgatory.
  36. The community is apathetic, and the apathy feeds itself.
  37. Nothing ever happens here because nothing is aimed at anything. There is no direction, no shared goal or purpose. It is every man for himself. This community is the only group on earth with a real reason to fix any of this. The only open question is whether it is capable. Every day nothing happens is a resounding vote for no.
  38. Truth is negotiable here. That is the root of nearly everything on this list.
  39. From the beginning, the community took on the wrong job. Not that it had much choice, though — nobody else took any job at all. It tried to solve the condition, which it cannot do, and neglected spreading awareness, which it can. Patients cannot do the science — a decade-plus of trying has proven that, and believing otherwise at this point is delusion. What we can do is make the condition known. Nothing improves until more people know it exists and more researchers take an interest. None of this is disputable.
  40. Some ideas, proposals, suggestions:
    1. Snap back to reality.
    2. Always adhere to the truth, or whatever is closest to it. Deferring to expert consensus should be the default in any health-related community, and ours should be no exception.
    3. Abandon “every man for himself.”
    4. Abandon the idea that our small, insular patient community can cure a rare, poorly understood medical condition on our own. Our strength is not in replacing doctors and researchers, but in making their work possible.
    5. Create a community data set. Create a repository for case reports.
    6. Record treatment failures, not just successes. This kills the survivorship bias problem.
    7. Vet “cured” and “recovered” posts. Label every claim by its evidence.
    8. Encourage anyone claiming to be cured or recovered to work with a doctor or researcher and get their case documented in the medical literature. It has been done once — if btcalvit could do it, others can too.
    9. Identify the most competent members of the community and put them to work somehow.
    10. Do not underestimate the power of a couple dozen highly organized, highly motivated, highly committed people.
    11. Instead of a foundation to fund research — proposed before, and beyond our means — create a fund for the tools that spread awareness.
    12. Register the condition with the rare-disease infrastructure. Databases like Orphanet, GARD, and NORD exist to catalog conditions exactly like this one — haven’t looked into it too much, but worth investigating properly.
    13. Send competant community representatives to urology and sexual medicine conferences, if it proves possible.
    14. Talk about the condition outside the community. Join other servers and other Reddit communities, write a Substack article, make a YouTube video, anything. Every mention outside our own walls reaches people our servers and subreddits never will.
    15. Latch the condition to issues already in the zeitgeist: the male loneliness epidemic, looksmaxxing, porn addiction and gooning, etc.
    16. To outsiders, present the condition as more widespread than our numbers on Reddit and Discord suggest.
    17. The condition is funny. Not for us, obviously. But it is funny. I’ve joked before that it’s the kind of thing you’d scare your friends with at a high school lunch table. Do not underestimate the power of humor to spread awareness.
    18. You cannot have progress unless it’s pointed in the right direction. A man digging with total dedication in the wrong spot doesn’t end up with treasure. He ends up with a very deep hole.
    19. Do not take anyone’s word by default. Use your head — and when a claim actually matters, check it.
    20. Beware of anonymous troll demons, Machiavellians, sadistic psychopaths.
    21. Do not let anyone pressure you into a risky treatment, procedure, purchase, etc. The decision is yours, and so are the consequences.
    22. Assume everyone in the community has little understanding of what they’re talking about. That should help put things into perspective.
  41. Some other things:
    1. On the “HF Unraveled” e-book: There isn’t much there. It’s basically just a long, poorly written HFS forum post. Granted, it was written in 2013, when nothing was really known about the condition — but the author writes as if plenty was, declaring with total confidence that this is a simple muscle problem, or “a cramp,” and that it’s basically nothing to worry about. In one breath he admits the postural correlations he lists might mean nothing; in the next he asserts that every man with prolonged HFS suffers from anxiety, and that men who fail to grasp the “mind-body connection” are “never able to heal themselves,” as if HFS isn’t a functional problem. It’s gobbledegook, frankly — at best guesswork by someone who thinks he knows things and clearly doesn’t. The copy I have has a buyer’s name stamped on it, so he was apparently selling this to desperate men with HFS. Nice. Awesome. What’s remarkable though is how much of it is still with us all these years later. Spend a day in the community, on Reddit and on Discord, and you’ll hear the same claims this book was making thirteen years ago, repeated almost word for word: “it’s a muscle problem,” “it’s tension,” “work on your mind-body connection,” “relax and you’ll heal,” etc. It’s all so tiresome.
    2. On the Hard Flaccid System Reset guide: AI slop, gobbledegook, nothing of substance. Quite funny that a rinky-dink website advertising “no paywall, no ads, no upsell” earns Amazon commission on the castor oil, sesame oil, gua sha tools and Epsom salt it tells you to buy. Draw your own conclusions from that. To his credit, whoever he is, he never tells anyone to avoid a doctor. I’ve never understood why people make “guides” like this. What are you actually trying to accomplish? You offer no proof that you’ve recovered yourself, or that you understand the condition at all. So what’s the point, other than leading desperate people on? Or in this case, potentially making a bit of money off of them?
    3. On Literature Greedy: In my opinion, he is the most credible supposed HFS recovery to come out of the community in recent memory, but only on one narrow point: the mechanism is at least plausible, all things considered. If something like central sensitization contributes to HFS, then NMDA antagonists — ketamine, or in his case its analogues — are a logical thing to try; I’ve covered this elsewhere. But these drugs also produce dissociative effects, which means they can change how a symptom is perceived without necessarily changing the symptom. Whether he genuinely experienced resolution of the hard flaccid state and its associated symptoms is therefore unknowable. Nor did he ever substantiate the claim beyond a few brief posts on Discord, and then he left abruptly, as they all do. I remain tentative about whether to believe him. At one point he offered to send me some of the ketamine analogue that supposedly gave him the most relief, free of charge. I don’t let strangers on the internet send me things, let alone drugs, so I politely declined. From my conversations with him, he seemed to be in intermittent contact with Goldstein and Komisaruk. If I remember correctly, he also told me he would let Goldstein know about his dissociative experimentation, though whether he actually did is anyone’s guess. During a phone call with Goldstein about sympathetic nerve blocks, he corroborated that an individual had come to him reporting success with a block at L2. And to Greedy’s credit, he did send me snip-its of his emails with Komisaruk. In any case, I would have hoped he was being honest with me, but I cannot be sure; I cannot be sure of anyone in this community being honest, least of all when they claim to have recovered from a condition like this, whatever that even means. Anyway, all around a very smart guy — clearly competent and well-read. If only more people like him had HFS and contributed to the community. I hope he’s doing well.
    4. On Btcalvit: From the looks of it, he was a teenager when he did most of his posting, which I try to keep in mind when I think about the issues I’ve had with him and his ideas. The community is largely moving on from many of those ideas, and I’m glad for that. But he did, on his own initiative, get his case documented in the medical literature, which may be the most respectable thing anyone in this community has ever done. I hope he’s doing well.
    5. On UnbrokenPelvis: I’ve had my issues with him in the past, but he’s a good man, and I’ve never doubted that. He’s trying to help a community of desperate young men, which is more than most would do. He does it for free too. That said, he just doesn’t know much about the condition, is all — no one really does, so I can’t hold it against him. I hope he’s doing well.
    6. On Seer: I don’t want to be harsh, so I’ll leave it at this. Hard as it may be to believe, I spent nearly three years of my life abstaining from masturbation, touching myself only to use the bathroom, to wash my balls (wash your penis too, bucko), or through my pants to adjust. I was scared of masturbation, scared to death of it. I didn’t want to risk making my condition any worse than it already was. And what do I have to show for it? Nothing. I am still here, and my flaccid penis is still as hard and as numb and as painful as ever. It took a doctor at a psychiatric hospital, and my father, to convince me to start doing it again. Half a dozen men in my family have had prostate cancer, so I have to just grin and bear it at least once a week, close my eyes, grit my teeth. It should be an enjoyable experience, right? It is not.
    7. On urologists, broadly: For HFS, barring sexual medicine specialists, they are a complete waste of time and provide no help whatsoever. Do not even bother with these people.
    8. On pelvic floor physical therapy and its practitioners: Most of the community already knows how I feel about pelvic floor physical therapy and the specialists who practice it. But for all my frustration with them, I don’t blame them for the widespread impression that HFS is just another pelvic floor condition. Some of that blame belongs to apathetic urologists who, not knowing what else to do, refer HFS patients to PFPT. Most of it comes down to the condition itself: it’s obscure, it’s new, and it’s poorly understood. A vacuum like that naturally gets filled by whatever happens to be available. What does bother me is how many pelvic floor physical therapists have responded to HFS patients showing up at their clinics. They put the condition on their websites — some even make YouTube videos about it — and present themselves as knowing what it is and how to treat it. Some of this, I suspect, comes down to money — HFS patients are new customers, and advertising a treatment for it on the internet brings them in. Those pages get indexed by search engines, and suddenly PFPT looks like “the” treatment for HFS — not because it works, but because it’s what comes up when you search. I lived this. After five or six months of PFPT that wasn’t working, my parents kept telling me to stick with it. Everything they found online pointed to PFPT, so they assumed it was the path to recovery. And they weren’t being unreasonable — they were just reading what the internet told them. But there’s a limit to what a person can endure. For me, doing PFPT for HFS was like Sisyphus rolling his boulder up the hill: session after session, month after month, and the boulder always ends up back at the bottom, if it even moved to begin with. Humans aren’t built to keep at something for months on end without seeing any results from it. And it goes from exhausting to torturous when the thing you’re being told to persist with has little to no basis as a treatment for the condition you suffer from in the first place. At least Sisyphus knew his task was pointless. Patients are told theirs isn’t — that the results are coming, they’re always coming, that they just need to keep pushing. And under normal circumstances, it doesn’t take half a year to see at least some improvement from PFPT. When it’s treating what it’s actually meant to treat — a genuine muscular problem — patients typically notice progress within weeks, a couple of months at most. Clearly, HFS is the exception, and the reason is simple: it isn’t ultimately a muscle or structural problem; I’ve written about this at length elsewhere on the site. You can’t stretch and strengthen your way out of a condition that doesn’t live in the muscles. After six months of nothing, any reasonable person would conclude that the therapy was never aimed at the real problem. And that wore me down, mentally and physically. My first suicide attempt came after those months of PFPT and the despair they left me with. The internet — including the patient community — had given me the impression that this condition could be cured or resolved if I just did the therapy. So when it didn’t work, it felt like proof that nothing would. I’m not saying this for sympathy. I’m saying it because people need to understand that this isn’t an abstract debate about treatment protocols. The cost of pointing desperate patients toward something with no real evidence behind it is measured in more than wasted months. Then there’s what we do to each other. When someone says PFPT — or any other baseless proposed treatment, for that matter — isn’t working for them, the response is too often “try harder” — or the quiet assumption that they must not be doing it right, not doing it enough, not committed enough to their own recovery, etc. I heard it myself. People told me things like “you’re lazy,” or “you’re just making excuses,” or “you can be cured — just keep doing it, it takes a long time.” I understand where it comes from. When a treatment is the only hope people have, questioning it feels dangerous, so it’s easier to question the patient instead. If the treatment is beyond doubt, then every failure has to be the patient’s fault. That’s not “support.” That’s dogma — and it lands on people at their absolute lowest, telling them that their continued suffering is their own doing. To be clear: PFPT is safe, and if you want to try it, go for it — it’s at least worth trying. It may genuinely help with some of the other symptoms of HFS. But it clearly does not help the main symptom, and that’s the distinction that matters. Pelvic floor physical therapists need to stop lying to their patients about what their treatment can and can’t do, and they need to stop presenting themselves as experts on a condition that no one yet understands. Be honest about its limits. Offer it for what it actually addresses, not for what desperate people are hoping it will fix. To end, what I want is simple: treatment for HFS that verifiably works. Not “treatment” that fills a gap, not “treatment” that exists only because nothing else does — treatment that works. I want that for myself, and I want it for every single person in this community, and for everyone who comes after us. We deserve it.
    9. On Goldstein: “The greatest of all time,” as they say in the sexual medicine community. I’ve never understood the hate he sometimes gets — beyond his appointments being expensive, which I’ve written about elsewhere on the site. This man is in his mid-seventies. He has a long and successful career behind him, and he could easily retire a happy man. Instead he chooses to keep working, and to help people suffering from conditions like ours. You can agree or disagree with his theories — that’s fine — but he’s a great man, and we should be thankful for the work he and his team have done for us.
    10. On the moderators of r/hardflaccidresearch: They’re doing a good job, and they should keep it up. I have only one other request — adhere to the truth, or to whatever is closest to it. Because the subreddit is the only real source of information about HFS on the internet, they have a responsibility to let patients know what there is to know about this condition, and as a health-related community, it’s their job to defer to expert consensus, even if that consensus is slim, not to the consensus of anonymous retards on the internet. Regarding “cured” or “recovered” posts, I hope they develop a plan to handle those as well.
    11. On proof: I’ve built a reputation — and taken plenty of fire — for asking people who post “cured” or “recovered” stories on Reddit or Discord to provide some degree of proof. Usually that meant asking for picture or video evidence, or asking them to contact someone actually researching the condition, like Goldstein, so their case could be verified and documented in the medical literature. This is a rare, poorly understood condition, after all. You’d think curing yourself would be a very big deal — the kind of thing that deserves proper medical scrutiny. That’s what would happen with any other rare condition. So what makes ours any different? I do this because I’ve long suspected that many of these posts are fabrications, or at best reports of minor perceived improvements made on a high of hope or something. Anyone can make an account on an internet forum and post whatever they want. There is nothing stopping them. After arguing with someone in a Reddit thread about this, he sent me a DM on Discord ridiculing me for merely suggesting that someone claiming a “cure” should prove it. I explained my reasoning. And he wasn’t letting up. So I decided to demonstrate it instead. I went to Reddit, made an alt account, and wrote a completely fake story about curing myself. A family member of mine was training for a marathon around that time — or perhaps they had just completed one, I cannot remember — so I claimed I’d cured myself through running, and I deliberately chose an absurd regimen: ten miles a day. I posted it to r/hardflaccidresearch. The post is still up (if the mods delete it, which they should, you can see it here). The responses started coming in. I replied to a few comments with vague non-answers, just like these posts always seem to do. I then shared the thread with the individual on Discord, then DMed his Reddit account to prove the poster was me. He threatened to report the post to the moderators. And I told him to go ahead — just to wait a while first, so I could make my point. Then I waited a while — and before the harder questions could start coming, I deleted the account. That, too, is a pattern many of these cured posts tend to follow; It’s worth noticing how often these posts come from brand-new accounts as well, quite incriminating if you ask me. I sent him screenshots of the comments: supportive and encouraging, with almost no one questioning whether any of it was true despite the absurdity of the post. To his credit, one person called out the bullshit from the start — but it made no difference to how the post was received. It drew 88 comments and 55 upvotes. My point to the individual on Discord was simple: look how easy this is. Look how easy it is to make an account and lead desperate people on. There are no guardrails, and the default is to believe the poster unconditionally. He conceded, and didn’t really know what to say after that. You can’t tell me this doesn’t happen. People here are desperate and hopeless, and that’s exactly the audience a fake post like this works on. Someone might write one to give people the hope they’re starving for. Someone might write one to push a theory, a treatment, a procedure, to sell something, or simply out of sadism. The motive doesn’t matter; the vulnerability is the same. So something needs to change. Be it pictures, video, a commitment to see someone who can document the case, anything. There has to be some way of ensuring these posts aren’t just fabrications. Curing HFS should be a far bigger deal than the community treats it as. Right now there is no seriousness, no rigor, no scrutiny at all for posts like these. That needs to change too.
    12. On predisposition: If there are preconditions to developing HFS, in most cases I think they’re psychological — not in the sense that the condition itself is psychological, but in the sense of what would lead someone to injure themselves this way in the first place. Usually it comes down to one of two things: body image issues, or an addiction to porn that produces harmful masturbation habits. I’ve also noticed something conspicuous about who ends up here. A lot of us seem to be chronically online, and a specific type of chronically online — users of imageboards, forums or online communities built around bodybuilding, looksmaxxing, inceldom, etc. Someone should look into this properly. Knowing what kind of person tends to develop HFS could tell us something more about the condition itself, more than what is already obvious at least.
    13. On activism: The best targets are young, ambitious medical students, doctors, and researchers who want to build a career on an unsolved condition, people with something to gain, people who want to LARP as House, M.D. Targets should not be limited to medicine, however. Publications and influencers that cover sex, men’s health, and internet culture would work too — a magazine like GQ, for example, or a YouTuber like MoistCr1TiKaL, who has spoken publicly about a penis injury of his own. One article or one video from any of them would put HFS in front of more people than this community has reached in its entire existence. The story is compelling enough to be worth telling: a condition born on the internet, involving the penis, unrecognized by medicine, predominantly affecting young, atomized men. Regardless, HFS must leave our corner of the internet. And getting eyes on us is possible. My brief attempt with meme pages on X in 2024 proved it can be done — it resulted in the most visited day our wiki article has ever had. We just have to be smart and creative about it, and commit to a plan — or plans — as a group.
    14. On the penis enlargement community: This might sound cynical, but the penis enlargement community is unironically one of our greatest allies in this. Their growth and ours are tied together: The more men deliberately do this to themselves, the more men end up with HFS, and the more men who end up with HFS, the more attention the condition eventually gets. I think this is largely why HFS first appeared in the medical literature around 2019 and 2020 — There were simply more people on the internet in the 2010s than in the 2000s. That meant more men exposed to the conditions and practices that cause this, and enough of them afterward to form a community at all. Put frankly, our recognition depends on a steady supply of new HFS patients. Looksmaxxing going mainstream is only adding to that supply. There is a second argument, though: these communities should not exist, or at the very least should be kept off major platforms like Reddit and YouTube, where impressionable young men find them, so it’s a moral duty to push for their removal. To be honest, why this content escapes classification as self-harm is beyond me. I remember doomscrolling through the penile injury subreddits. Most of the men posting there had injured themselves doing penis enlargement. Strange how those subreddits aren’t around anymore. Wonder why. In any case, framing it — penis enlargement — as self-harm could help us spread awareness. The problem is scale, though. Declaring war on a community substantially larger than ours would require influential people on our side. We could recruit journalists, influencers. I’ve brought up MoistCr1TiKaL before, as an example — he injured himself trying to enlarge his penis, and he made a video warning people against it. Getting someone like him on board is probably the only way we could make a real difference. He has the audience, he has firsthand experience with this exact subject, and he reached his conclusions on his own. All we would be asking is for him to say again, louder, what he has already said once. So the question is simple. Do we want more people getting HFS, or more people knowing what HFS is? Doing nothing gets us the first. Only doing something gets us the second. Which of the two serves us better is up for debate.
    15. On the NoFap community: HFS could be further introduced to them as a consequence of aggressive masturbation, much the same way “death grip” already is.
    16. On memetics: There’s real power in memes as a vehicle for awareness, and it’s worth taking seriously rather than dismissing as unserious. Ideas don’t spread online because they’re important; they spread because they’re funny, shocking, strange enough that people pass them along without being asked, etc. Awareness campaigns fail all the time because they lead with sincerity, and sincerity doesn’t travel. Jokes travel, though. Humor travels. And this is where HFS holds a card that almost no other rare condition does: it involves the penis. How many dick jokes do you know? How many have you heard? People cannot resist talking or thinking about the penis. That’s just a fact of life. My meme experiment on X already showed this on a small scale. No other patient community gets handed a subject this inherently attention-grabbing. And I think it would be a waste not to use it.
    17. On artifical intelligence: If humans never make substantial progress on HFS, artificial intelligence eventually will. Progress on a condition like this has always required years of study and teams of doctors and researchers who care enough to take it on. AI removes that requirement. It doesn’t need to be persuaded that we matter, doesn’t need funding proportional to our numbers, doesn’t need a career incentive to look at our problem, etc. A sufficiently capable system, in time, could generate and test hypotheses about HFS as readily as it could for any disease with a foundation behind it. I’m not saying this arrives next year, in five years, or in ten. But it will arrive. Make sure you’re alive for at least the next decade or so. It will be difficult for many of you, given everything, but the promise of artificial intelligence is too great to walk away from now. It is my biggest source of optimism and the strongest reason I have to keep going.

To close: I’ve mostly given up on this community, and I’ve stopped expecting this condition to be figured out any time soon — least of all by a handful of anonymous retards on the internet. This site is unironically my ticket out of this community for the foreseeable future.

That said, I’ll be back if and when there’s an update on my condition or my situation. I am not suicidal. If you do not hear from me for a long time, assume that I am still alive and that I still have HFS.

I wish everyone luck.
